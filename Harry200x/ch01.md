# Chapter One

*31 October 1981 to February 1982*

Harry was in his cot when the front door broke.

He knew the sound of that door. It opened and shut a dozen times a day and the sounds it made belonged to the house, like the kettle and the cat. This sound had a crack in it, and his mother stopped singing in the middle of a word. She had been sitting on the end of the cot with his toy broomstick across her knees, and she stood up so quickly that the broomstick fell on the floor.

His father shouted downstairs. His mother went out of the room and came back and lifted him out of the cot and then put him into it again, which was wrong, because being lifted meant he was going somewhere. She dragged the chest of drawers across the carpet until it stood against the door. Downstairs there was a flash of green that lit the landing and came in under the door, and after it his father was not shouting any more.

His mother had both hands on the rail of the cot. She was saying his name over and over with other things in between, and he reached up to be held because she was crying, and she did not pick him up. Then the door came open in spite of the drawers and a man stood in it.

Harry had never seen the man. He was tall, with a pale flat face, and his mother put herself between him and the cot with her arms out and begged. Harry knew begging. It was how he asked for things he wanted very badly. The man said one short thing. The green light came again, close, and his mother went down onto the floor and lay there.

He waited for her to get up. The man came to the cot and looked down, and Harry looked back, because the man's eyes were red and he had not known that eyes could be. The man said something with a laugh in it and pointed a stick at him, and the green light came out of the end of the stick straight at his face.

There was pain in his forehead, which he had a word for, or the beginning of one. Then the room came apart. The ceiling went up and the wall by the window went out, and the man made a sound that was not a word and went too, in a way Harry could not follow, and everything slowed down.

He did not understand it as slowing down. He was fifteen months old, and it was a long time before he had any way to put what had happened to him into words, even inside his own head. What he knew was that the dust from the broken ceiling was still coming down, and that it went on coming down for a very long time, every grey piece of it hanging in the air and turning over as it fell, so that he could have counted them if he had known how to count. A piece of plaster the size of his hand went past the cot and he watched it the whole way to the floor. When he began to cry, the cry came out of him long and low, like something being drawn through a pipe, and he stopped, because that frightened him more than the man had.

Stars were out above him where the roof had been. They did nothing. His mother did nothing. He waited for her, and the waiting was the longest thing that had ever happened to him, longer than a night, longer than being left in the pram outside the shop while she went in. The cold came into the room slowly. He went on waiting.

A long while after that a very big man came up through the broken door with his hands over his face and knelt by Harry's mother and made the long low sound Harry had made, and went on making it. Then he lifted Harry out of the cot, very carefully for the size of him, and his beard was wet. Another man came, with black hair all over the place and a white, stretched face that Harry would keep for the rest of his life exactly as it was then, and that man took him out of the big man's arms and held him against his chest hard enough that Harry could feel his heart going. It went slowly. Everything did. Then the black-haired man gave him back and said a great deal, very fast for him and very slow for Harry, and the two of them went out into the dark and the big man climbed onto something that roared.

They went up. Harry had never been in the air, and nothing surprised him any more that night. The wind should have been fast. Instead it pressed on his face and stayed there, steady as a hand, and the stars slid overhead so slowly that he could follow one from one side of the sky to the other if he chose to. He followed one. It was something to do. The engine made a sound so long and deep that he could feel each beat of it come up through the big man's chest. He thought the night would never end, and for a very long time it did not.

When it did there was a street of houses all alike, and an old man with a long silver beard and small glasses, and a woman standing by him on the pavement, talking.

The woman and the old man talked for a long while, and then the old man came and looked at Harry in the big man's arms. His eyes were blue and he looked for a long time, and Harry looked back and did not cry. The old man touched his forehead with a stick, which made Harry flinch, but nothing green came out of it, and there was no pain. Harry was wrapped tighter in his blanket and put down on a step by a door with a number on it, and the big man blew his nose like a horn and climbed back on the roaring thing and was gone.

Then the woman became a cat, and he saw it happen. She had taken a handkerchief out and put it away again, and then she was shorter, and her cloak was lying closer to her and turning into fur in patches, and her face came forward and her ears went up the sides of her head and became a cat's ears, and the whole business took, by his reckoning, about as long as a bath. The cat went round the corner of the house without looking back. The old man stood over Harry a moment more. He lifted something small and silver in a hand with a great many rings on it, and all the lights in the street came on at once, and the old man's stick touched Harry's forehead again, and that was the last thing he saw.

---

He woke to screaming.

That is, he woke to a sound that began low and rose and went on rising for what felt like an hour, and the face above him was a woman's with its mouth open, and she was leaning down to him so slowly that he had time to look at all of her before she arrived: the pale eyes, the long neck, the hair in rollers, the envelope on the step beside him that her hand was already reaching for. It was his aunt. He did not know that yet. He knew that she looked at him the way his mother had looked at the man in the doorway, and then she read the letter, standing in her dressing-gown on the step beside the milk bottles, and her face did several things one after another while he watched. Then she picked him up with the letter, looked up the street and down it, and took him in.

---

The house was number four, Privet Drive. He learned that later, from the postman, who read the envelopes to himself at the gate as he sorted them, and from the envelopes, which he could read before he could read anything else because he saw so many of them.

In the first weeks he could not read anything. He could not do anything. At night he was put in a cot in a room full of boxes and broken toys, and in the day he was put in a playpen in the corner of the front room, and twice a day he was fed and changed by a woman who did both fast and said very little while she did them, and the rest of the time he lay or sat and looked at whatever was in front of him and found that he could look at it for a very long time.

The ceiling of the room with the boxes had a crack in it shaped like a river. The light from the window moved across the crack through the morning, and if he watched the edge of the light he could see it move. It moved at the pace of the shadow on a sundial, which was fast enough for him.

Everything was like that. He came to it slowly, the way a person comes to know that they have gone deaf, by what was missing. Sounds had stretched. A door shutting downstairs was not a bang but a long low shove of noise that took as long to arrive and pass as a wave does on a beach. When his aunt spoke, each word reached him as a shape that lasted and changed as it went, and he had to hold the whole of it until it ended before he could tell what it had been, and by then the next one had begun. It was like being spoken to very slowly by someone a long way off. After a while it was also easy. He had a great deal of time to practise, and nothing else to practise on.

Movement had stretched too. His aunt crossed the room to him at the speed of someone wading. The other baby, Dudley, who was his own size and a good deal heavier, threw things, and Harry could watch a thing leave Dudley's hand and turn in the air and know well before it landed where it would land. He could do nothing about it. His own arm, when he told it to move, moved at the same pace as everything else. He got no further than that for a long while. It was simply how things were: the room was slow, and his arm was part of the room.

He had no words for any of this, or for much else. He had Mummy and Daddy and cat and no and up, and none of them was any use to him now except no, which his aunt said to him a great deal, and up, which did not work on her at all. More came, because there was nothing in the room but talk, and nothing for him to do but hold it.

---

He loved the television for how it moved. Up close, the picture was not a picture but a bright line that ran across the glass from top to bottom, over and over, leaving a trail of light behind it that faded as it went, and the pictures were made of hundreds of these lines laid down one after another. He watched the line go down the screen for a whole morning once, and when his aunt switched the set off the picture shrank to a dot in the middle of the glass that took about as long to go out as it took her to cross the room.

The sound was the useful part. People on the television talked for hours each day, and they talked about the same things in the same words, again and again, and nobody told them to be quiet. He learned the shape of the word today before he knew what it meant, because a man said it each evening at the same hour with the same picture behind him. He learned news. He learned weather from the man who stood in front of the map, and rain from the same man, and rain was the first word he was sure of, because the man said it and then it rained on the window, and his aunt said it when she brought the washing in, and once she stood at the window with a cup of tea and said it to nobody.

His aunt talked to Dudley. She talked to him all day in a voice she used for nothing else, and most of what she said was his name and the names of things. Juice. Shoes. Teddy. Dinner. She held things up and named them and Dudley grabbed them, and Harry, in his pen in the corner, took the names and kept them. She did not hold things up for Harry. She said no to him, and quiet, and now and then the boy to her husband, who did not look at him. But she read to Dudley. She bought him books with pictures in them and sat him on her knee on the settee with the book open in front of them both, facing Harry's corner as it happened, and read.

The books were small, with a ladybird on the back. The words in them were very large and there were very few of them, and they came round again and again. Here is Peter. Here is Jane. Peter is here, and Jane is here. His aunt read each page slowly for Dudley with her finger under the words, and Dudley hit the page, and she turned over, and Harry, who could see the shapes on the page well enough from where he sat and had all the time there was to look at them, began to match the shapes to the sounds.

It took him the winter. The matching was easy. The books came out only when his aunt felt like it, perhaps twice in a week, and he had to wait for them. In between he lay in the pen and went over what he had. He found that he could call up the page as he had seen it and look at it again, the shapes and the finger and the sound together, and look for as long as he liked, and that nothing he had seen went away. By February he had Peter and Jane and here and is and the and dog and ball, and a dozen more, and the word that came up in white letters at the bottom of the screen each evening under the newsreader's face, which was his name.

In February the telephone gave him two more words, said together.

His aunt used the telephone in the hall. It was a long way from his pen, and her voice on it was lower than the voice she used on Dudley and higher than the one she used on her husband, and she talked for a long time to someone called Marge. He could not hear Marge. He could hear his aunt, and when her voice went lower still he listened harder, as he did whenever anything changed.

"No, the other one," she said. "Her boy." Then a long time of Marge. "Car crash. Yes. Well, what could we do, Marge? There was nobody else." A pause. "No. No, I don't think so. He's very quiet. He hardly cries at all." Something about Dudley, and her voice went up again, and the useful part of the conversation was over.

Boy was him. He had that from his uncle, who said the boy to his aunt with his eyes on the pen. Crash he had from the television, where it went with cars folded up on motorways and the particular voice. The two had been said together, in the low voice, with her in front of them, and he kept them together the way he kept rain and the window, without yet knowing what the pairing was for.

---

The bottle happened on a grey afternoon in February.

Dudley had had his and was asleep on the settee with his mouth open. Harry's was on the sideboard, where his aunt had put it down when the telephone rang, and the telephone had turned out to be Marge, and his aunt had taken it upstairs with her on its long cord and shut the bedroom door. Harry was hungry. He had been hungry for some time, and he had done the things he knew how to do about it, which were to make a noise and to hold out his arms, and the first had brought his aunt's head round the door and taken it away again, and the second had done nothing at all.

He looked at the bottle. There was nothing else in the room that he had not looked at already, and the bottle was the thing he wanted, and he looked at it with all the attention he had, which was a great deal. He knew exactly where it was. He knew exactly where he wanted it to be, which was in his hands, and he could see, as clearly as he could see the bottle itself, the way it would have to come: off the sideboard, across the room, over the bar of the pen, down.

It came. It lifted off the sideboard and crossed the room at a walking pace, which to Harry was no pace at all, so that he had a long time to watch it come and to be astonished. It did not wobble. It came the way he had seen it coming, over the bar of the pen and down into his hands, and he got the teat into his mouth and drank, because he was hungry.

Someone noticed. His aunt had come down the stairs with the telephone in her hand to put it back on its table in the hall, and from the hall you could see through the open door of the front room to the pen, and she had seen the bottle in the air with nothing holding it. He knew that from the sound she made. It was the sound from the doorstep, the one that began low and rose, only shorter, because she stopped it with her hand. She came across the room at her wading pace and took the bottle out of his hands and out of his mouth, and stood looking at him with it dripping onto the carpet, and did not give it back.

He did not get it back until evening, and then only when his uncle had come home and the two of them had stood in the kitchen with the door shut for a long time, talking in the low voices.

He heard every word. The kitchen door was thin, and the voices came through it to him in the pen as long slow shapes that he had become very good at holding. He heard his uncle say that he had known it, that he had said so from the first, and his aunt say that of course he had, what had Vernon expected, with her for a mother. He heard his uncle say one of them, with a space round the words. He heard the word freak, which he did not know, and the word normal, which he had from the television.

"We'll have none of it," his uncle said. "None. Not in my house."

"That man said we had to keep him."

"I don't care what that man said."

"He said if we didn't, Vernon. He said what would happen if we didn't. You read it."

A silence, which to Harry was a long one.

"Fine," his uncle said. "Fine. But I won't have it. The first sign of any more of it and he goes in the cupboard, and he can stay there, and you can write and tell that man so, if you know where to write."

They came out after that, and his aunt gave him the bottle without a word, and he drank it cold.

What he had by the time he had finished it was a pairing, like rain and the window. The bottle in the air went with the sound his aunt had made, and the sound went with the low voices in the kitchen, and in the low voices had been her, and that man, and a word he had not heard before, which was cupboard, said in the voice his uncle used when his hand came down flat on the table. He did not want the sound again. He did not want the flat hand. The bottle in the air and those two things had come together once and he kept them together, the way he had kept his hand away from the fire since the day it had been hot.

He lay in the cot that night in the room with the boxes and listened to the house: the long slow tick of the clock in the hall, which to him was a sound with a beginning and a middle and an end, the drawn-out hum of the fridge, the pipes, the bed upstairs creaking as his uncle turned over in it, the wind at the window. He knew them all apart by now. He went through them one at a time, as he did every night, and put each where it belonged, and then he went back to the bottle, and the way it had come, and the way he had seen it coming before it came.
