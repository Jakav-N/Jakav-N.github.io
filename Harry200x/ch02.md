# Chapter Two

*March to December 1982*

By March he could follow nearly everything said in the house. The trouble was saying anything back.

Understanding had only needed listening, and he could listen for as long as it took. Speaking needed the mouth, and his mouth went at the world's pace, like his arm. He knew exactly how water ought to sound; he had heard his aunt say it to Dudley a hundred times. What came out when he tried it, lying in the cot at night with the door shut, was a shape with the right beginning and nothing after it, and the next attempt was no better for all the thought he had given the first. Tongue and lips had to learn in their own time. He gave them an hour a night, in whispers, listening to each try come out long and wrong, telling them what to change. They changed a little. It took weeks.

He chose his first word with care. The word his aunt wanted most in the world was please. She said it to Dudley a dozen times a day with his juice held out of reach, and Dudley hit the cup and got the juice anyway, and the word went unsaid. So one April morning in the kitchen, strapped into the high chair that had been Dudley's, Harry waited until his aunt reached for the spoon.

"Please," he said.

She stopped with the spoon in the air.

"Water, please." It was what he wanted, and he had the second word ready, and he did not know when he would get another chance to use it.

She looked at him for a long time by anyone's measure. Then she filled his cup at the tap, gave it to him, fed him, and said nothing about it until that evening, when the kitchen door was shut and his uncle was home.

"He said please. Clear as you like. And then water."

"Dudley says please."

"Dudley says won't." A pause. "He looked right at me, Vernon."

"Showing off. You said she was the same."

"Yes," his aunt said, and that was the end of it.

He had his water. He had also been looked at in a way he did not want, and the two things had arrived together. After that he spoke only when he wanted something badly enough to be looked at for it, which was not often. Speaking was slow in any case. A sentence took a long time to get out of him and his aunt's answer took longer to arrive, and between the two he had more time than he could use.

---

That spring the television had a war on it.

His uncle watched the news with the sound up. The news was ships, and maps with arrows, and a man in a suit in front of the ships saying the names of other ships and the numbers of men. Harry liked the maps. He learned the shape of the islands before he learned where they were, and he learned casualties, a long word for something the man said in a particular voice. There were photographs of men in uniform, one after another, and then a ship burning on grey water. "Terrible," his aunt said, and went to put the kettle on. His uncle was pleased when a ship on the other side went down with a great many men in it, and said so. Harry held that with the rest.

He had the car crash by then. It had come in his aunt's lowered voice, and the news supplied the rest: people stopped and did not get up, at sea, on motorways, in houses, and other people said so in that particular voice and then put the kettle on. His mother had not got up. He had known that the way he knew the ceiling, as a thing that was there. Now he had the word for it. The word was in the news every evening, and it belonged to a great many people besides him.

---

The new television came in May.

His uncle carried it in from the car himself and would not let his aunt help. It had a handset with buttons; press the right ones and the picture gave way to pages of writing, white and yellow and green on black, with numbers along the top. His uncle had bought it for the cricket and the share prices, as he told his aunt several times, and Marge on the telephone. The old set went into the room with the boxes with its flex wound round it and stood in the corner facing the wall.

His aunt found the subtitles by pressing buttons while Dudley screamed. She had a programme in the evenings that she did not like to miss, and Dudley had no patience with it. One evening in June the words appeared along the bottom of the picture in a black box, a line or two at a time, a little behind the voices. She left them on so that she could follow the story over the noise, and after that she left them on most evenings from habit.

For Harry, in the pen, a line of subtitle stayed on the screen a very long time. The voice that went with it arrived slowly, as voices did, and he could hold the two together and look from one to the other at leisure. He already had the words. What he had lacked was their spelling, and now he had a line of it every few seconds for an hour each evening, with the sound to check it against. Within a week he had the letters. Within a month he had most of the rules, and after that the exceptions, which outnumbered the rules and came one at a time, as the noises of the house had.

Then there was print everywhere. The newspaper his uncle left folded on the arm of his chair gave him the top half of the front page and, when it slid to the floor, the rest. The cereal box on the kitchen table he read once. The Radio Times lay on the settee. By the telephone his aunt kept a thick catalogue with a picture on every page and a price beneath it, often two prices, the old one crossed out, the new one beside it, and the difference written in red; he learned his numbers from the prices and his sums from the differences, because the catalogue said what a thing had cost and what it cost now and what you saved, and the three had to agree, and they did, whenever he checked.

He was careful. He read the newspaper when his uncle was out of the room and his aunt in the kitchen. He did not reach for things. He did not turn a page where anyone could see him turn it. By day the only things in his hands were what his aunt put there, which were Dudley's old toys, and he looked at those as a baby was meant to and read the cereal box past them.

Dudley's birthday fell in June. There was a party. Marge came with a dog, and Harry was shut in the room with the boxes for the afternoon, out of the way. He could climb out of the cot by then. The box under the window held the Ladybird books Dudley had thrown and his aunt had tidied away, and beneath them last year's catalogue with half its pages gone; he read all of it sitting on the floor with his back to the cot, and was back in the cot with the lid on the box before the door opened. Nobody mentioned a birthday for him. He did not know he had one to expect.

---

The bottle had been in February. He did not try again until the room with the boxes was his at night, with the door shut and the house asleep. The cupboard was one reason for waiting. The other was that he had only one thing to go on: that he had seen the bottle coming before it came.

So he began with that. A sock had come off in the night and lay at the end of the cot. He looked at it and saw it rising, as clearly as he had seen the bottle, and it rose. It rose exactly as far as he had seen it rise, and hung there, and when he stopped seeing it hang it fell. He did it again. He did it with the other sock, and then with a bead from the jar on the windowsill, which came to him across the room in a straight line at the pace of a slow walk. The bottle had come at that pace. He began to think it was the only pace there was.

Wanting did nothing. He tested that: he wanted the sock to move as hard as he had wanted the bottle, hungry in the pen, and kept his mind off how it would move, and it lay where it was. The picture did the work, and the picture had to be finished. He learned that from the jar of buttons. He saw it lift off the sill and come towards him and did not see it land, because he had not decided where it would land, and it came, and stopped in the air above the cot where the picture stopped, and dropped. The jar cracked on the floor and the buttons went everywhere. He spent the rest of the night on the floor in the dark picking them up one at a time by feel, and he hid the cracked jar at the bottom of a box under a pile of curtains. His aunt did not notice. Nothing in that room was looked at.

The light was harder. He wanted to read at night and the room was dark, so he saw a light above his hand, small and steady like the landing light through the crack of the door; nothing came. He saw it as the dot the old television had shrunk to, then as the bulb on the ceiling. For many nights nothing came. Then something did, a faintness over his palm that he was not sure of until he moved his hand and it moved too. After that it was practice, which went at the world's pace like everything else, and by autumn he had a light the size of an egg that lasted as long as he held it and went out when he let go. By it he read the Ladybird books, all twelve, and the back of the catalogue, and the labels on the boxes, which said Xmas Decs, Dudley Baby Clothes, and Marge's Things Do Not Throw.

That was the whole of what he could do, and he knew it. Socks, beads, pages. The books in the box were too heavy for anything but his hands. Anything larger did nothing however he looked at it. He did not know why the bottle had come and the books would not, and there was nobody to ask.

---

What put him in the cupboard was nothing he did on purpose.

In October he dreamed of the green light. He did not often dream, or did not remember it, and of this one he kept only the light, the stick and the pain in his forehead. Then he was awake and the room was wrong. Every box in it, from under the window and from the corner by the old television, stood stacked against the door in a wall that reached the handle. The bulb had come out of its socket and lay in pieces on the floor between the cot and the wall. He had done it, and he had no memory of doing it.

In the morning his aunt could not open the door. His uncle came up and put his shoulder to it, and the boxes went over, and he stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves looking at the room and at Harry in the cot without saying anything for a long time by anyone's measure.

By that evening Harry was in the cupboard under the stairs. His uncle cleared out the vacuum cleaner and the coats and the ironing board, laid the cot mattress on the floor, and fitted a bolt to the outside of the door with a screwdriver from the garage. His aunt stood in the hall with her arms folded. "At least it's where we can shut the door," she said. "Yes," said his uncle, and shut it.

Harry lay on the mattress in the dark and took stock. The dark did not matter; he made his light and looked round. The cupboard was the width of the mattress and a little longer, with the underside of the stairs for a ceiling, stepping down towards the far end, and a spider in the highest corner. The quiet was good. There was no television in here, but there had been none in the room with the boxes either, and he still spent the days in the pen, which was where the television was. The one new thing was the bolt.

He looked at the door where the bolt would be on the other side and saw it sliding back, and heard it slide. He saw it slide home again, and heard that too. Then he put out his light and lay in the dark and went nowhere. There was nowhere yet to go.

---

In December his uncle's firm gave a dinner for the directors and their wives, and his aunt needed somewhere to put the boys.

Dudley went to a friend of hers called Yvonne. Harry went to Mrs Figg. He knew Mrs Figg from the pushchair: an old woman with a string bag who stopped his aunt in the street and looked at him over the top of the pushchair while they talked. His aunt had said once, afterwards, that she smelled of cabbage and he was not to stare.

She lived two streets away on Wisteria Walk. His aunt pushed him there in the dark with Dudley's old blanket over his knees and handed him over on the step with his bag. "He's no trouble," she said. "He's very quiet." Mrs Figg said, "I'm sure," and took him in.

The house did smell of cabbage. It smelled of cabbage and of cats, of which there were four, and of a gas fire that had been on a long time. There were photographs of cats on every surface that would take a frame, the top of the television included, and the cats themselves sat on the chairs and the windowsill and the stairs, and all four looked at him as he came in and went on looking. Mrs Figg put him on the hearthrug in front of the fire, gave him a biscuit, and told him not to let Mr Tibbles have any. She talked to him as she talked to the cats, a good deal and without waiting for an answer, in a dry, slightly cracked voice.

"That's Mr Tibbles. Don't stare at him, he'll only stare back, he's got no manners. Snowy, off the arm. That's Mr Paws, he's not well, don't go near his tail. Tufty's under the sideboard. She'll come out when she's ready and not before."

Mr Tibbles did stare back. He was a big cat with a flat face and yellow eyes, and he sat an arm's length from Harry with his tail round his feet and did not blink while the clock on the mantelpiece ticked eleven times. Harry counted, because no animal had ever looked at him like that and he wanted to know how long it would last. It lasted all evening.

Mrs Figg made tea, lowered herself into the chair by the fire, and looked at him too, over the cup. Was he warm enough? Had he had his tea? He said yes to both. "Good," she said, and that was the conversation for a while.

He was looking at the shelf by the fire, because it had books on it. She saw him looking, got up, took down a big one with a cat on the cover, and put it in his lap. "Look at the pictures. Mind the pages." He looked at the pictures. He also read the words under them, and the facing page, and turned over and read on, and he was on the fourth page before he noticed that she had stopped drinking her tea and was watching his eyes travel along the lines.

"You're not looking at the pictures."

"No."

"What's that say, then? That bit, under the grey one."

He read it to her. It took a while in his voice, which still stumbled on the longer words and had to go back for one. The Russian Blue, it said, is a quiet and reserved cat which forms a strong attachment to one person. Mrs Figg heard him out and set her cup down on the hearth.

"Read me the next bit."

He read her the next bit, about the Abyssinian, and the bit after that, until she said, "All right," and he stopped. She sat back. Mr Tibbles left the rug and went to sit on the arm of her chair, and the two of them looked at him together.

"Who taught you that?"

"Nobody. My aunt reads to Dudley." She had asked, so he gave her the rest. "The television has the words on it."

"Does your aunt know you can do that?"

"No."

"Hm." She looked at him a while longer. "You're two," she said, not quite to him. "Two and a bit." Then she asked whether he would like another biscuit, and he said yes please, and had it, and she let him keep the book on his knees for the rest of the evening.

Later she went into the kitchen to wash the cups, and Mr Tibbles went with her, and she talked to him over the running tap. She did not lower her voice; she had no reason to think she needed to, with the boy in the other room and the water running. Harry lay on the hearthrug with the cat book and held the words as they came through the wall, long and slow and perfectly clear.

"He said to keep an eye on him. He never said anything about this." The tap went off. "Dumbledore'll want to hear, Mr Tibbles. I shall have to write."

His aunt came for him at eleven with the pushchair and hoped he had been no bother. No bother at all, said Mrs Figg; she would have him any time. "That's very kind," his aunt said, and took him home, and put him in the cupboard, and bolted the door.

He lay in the dark without his light and went over the evening, as he went over every day. Most of it was the cats and the fire and the taste of the biscuit. Two pieces he kept apart from the rest: Mrs Figg setting her cup down on the hearth when he read to her, and asking whether his aunt knew; and a word he had not had that morning, said to a cat over a running tap, which was Dumbledore, and which was going to be written to.
