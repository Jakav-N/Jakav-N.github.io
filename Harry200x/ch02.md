# Chapter Two

*March to December 1982*

By March he could follow nearly everything that was said in the house, and the trouble was saying anything back.

Understanding had been a matter of listening, and he could listen for as long as it took. Speaking was a matter of the mouth, and the mouth was his, and it went at the world's pace, like his arm. He could hear in his head exactly how water ought to sound. He had heard his aunt say it to Dudley a hundred times. What came out when he tried it, lying in the cot at night with the door shut, was a shape with the right beginning and nothing after it, and the next try was no better for his having gone over the first one at length. The tongue and the lips had to learn in their own time. He gave them an hour a night, in whispers, and listened to each attempt come out long and wrong, and told them what to change, and they changed a little, and it took weeks.

He chose his first word with some care. The word his aunt wanted most in the world was please. She said it to Dudley a dozen times a day, holding his juice out of reach, and Dudley hit the cup and got the juice anyway, and the word went unsaid. So one morning in April, in the kitchen, strapped into the high chair that had been Dudley's, Harry waited until his aunt reached for the spoon, and said, "Please." She stopped with the spoon in the air. He said, "Water, please," because that was what he wanted, and because he had the second word ready and did not know when he would get another chance to use it.

She looked at him for a long time, by his measure and by hers. Then she filled his cup at the tap and gave it to him, and fed him, and said nothing about it, and that evening in the kitchen with the door shut she told his uncle.

"He said please. Clear as you like. And then water."

"Dudley says please."

"Dudley says won't." A pause. "He looked right at me, Vernon."

"Showing off. You said she was the same."

"Yes," his aunt said, and that was the end of it.

He had his water, and he had been looked at in a way he had not wanted, and the two had come together. He spoke after that when there was something he wanted badly enough to be looked at for, which was not often. Speaking was slow in any case. A sentence took a long time to get out of him, and his aunt's answer took longer to arrive, and between the two he could go over everything he had heard that day, twice, and still be waiting.

---

That spring the television had a war on it.

His uncle watched the news with the sound up, and the news was ships, and maps with arrows on them, and a man in a suit standing in front of the ships saying the names of other ships and the numbers of men. Harry liked the maps. He learned the shape of the islands before he learned where they were, and he learned casualties, which was a long word for a thing the man said in a particular voice. There were photographs of men in uniform, one after another, and then a ship on fire in grey water, and his aunt said, "Terrible," and went to put the kettle on. His uncle was pleased when a ship on the other side went down with a great many men in it, and said so, and Harry held that with the rest.

He had the car crash by then. It had been in his aunt's lowered voice, and the news gave him the rest of it: people stopped, and did not get up, and it happened at sea and on motorways and in houses, and other people said it in a particular voice and then put the kettle on. His mother had not got up. He had known that in the way he knew the ceiling, as a thing that was there. Now he had the word for it, and the word was in the news every evening, and belonged to a great many other people besides him.

---

The new television came in May.

His uncle carried it in from the car himself and would not let his aunt help. It had a handset with buttons, and if you pressed the right ones the picture went away and pages of writing came instead, white and yellow and green on black, with numbers at the top. His uncle bought it for the cricket and the share prices and said so several times, to his aunt and to Marge on the telephone and to the man who came to take the old set to the tip. The old set did not go to the tip. It went into the room with the boxes, with its flex wound round it, and stood in the corner facing the wall.

His aunt found the page with the subtitles on it by pressing buttons while Dudley screamed. She had a programme in the evenings she did not like to miss, and Dudley did not care for it, and one evening in June the words came up along the bottom of the picture in a black box, a line or two at a time, a little after the people on the screen had said them. She left them there so that she could follow the story over the noise. She left them there after that most evenings, out of habit.

For Harry, in the pen, a line of subtitle stayed on the screen for a very long time. The voice that went with it arrived slowly, as voices did, and he could hold the two together and look from one to the other at his leisure. He already had the words. What he had not had was how they were spelled, and now he had a line of it every few seconds for an hour every evening, with the sound to check it against. He had the letters within the week. He had most of the rules within the month, and the exceptions after that, one at a time, as they came, the way he had learned the noises of the house. English spelling turned out to have been argued over for a long time by people who had not agreed, and there was nothing to do about that but learn it.

After that there was print everywhere. The newspaper his uncle left folded on the arm of his chair, from which he could read the top half of the front page and, when it slid to the floor, the rest. The cereal box on the kitchen table, which he read every morning until he could have recited it. The Radio Times on the settee. The catalogue his aunt kept by the telephone, a thick one with a picture of something on every page and a price under it, and sometimes two prices, the old one crossed out and the new one beside it and the difference written in red. He learned his numbers from the prices and his sums from the differences, because the catalogue said what a thing had been and what it was now and what you saved, and the three had to agree, and they always did, when he checked.

He was careful about it. He read the newspaper when his uncle was out of the room and his aunt was in the kitchen. He did not reach for things. He did not turn pages where anyone could see him turn them. In the daytime the only things in his hands were the things his aunt put there, which were Dudley's old toys, and he looked at those the way a baby was meant to look at them, and read the cereal box past them.

Dudley's birthday was in June. There was a party, and Marge came with a dog, and Harry was put in the room with the boxes for the afternoon with the door shut, out of the way. He could climb out of the cot by then. The box nearest the window had the Ladybird books in it, the ones Dudley had thrown and his aunt had tidied away, and under them the catalogue from the year before with half its pages gone, and he read all of it, sitting on the floor with his back to the cot, and was back in the cot with the lid on the box before the door opened. Nobody mentioned a birthday for him. He did not know that he had one to expect.

---

The bottle had been in February, and he had not tried it again until the room with the boxes was his at night and the door was shut and the house was asleep. There were two reasons for the wait. The first was the cupboard. The second was that he had wanted to think about it, and had, and had come to no conclusion except that he had seen the bottle coming before it came.

So he began with that. There was a sock on the end of the cot that had come off in the night, and he looked at it and saw it rising, as clearly as he had seen the bottle, and it rose. It rose as far as he had seen it rise and stopped there, and hung, and when he stopped seeing it hanging it fell. He did it again. He did it with the other sock. He did it with a bead from the jar of buttons and beads on the windowsill, and the bead came across the room to him in a straight line at the pace of a slow walk, which was the pace the bottle had come at, and he began to think that was the only pace there was.

Wanting did nothing. He tested that. He wanted the sock to move as hard as he had wanted the bottle, hungry, in the pen, and kept his mind off how it would move, and it lay there. The picture was the whole of it. The picture had to be complete, too. He found that out with the jar of buttons, which he saw lifting off the sill and coming to him and did not see landing, because he had not decided where it should land, and it came, and stopped in the air above the cot where the picture stopped, and dropped. The jar cracked on the floor. The buttons went everywhere. He spent the rest of the night on the floor in the dark picking them up by hand, one at a time, feeling for them, and put the cracked jar at the bottom of a box under a pile of curtains, and his aunt did not notice. Nothing in that room was looked at.

The light was harder. He wanted to read at night and the room was dark, and he saw a light over his hand, small and steady like the landing light through the crack of the door, and nothing came. He saw it differently. He saw it as the dot the old television had shrunk to. He saw it as the bulb on the ceiling. Nothing came for many nights, and then something did, a faintness over his palm that he was not sure of until he moved his hand and it moved with it. After that it was practice, which went at the world's pace like everything else, and by the autumn he had a light the size of an egg that stayed as long as he held it and went out when he stopped. He read the Ladybird books by it, all twelve, and the back of the catalogue, and the labels on the boxes, which said Xmas Decs and Dudley Baby Clothes and Marge's Things Do Not Throw.

That was the whole of what he could do, and he knew it. Socks and beads and pages. The books in the box were too heavy for him to lift except by hand. Anything bigger did nothing at all when he looked at it. He did not know why the bottle had come and the books would not, and he did not have anyone to ask.

---

What took him to the cupboard was not anything he did on purpose.

He dreamed, in October, of the green light. He did not often dream, or did not remember it, and this one he remembered only as the light and the stick and the pain in his forehead, and then he was awake, and the room was wrong. The boxes had moved. All of them, the ones from under the window and the ones from the corner by the old television, were stacked against the door in a wall that reached the handle, and the bulb had come out of its socket and was in pieces on the floor between the cot and the wall, and he had done it, and he had no memory of doing it at all.

His aunt could not open the door in the morning. His uncle came up and put his shoulder to it and the boxes went over, and his uncle stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves and looked at the room and at Harry in the cot and did not say anything for a long time, by anyone's measure.

He was in the cupboard under the stairs by that evening. His uncle moved the vacuum cleaner and the coats and the ironing board out of it and put the cot mattress in, on the floor, and fitted a bolt to the outside of the door with a screwdriver from the garage, and his aunt stood in the hall with her arms folded and said, "At least it's where we can shut the door," and his uncle said, "Yes," and shut it.

He lay on the mattress in the dark and took stock. The dark was nothing; he had his light, and he made it, and looked round. The cupboard was the width of the mattress and a little longer, with the underside of the stairs for a ceiling, going down in steps to the far end, and a spider in the highest corner that he watched for a while, because its legs moved slowly enough to see how they worked. The quiet was good. There was no television in the cupboard, but there had been no television in the room with the boxes either, and he was still in the pen in the daytime, and that was where the television was. The one thing that was different was the bolt.

He looked at the door where the bolt would be, on the other side, and saw it sliding back, and heard it slide. He saw it sliding home again, and heard that too. Then he put his light out and lay in the dark, and did not go anywhere. There was nowhere yet to go.

---

In December his uncle's firm gave a dinner for the directors and their wives, and his aunt needed somewhere to put the boys.

Dudley went to a friend of hers called Yvonne. Harry went to Mrs Figg. He had seen Mrs Figg before, from the pushchair, an old woman with a string bag who stopped to talk to his aunt in the street and looked at him over the top of the pushchair while she did it, and his aunt had said once, after, that she smelled of cabbage and he was not to stare.

She lived two streets away on Wisteria Walk. His aunt pushed him there in the dark with Dudley's old blanket over his knees and handed him over on the step with his bag, and said, "He's no trouble. He's very quiet," and Mrs Figg said, "I'm sure," and took him in.

The house did smell of cabbage. It smelled of cabbage and of cats, of which there were four, and of a gas fire that had been on for a long time. There were photographs of cats on every surface that would take a frame, including the top of the television, and the cats themselves were on the chairs and the windowsill and the stairs, and all four of them looked at him when he came in and went on looking. Mrs Figg sat him on the hearthrug in front of the fire and gave him a biscuit and talked to him in a voice he had heard his aunt use on Dudley and nobody else, high and slow, and talked to the cats in her ordinary voice, which was dry and a little cracked and had a great deal more in it.

"Mr Tibbles, don't stare at him, it's rude. Snowy, off the arm. That's Mr Paws, dear, he's not well, don't mind him. Tufty's under the sideboard, she'll come out when she's ready."

Mr Tibbles did not stop staring. He was a big cat with a flat face and yellow eyes, and he sat an arm's length from Harry on the rug with his tail round his feet and did not blink for as long as it took the clock on the mantelpiece to tick eleven times, which Harry counted, because he had never been looked at by an animal like that and wanted to know how long it would go on. It went on all evening.

Mrs Figg made tea and came back with it and lowered herself into the chair by the fire and looked at him too, over the cup.

"Can you say your name, dear? Can you say Harry?"

He considered it. Speaking was slow and it bought the look. But she was not his aunt, and she had asked, and he found that he wanted to see what she did with the answer.

"Harry," he said.

She went still, the way his aunt had gone still on the step with the letter, and Mr Tibbles turned his head and looked at her, and then back at Harry.

"Well," Mrs Figg said, in her ordinary voice. "You can talk, then."

"Yes."

"Does your aunt know you can talk like that?"

"Yes."

"Hm," said Mrs Figg, and drank her tea, and did not use the high slow voice again that evening or ever.

He was looking at the shelf by the fire, because it had books on it, and she saw him looking and got up and took one down, a big one with a cat on the cover, and put it in his lap. "You can look at the pictures," she said. He looked at the pictures. He also read the words under them, and then the words on the facing page, and turned over, and read those, and turned over, and he was on the fourth page before he understood that she had stopped drinking her tea and was watching his eyes go along the lines.

"What's that say, then," she said. "That bit. Under the grey one."

He read it to her. It took a while, in his voice, which was still not good at the longer words and had to go back for one of them. The Russian Blue, it said, is a quiet and reserved cat which forms a strong attachment to one person. Mrs Figg listened to the end of it and then set her cup down on the hearth and sat back in her chair, and Mr Tibbles got up and went and sat on the arm of it beside her, and the two of them looked at him together.

"How old are you, Harry?"

He did not know. He said so. She asked him what else he could read, and he told her: the newspaper, the catalogue, the television, the boxes. She asked whether anyone had taught him. He said his aunt read to Dudley. She asked nothing else for a time, and then she asked whether he would like another biscuit, and he said yes please, and had it.

Later she went into the kitchen to wash the cups, and Mr Tibbles went with her, and she talked to him while she ran the tap. She did not lower her voice. She did not think she needed to, with him in the other room and the water running, and he lay on the hearthrug with the cat book and held the words as they came through the wall, long and slow and perfectly clear.

"He said to keep an eye on him. He never said anything about this." The tap went off. "Dumbledore'll want to hear, Mr Tibbles. I shall have to write."

His aunt came for him at eleven, with the pushchair, and said she hoped he had been no bother. Mrs Figg said that he had been no bother at all and that she would have him any time, and his aunt said, "That's very kind," and took him home and put him in the cupboard and bolted the door.

He lay in the dark and did not make his light. He went over the evening instead, as he went over every day, and most of it was the cats and the fire and the taste of the biscuit. Two pieces he kept apart from the rest. Mrs Figg had gone still at his name, the way his aunt had gone still on the step. And there was a word he had not had that morning, said to a cat with the tap running, which was Dumbledore, and which was going to be written to.
