# Chapter Three

*January to August 1983*

The first time he left the cupboard at night was in January, because the encyclopaedia had come.

A man in a raincoat had brought it to the door in the first week of the year, carrying a case, and his uncle had let him in, which his uncle did not do with men at the door. Harry watched from the pen. The man opened the case on the dining table, took out a book with a red cover and gold on the spine, opened it at a picture of a volcano, and talked for most of an hour. His uncle glanced at Dudley, who was under the table, and signed something. A fortnight later a box arrived. Twenty red books went onto the bottom shelf in the front room, still in their plastic, where his aunt dusted them on Fridays with everything else on the shelf. Nobody opened one.

He slid the bolt on a Tuesday night, after the hall clock had struck one. By then he had listened to the house for an hour and knew where everyone was by their breathing: his uncle's coming through the floor like a saw, his aunt's lighter and further off, Dudley's with the catch it had had since Christmas. He saw the bolt drawing back, heard it draw, pushed the door, and went out into the dark hall on his hands and knees, which was quieter than feet.

By his light the front room was a different room. The light was still the size of an egg and showed him a circle no bigger than a dinner plate, so that the room arrived one plate at a time: the arm of his uncle's chair, a table leg, the glass front of the cabinet with the china behind it, the shelf. He got the plastic off the first volume with his teeth in the end. Then he sat on the carpet with his back to the shelf, the book open on his knees and the light above the page, and read.

The book was slower than he was. His eyes travelled along a line and down to the next at the pace eyes travel, and nothing could hurry them. He could make them jump sooner than most people's, since he had no thinking to wait for between one jump and the next, and he took in as much at each stop as the eye would give; even so, a page took about as long as his uncle took to climb the stairs. Inside each page he sat with time to spare and nothing to spend it on but the page. That night he read the first volume and the second and was into the third when the first bird sang. He pressed the plastic flat round them again, returned them to their places, went back along the hall, drew the cupboard door to, and watched and heard the bolt slide home. On Friday his aunt dusted the shelf without looking at the plastic.

The twenty volumes took him a fortnight. He went out five nights in seven and read until the first bird. He learned which floorboards spoke, and that his uncle got up once in the night at about half past two, for which the door had to be bolted and the light out. The encyclopaedia had been written for children and said so; still, it held the Romans and the planets, the working of a steam engine and the names of the bones, and he took it all, because he did not yet know what he would need, and it stayed.

---

After the encyclopaedia came the rest of the shelf, and after the shelf, the house.

The dictionary took four nights, read straight through. The atlas he read first as a book, country by country, and then as one picture, until he could have drawn the coasts. Six fat volumes marked Reader's Digest turned out to hold four novels each, cut short; these were the first stories he had read, and they worked on him differently. The encyclopaedia had told him what things were. The stories told him what people were like inside, which until then he had only had from the outside, from faces and the lowered voice, and the two matched more often than not. One of them had a woman in it who kept her house very clean, loved her son past all sense, and had no kind word for anyone else. He thought about her for a long while.

The kitchen gave him his aunt's cookery books and the back of every packet. Under the telephone directory in the hall lay a thicker yellow one that listed every business in the town by trade, and from it he learned that Little Whinging had three plumbers, two solicitors, a dentist, a man who repaired televisions, and a public library on the High Street open Tuesday to Saturday, about which he could do nothing. Down the side of his uncle's chair were the Highway Code and a manual for the car. The television, with the sound turned to nothing and the door shut, would show him the pages of writing, and once the shelf was done he gave an hour most nights to those with his back to the stairs. Each page stayed up for half a minute, which was a long while for him. He read the news and the weather for places he had found in the atlas, and the share prices, which rose and fell for reasons the pages did not give. He began to want the reasons.

By the end of February nothing in the house with print on it remained unread, except whatever was in his aunt's bureau.

After that the print arrived by the day, and he learned what a day's worth was. The newspaper on the arm of the chair was an hour. The pages on the television had been one whole night the first time; now they were only what had changed since yesterday, and that took about as long as the kettle. The local paper came on Thursdays. The post was bills. In between were the nights, and the nights were long.

---

The bureau stood in the corner of the front room by the window. It had a flap that came down to write on and three drawers beneath, and the keys were in the drawers. His aunt did her letters at it on Sunday afternoons; he had seen her take things from the bottom drawer and put them back. He had left it alone while there was a shelf to read, and because it was hers in a way the shelf was not, and he knew what the flat hand went with.

He opened it in March. In the bottom drawer was a biscuit tin with a Scottish castle on the lid, and in the tin were papers: his aunt and uncle's marriage lines, Dudley's birth certificate, photographs of his aunt as a girl beside another girl with red hair who was laughing, and two letters whose envelopes had been opened and closed so many times that the flaps had gone soft. Both were addressed in the same slanting, looping hand, in green ink. One said Miss Petunia Evans. The other said Mrs Petunia Dursley.

He read the second one first, because it was the letter from the step; he knew the envelope. It ran to two closely written sheets. It began by saying that the writer was very sorry to be the bearer of the news it contained, and then it contained the news.

Lily and James Potter were dead. They had been killed on the night of the thirty-first of October at their house in a place called Godric's Hollow, by a man the letter named as Lord Voldemort, a name Petunia would know, or at least the fear of it. Their son had lived. The man had tried to kill the boy as well, had failed, and was gone, though the writer did not believe him dead. In dying for her son, the letter said, Lily had left a protection on him, and that protection would hold while he lived under the roof of her blood, which was Petunia's roof. The writer asked her to take the boy in and raise him, knowing it was a great deal to ask, and said what might come looking for him if she did not. The boy's name was Harry James Potter. He had been one year old on the thirty-first of July. The letter was signed Albus Dumbledore.

Harry sat on the carpet for a while with the letter in his hands and his light above it. Then he read the other one.

It was older and shorter, and it was written to a girl. The writer thanked her for her letter and said she had written it with great feeling. She could not come to the school, he said, because she had no magic, and magic was not a thing anyone could give her; he was sorry. She had a sister who loved her very much, and he hoped the two of them would not let this come between them. He wished her well. The hand had not changed in ten years, and the signature was the same.

He folded the letters into their envelopes, laid the envelopes in the tin under the photographs, put the tin in the drawer and shut it, and sat where he was with the light out.

He was two years and seven months old, and his birthday was in July. He had known neither.

He had thought about his aunt a great deal, there being so much time and so little else, and he had thought about her the way he had learned to think about the people in the novels, from what she did. What she did was feed him and keep him and not look at him, and love Dudley with the whole of herself in a voice she kept for nobody else. Now he had some of the inside of it. It changed what he saw when she crossed the room to him in the mornings at her wading pace with her mouth set. It did not change what she did; he had not expected it to. But after March he looked at her differently, and in the long slow seconds of her crossing the carpet what he found he felt about her, more than anything else, was sorry.

---

The magic went on in the cupboard at night, and it did not go far.

He could draw the bolt now without looking at the door. The light had steadied and grown to the size of an orange, and no longer wavered when he turned his head. If a book on the shelf was thin, and he saw the whole of its path, he could pull it across the carpet to him; he could turn a page without touching it, which was no use, since his hands were there anyway. In January, when the cupboard was cold enough for his breath to show in the light, he had tried for warmth, picturing his hands as they were when he held them to the gas fire at Mrs Figg's. After a week of nights he got something: a heat in the palms that lasted as long as the picture did and left him tired afterwards. He used it on the coldest nights, and never for long.

That was all. Small, light things moved, and nothing else did. Through the empty nights of March he tried a good many other things, one a night, with a night between each so that if anything went wrong it would be a small wrong and his own. Nothing went wrong. Nothing much went right either. The same things worked every night and the same things failed, so there was a reason; he did not have it, and nobody in the house could give it to him.

There was a pencil on the flap of the bureau, and he took that into the cupboard as well. He could not write. His hand was two years old and had never held a pencil, and though he knew from the Highway Code and the backs of packets what writing ought to look like, his fingers could no more produce it than his mouth had once been able to produce water. He had no wish to write for himself; there was nothing he would ever need to read again. A list for somebody else, though, would have to be written down, and he had somebody in mind. So he practised on the margins of the newspaper in the rack before it went out to the bin, letters first and then words, an hour a night. For a long while the letters were very bad. Then they were less bad. His aunt found the pencil on the hall floor once, put it back on the bureau, and thought nothing of it.

He did sums, too. The encyclopaedia had given him numbers and what could be done with them, fractions and the areas of shapes and a page on the numbers that would not divide, and when there was nothing left to read he went on from where it had stopped. He found things about those numbers that the encyclopaedia had not said, and things about the shapes, with no way of knowing whether anyone had found them before him, or whether they were even true, except by working them again. It was the first thing he had ever had to think about that did not run out.

The days were better than the nights, because the days had the wireless. His aunt kept it on in the kitchen from breakfast until the lunchtime news. People on it argued about the government; a woman talked to other women about their lives; there were plays, and a serial about a farm. Harry sat on the kitchen floor with Dudley's bricks and listened to all of it. When the weather allowed, his aunt put him out in the garden, and he went along the flowerbeds looking at what grew in them, which he had read about and never seen.

In April he asked his aunt for a library ticket.

He asked plainly, at breakfast, once his uncle had gone to work. His aunt looked at him over the teapot and said that he was two, that they did not give tickets to two-year-olds, and that she would thank him not to go on about it. He did not go on about it. He waited for the next time Mrs Figg stopped them in the street, and then, while his aunt was saying something about the weather, he looked up out of the pushchair and asked Mrs Figg whether she had any more books.

His aunt's face went red. Mrs Figg studied him over the top of the pushchair for a while before she turned to his aunt. As a matter of fact, she said, she had a shelf full; and if Petunia ever wanted the boy off her hands for a day, she was only two streets away, and the cats would not mind. His aunt, whose Tuesdays were her hair and then the big shop with Dudley to get round the supermarket, said that was very kind. The following Tuesday Harry was on Mrs Figg's step with his bag.

Mrs Figg's shelf lasted two Tuesdays. There was a row of green-backed detective stories on the landing, a fat blue book called Pears Cyclopaedia by the fire that held a great deal the red encyclopaedia had not, and the cat books, and that was the shelf. On the third Tuesday he gave her the list. It was written in pencil on the back of an envelope, in letters that leaned and would not keep to a line, and it said what he wanted from the library on the High Street: a history of England that was not for children; a book about the stars; a book about money, and why the prices on the television went up and down; a book about the body; a book of sums with letters in them. Mrs Figg read it twice.

"Where did you get algebra from?"

"The encyclopaedia said there was such a thing. It didn't say what."

"Hm," said Mrs Figg, and put the envelope in her bag. On the Tuesday after there were six books on the low table when he came in, each with the library's stamp inside the cover. The woman at the desk had asked who they were for, Mrs Figg said, and she had told her a boy she minded, which was true, and the woman had said nothing further. There were six every week after that. He had them read by Tuesday night and the rest of the week to think about them, and gave her the next list on the back of the next envelope. His writing improved, because someone had to read it.

Most Tuesdays she asked him what he had made of what she had brought. Nobody had asked him that before. He found he had a great deal to say, and that saying it was still slow, and that she waited, with Mr Tibbles on the arm of her chair, and sometimes told him he was wrong.

About Dumbledore she said nothing. She said nothing about his aunt either, or his mother, or the letter, or why she had been told to keep an eye on him, and he did not ask, because he could see her not saying it. Some Tuesday afternoons she wrote at the kitchen table with her back to the door and put the letter in her bag before he could see the address. He let her be.

---

The summer he turned three there was an election, and a man came to the door about it.

The man stood on the step in a blue rosette and talked to his uncle for a long time. His uncle agreed with everything, shut the door, and said something else about him to his aunt. That month the news was the election and nothing else: men at lecterns, crowds, the long slow sweep of a hand across a map that turned blue as it went, and the number that came up every evening on the pages of writing, the number of people without work, which had a three at the front and six noughts after it. Harry knew what a million was by then. He looked at the number and at the men at the lecterns and could not make the two go together.

The local paper came through the door on Thursdays. His aunt read it for the houses and threw it out, and he read it from the rack in the hall at night before it reached the bin. In June it carried a boy who had gone into the canal after a football and not come out, with his school photograph and his age underneath, which was six. It carried a woman whose chip pan had caught, and her house with it, and a man who had lost his job at the works and then his van. Harry read all of it. From the novels he knew what people were like inside; the Thursday paper gave him the outside, and the two went together as they always had, and he did not like what they made.

He had been thinking for a long time about what he wanted. There was so much time, and the question kept coming round in it like the pages on the television. For a long while the answers had been small and near: the bottle, the light, out of the cupboard, into the books. He had those now, or as many of them as could be had. In the summer he turned three the question came round with the Thursday paper in it.

What he found, when he looked at it straight, was that he minded. He minded the boy in the canal, whom he had never seen, in the way he had minded the long time his mother lay on the floor and did not get up. He minded the man who had lost his van. He minded Dudley's cough, which was back, and his aunt up in the night with it, and his aunt herself, with her tin and her twelve years. He had not decided any of this. He had looked, as he looked at everything, long enough and from near enough, and found it there, and it did not go away under looking, and so it was his.

If he minded, then what he wanted was for things to be otherwise: for the boy not to go into the canal, for the man to keep his van. How any of that was done he did not know. He knew that there was a town outside this house with three plumbers and a library in it, and a country beyond the town with a number on the television, and that nobody in either had what he had, which was time. Every one of them went through their days at the pace of his aunt crossing the carpet, while he sat inside each of their seconds with room to spare. So far he had spent it on reading.

He thought about the people who wrote in green ink, too. There was a school that had had a place for his mother. There was a man who could put out the lights of a street with his hand, and Mrs Figg writing to him on Tuesdays, and a whole lot of them somewhere. If anything could be done about canals and chip pans and the number with the three at the front, he thought it was at least as likely to be found among them as among the plumbers.

That was as far as he got that summer, except for one small thing.

The canal the boy had drowned in ran behind the recreation ground, and the paper had said there was no fence along the towpath on that side. On a Tuesday he asked Mrs Figg who was in charge of fences. The council, she supposed, and why did he want to know. He told her. She looked at him for a while, then fetched a writing pad from the sideboard drawer. He said what the letter should say and she wrote it, since his hand would not yet do for a council, and she signed it, since a council would not read a letter from somebody who was three. It said that a child had drowned, and where, and that there was no fence there, and asked when there would be one. It went in the post on the Wednesday. Three weeks later a man with a long title wrote back to say that the matter was under consideration. In October a fence went up along the towpath behind the recreation ground. It might have had nothing to do with the letter. Harry read about it in the Thursday paper.

The second letter was easier, because Mrs Figg had the pad out already. It went to the Thursday paper itself, about the chip pan, and said that a fire guard cost four pounds at the ironmonger's on the High Street and that the paper might say so. The paper printed it, over Mrs Figg's name, under a heading she did not care for. The ironmonger wrote to thank her.

---

In July he put a biscuit wrapper in Mrs Figg's kitchen bin and found a newspaper in it.

It was neither the local paper nor his uncle's. The sheets were larger and thicker and yellowish, and the photograph on the front moved. A man in a tall hat was waving; his hand came up, went down, came up again, very slowly, over and over. Harry stood at the bin with the lid in one hand and watched the hand go up and down four times before he lifted the paper out. The name across the top was the Daily Prophet. The date was the week before. Mrs Figg was upstairs. He took the paper into the front room, sat on the hearthrug, and had read every word before she came down, by which time it was folded back in the bin under the wrapper and he was reading the Pears.

There was a Ministry of Magic, with a Minister who was a woman. There was a prison called Azkaban, mentioned in a piece about a man who had been sent there. There was a shop in London that sold cauldrons, and a page of advertisements for things whose use he could not guess. There was a game played in the air, with scores. And on the fourth page, in a piece about the governors of a school, there was the school's name, which he had from the letter in the tin, and beneath it the name of its Headmaster, who was Albus Dumbledore.

He did not ask on the next Tuesday, or the one after. He wanted to see whether she would say anything herself, now that he had read her paper, and she did not. So on the third Tuesday in August, with his cup of milk on the hearth and Mr Tibbles an arm's length away and Mrs Figg settling into her chair with her tea, he asked.

"Do you know Albus Dumbledore?"

Mrs Figg set her cup down on the hearth without drinking from it. Mr Tibbles left the rug and went to sit on the arm of her chair. She looked at Harry for a long time by her own measure, and he waited, as he was good at waiting, and watched her face do several things and settle.

"Yes," she said. "I'll have to write to him, won't I."
