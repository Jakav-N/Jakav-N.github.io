# Chapter Three

*January to August 1983*

The first time he went out of the cupboard at night was in January, and he went because the encyclopaedia had come.

A man had brought it to the door in the first week of the year, a man in a raincoat with a case, and his uncle had let him in, which his uncle did not do with men at the door. Harry had watched from the pen. The man opened his case on the dining table and took out a book with a red cover and gold on the spine and opened it at a picture of a volcano, and talked for most of an hour, and his uncle looked at Dudley, who was under the table, and signed something. Two weeks later a box came, and twenty red books went onto the bottom shelf in the front room in a row, still in their plastic, and his aunt dusted them on Fridays with the other things on the shelf, and nobody opened one.

He slid the bolt on a Tuesday night when the clock in the hall had done one. He had been listening to the house for an hour by then and knew where everyone was by their breathing, his uncle's through the floor like a saw, his aunt's lighter and further off, Dudley's with the catch in it that it had had since Christmas. He saw the bolt drawing back and heard it draw, and pushed the door, and it opened onto the dark hall, and he went out on his hands and knees because that was quieter than feet.

The front room by his light was a different room. The light was the size of an egg still, and it showed him a circle the size of a dinner plate and nothing else, so that the room came to him one plate at a time: the arm of his uncle's chair, the leg of the table, the glass front of the cabinet with his aunt's china behind it, the shelf. He got the plastic off the first volume with his teeth, in the end, and sat on the carpet with his back against the shelf and the book open on his knees and his light over the page, and read.

The book was slower than he was. That was the difficulty. His eyes went along a line and down to the next line at the pace eyes went at, and there was no hurrying them, and a page took as long as a page took, and he sat inside each one with time to spare and nothing to spend it on but the page. He read the first volume that night from A to the middle of B. He put the plastic back round it, pressed flat, and put it in its place in the row, and went back along the hall and into the cupboard and drew the door to and saw the bolt sliding home, and heard it, and lay down. His aunt dusted the shelf on Friday and did not look at the plastic.

He had the twenty volumes by March. He went out five nights in seven and read until the first bird. He learned where the floorboards spoke and went round them, and that his uncle got up once in the night about half past two, for which the cupboard door had to be bolted and his light out. The encyclopaedia had been written for children, and it said so, and it still had in it the Romans and the planets and how a steam engine worked and the names of the bones, and he took all of it, because he did not know yet what he would want, and it went in and stayed.

---

After the encyclopaedia there was the rest of the shelf, and after the shelf there was the house.

The dictionary he read straight through, which took a fortnight and settled a great many arguments the subtitles had started. The atlas he read as a book, country by country, and then as a picture, the whole of it, until he could have drawn the coasts. There were six fat books with Reader's Digest on them that turned out each to be four books cut short, and these were the first stories he had read, and he read them differently from anything before. The encyclopaedia told him what things were. The stories told him what people were like inside, which he had only ever had from outside, from faces and the lowered voice, and he found that the two matched up more often than not. There was a woman in one of the stories who kept her house very clean and loved her son past all sense and could not say a kind word to anyone else, and he read her twice.

The kitchen had his aunt's cookery books and the back of every packet. The hall had the telephone directory, and under it a thicker yellow one that listed every business in the town by what it did, and he read that too, and learned that Little Whinging had three plumbers, two solicitors, a dentist, a man who repaired televisions, and a public library on the High Street, open Tuesday to Saturday, which he could do nothing about. His uncle's chair had the Highway Code down the side of it and a book about the car. The front room had the television, which at night could be made to show the pages of writing with the sound turned to nothing, and after the shelf was done he spent an hour most nights on those, with the door shut and his back to the stairs. The pages changed by themselves every half minute, which gave him a long while with each. He read the news on them, and the weather for places he had found in the atlas, and the prices of shares, which went up and down for reasons the pages did not give, and he began to want to know the reasons.

By spring there was nothing left in the house with print on it that he had not read, except whatever was in his aunt's bureau.

---

The bureau stood in the corner of the front room by the window, and it had a flap that came down to write on and three drawers under it, and the drawers had keys, and the keys were in them. His aunt did her letters at it on Sunday afternoons. He had seen her take things out of the bottom drawer and put them back. He had not opened it before because there had been the shelf to read, and because it was hers in a way the shelf was not, and he knew what the flat hand went with.

He opened it in April. The bottom drawer had a biscuit tin in it with a picture of a Scottish castle on the lid, and in the tin were papers: his aunt and uncle's marriage lines, Dudley's birth certificate, some photographs of his aunt as a girl with another girl who had red hair and was laughing, and two letters in envelopes that had been opened and closed again many times, so that the flaps were soft. The envelopes were addressed in the same hand, a slanting, looping hand in green ink. The first said Miss Petunia Evans. The second said Mrs Petunia Dursley.

He read the second first, because it was the one from the step. He knew it by the envelope. It was two sheets, written close, and it began by saying that the writer was very sorry to be the bearer of the news it contained, and then it contained the news.

Lily and James Potter were dead. They had been killed on the night of the thirty-first of October at their house in a place called Godric's Hollow, by a man the letter called Lord Voldemort, and the letter said that Petunia would know the name or at least the fear of it. Their son had lived. The man who had killed them had tried to kill the boy too and had failed and was gone, though the writer did not think him dead. The letter said that in dying for her son Lily had left a protection on him, and that the protection would hold while he lived under the roof of her blood, which was Petunia's roof, and that the writer asked her to take him in and raise him, knowing it was a great deal to ask. It said what might come looking for the boy if she did not. It said the boy's name was Harry James Potter and that he had been one year old on the thirty-first of July. It was signed Albus Dumbledore.

He sat on the carpet with the letter in his hands and his light over it and read it again, and then he read the other one.

It was older, and shorter, and it was addressed to a girl. The writer thanked her for her letter and said that it had been written with great feeling and that he was sorry to have to answer it as he must. He said that there was no place for her at the school, that the thing that made a place was not given or withheld by anyone but simply was or was not, and that he was sorry for it. He said that she had a sister who loved her very much and that he hoped that the two of them would not let this come between them. He wished her well in a hand that had not changed in ten years. It was signed as the other was.

He put the letters back in their envelopes, and the envelopes back in the tin under the photographs, and the tin back in the drawer, and the drawer shut, and sat where he was for a while with his light out.

He had a name, then, and a day, and he was two years and nine months old. The her was Lily, and the red-haired girl laughing in the photographs was Lily, and the man in the doorway with the red eyes had a name, and the name had made his aunt afraid before she ever saw him on her step. The car crash was a thing she had told Marge. And his aunt, who dusted the encyclopaedia and did not open it, had once been a girl who wrote to a school to ask to be let in, and had kept the answer for twelve years in a tin, under the photographs of the sister who had got in instead.

He had thought about his aunt a great deal, there being a great deal of time and not much else. He had thought about her in the way he had come to think about the people in the condensed novels, from what she did, and what she did had been feed him and keep him and not look at him, and love Dudley with the whole of herself in a voice she kept for nobody else. He had the inside of it now, or some of it, and it changed what he saw when she came across the room to him in the mornings at her wading pace with her mouth set. It did not change what she did. He had not expected it to. But he looked at her differently after April, and in the long slow seconds while she crossed the carpet he found that what he felt about her, more than anything, was sorry.

---

The magic went on in the cupboard, at night, before he went out and after he came back, and it did not go far.

The bolt he could do without looking at the door now. The light was steadier and a little bigger, the size of an orange, and it did not waver when he turned his head. He could pull a book across the carpet to him from the shelf if it was a thin one and he saw the whole of its path, and he could turn a page without touching it, which was no use, since his hands were there anyway. In January, when the cupboard was so cold that his breath showed in his light, he had tried for warmth, seeing his hands as they were when he held them to the gas fire at Mrs Figg's, and after a week of nights he had got something, a warmth in the palms that lasted as long as the picture did and left him tired after. He used it on the cold nights. He did not use it for long.

That was all. Small, light things moved, and nothing else did. The light came and the warmth came and nothing else he tried came at all, and he tried a good many things, carefully, one at a time, with a night between each so that if anything went wrong it would be a small wrong and his own. Nothing went wrong. Nothing much went right either. He did not know why. The same things worked each night and the same things failed, so there was a reason, and he did not have it, and nobody in the house could give it to him.

---

The summer he turned three there was an election, and a man came to the door about it.

He stood on the step in a blue rosette and talked to his uncle for a long time, and his uncle agreed with everything he said and shut the door and said something else about him to his aunt. The news that month was the election and nothing but, men at lecterns and crowds and the long slow sweep of a hand across a map that turned blue as it went, and the number that came up every evening on the pages of writing, which was the number of people without work and had a three at the front of it and six noughts after. Harry knew what a million was by then. He had read it. He looked at the number and at the men at the lecterns and could not make them go together.

The local paper came through the door on Thursdays and his aunt read it for the houses and threw it out, and he read it from the rack in the hall, at night, before the bin. In June there was a boy in it who had gone into the canal after a football and not come out, with a photograph, a school photograph, and under it his age, which was six. There was a woman whose chip pan had caught, and the house with it, and a man who had lost his job at the works and then his van and then, the paper said, his way. Harry read it all. He had read the condensed novels and knew the shape of people from inside, and the paper gave him the outside of them every Thursday, and the two went together as they had before, and he did not like what they made.

He had been thinking for a long time about what he wanted. He had not been able to help it; there was so much time, and the question kept coming round in it like the pages on the television. For a long while the answers had been small and near. The bottle. The light. Out of the cupboard, into the books. Mrs Figg's biscuits, which were a better make than his aunt's. He had all of those now, or as many of them as could be had, and the question kept coming round, and in the summer he turned three it began to come round with the Thursday paper in it, and the number with the three at the front, and the woman with the chip pan, and the boy who was six.

What he found, when he looked at it straight, was that he minded. He minded the boy in the canal, whom he had never seen, in the way he had minded the long time his mother had lain on the floor and not got up. He minded the man who had lost his van. He minded Dudley's cough, which was back, and his aunt up in the night with it, and his aunt herself, with her tin and her twelve years. It was not a thing he had decided. He had looked, as he looked at everything, for long enough and from near enough, and found it there, and it did not go away when he looked at it, and so it was his.

He sat with it, that summer, sitting on the front room carpet at two in the morning with the Thursday paper and his light. If he minded, then what he wanted was for it to be otherwise. For the boy not to go into the canal; for the man to keep his van. He did not know how any of that was done. He knew it was not done from the pen. He knew that there was a town outside this house with three plumbers and a library in it, and a country past that with a number on the television, and that none of them had what he had, which was time. He had so much of it. Every one of them went through their days at the pace of his aunt crossing the carpet, and he sat inside each of their seconds with room to spare, and had done since the night the ceiling came off, and so far he had spent it on reading.

He thought, too, about the people who wrote in green ink. There was a school that had not had a place for his aunt and had had one for his mother. There were people who wrote in green ink and said a name his aunt had been afraid of. There was Mrs Figg, who had been told to keep an eye on him and had written to someone about it. There was a whole lot of them somewhere, with a school and letters and a man who could put out the lights in a street with his hand, and if there was anything that could be done about canals and chip pans and the number with the three at the front, he thought it was at least as likely to be among them as among the plumbers.

That was as far as he got that summer.

---

He had been going to Mrs Figg on Tuesdays since the spring.

She had offered, in the street, in his aunt's hearing, and his aunt had taken her up on it within the week, because Tuesday was her hair and the big shop and Dudley was more than enough in a trolley by himself. So on Tuesday mornings his aunt wheeled him the two streets to Wisteria Walk and left him on the step with his bag, and collected him at four, and in between there were the cats and the gas fire and Mrs Figg, who talked to him as she talked to the cats and did not mind him reading.

She had books. Not many, and most of them about cats, but there were a row of green-backed detective stories on the landing and a fat blue book called Pears Cyclopaedia on the shelf by the fire that had in it a great many things the red encyclopaedia had not, and she let him have whatever he reached for, and asked him afterwards what he had made of it. Nobody had asked him that before. He found that he had a good deal to say, and that saying it was still slow, and that she waited.

She did not say anything to him about Dumbledore. She did not say anything about his aunt either, or his mother, or the letter, or why she had been told to keep an eye on him, and he did not ask, because he could see her not saying it. She watched him, over her cup, with Mr Tibbles on the arm of her chair watching too, and some Tuesdays she wrote at the kitchen table in the afternoon with her back to the door and put the letter in her bag before he could see where it was going. He let her be.

In July he put a biscuit wrapper in her kitchen bin and found a newspaper in it.

It was not the local paper or his uncle's. It was larger, and the paper was thick and yellowish, and the photograph on the front moved. A man in a tall hat was waving, and his hand came up and went down and came up again, very slowly, over and over, and Harry stood at the bin with the lid in one hand and watched the hand go up and down four times before he took the paper out. The name across the top was the Daily Prophet. The date was the week before. He took it into the front room and sat on the hearthrug with it, and Mrs Figg was upstairs, and he read every word before she came down, and when she came down he had folded it and put it back in the bin under the wrapper, and he was reading the Pears.

There was a Ministry of Magic, and it had a Minister, a woman. There was a prison called Azkaban, in a piece about a man who had been sent there. There was a shop in London that sold cauldrons and a page of advertisements for things he did not know the use of. There was a game played in the air and the scores of it. And there was, on the fourth page, in a piece about the governors of a school, the name of the school, which he had from the letter in the tin, and under it the name of its Headmaster, who was Albus Dumbledore.

He went to Mrs Figg on the next Tuesday, and the next, and did not say anything. He wanted to be sure of what he was asking before he asked it, and he wanted to be sure that asking was better than not. He had the letter, and the hand that wrote in green, and the name on the fourth page, and the name said to a cat with the tap running, and they were one name, and the man it belonged to had put him on the step and had Mrs Figg watching him and was Headmaster of the school his aunt had not been let into. He turned it over for a fortnight of Tuesdays. Then, on the third Tuesday in August, with his cup of milk on the hearth and Mr Tibbles an arm's length off and Mrs Figg settling into her chair with her tea, he asked her.

"Do you know Albus Dumbledore?"

Mrs Figg put her cup down on the hearth without drinking from it. Mr Tibbles got up off the rug and went to sit on the arm of her chair. She looked at Harry for a long time, by her measure, and he waited, as he was good at waiting, and watched her face do several things and settle.

"Yes," she said. "I'll have to write to him, won't I."
