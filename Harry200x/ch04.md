# Chapter Four

*September to October 1983*

Dumbledore came on the second Tuesday in September, at eleven in the morning, by the back door.

Harry heard him before he saw him. There was a sound in the garden like a car door shutting a street away, stretched as all sounds were, so that he had the whole length of it to wonder at, and then feet on the path, and Mrs Figg getting out of her chair faster than he had seen her get out of anything, and the back door, and a voice in the kitchen that he knew.

He had heard it once, on a pavement, in the dark, two years before, talking for a long while to a woman who was about to be a cat. He had not had a word of what it said then. He had all of them now, and they came through the kitchen wall long and slow and clear, saying that it was very good of Arabella to have him, and that the cats looked well, and that he would not say no to a cup of tea. Then the kitchen door opened and the man from the step came into the front room.

He was tall and old, with the beard and the half-moon glasses, and a suit of some dark red stuff, and he stopped in the doorway and looked at Harry on the hearthrug, and Harry looked back at him, as he had on the pavement, and did not cry.

"You put the lights out," Harry said.

The old man did not say anything for a moment. Then he came and sat down in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's, slowly, and put his hands on his knees. Mr Tibbles went and sat on the arm of the chair and looked up at him, and the old man looked at the cat, and then at Harry.

"I did," he said. "And on again afterwards. Do you remember that as well?"

"Yes. And the woman who turned into a cat. And the big man who cried."

"You were fifteen months old."

"Yes."

"You were asleep, I had thought."

"You made me go to sleep. After the lights."

The old man took that in. Mrs Figg came through with the tea on a tray and a plate of the better biscuits, and put it on the low table between the chairs, and sat down in her own chair with Snowy, and did not say anything, and the old man took a cup and held it without drinking.

"My name is Albus Dumbledore."

"I know. You wrote the letters."

"Which letters are those?"

"The ones in the tin in my aunt's bureau. The one to her when she was a girl and the one she read on the step. I put them back."

"I see." Dumbledore drank some of his tea. "Did you read anything else of your aunt's?"

"There wasn't anything else. I'd read the rest of the house."

"Her letters were hers, Harry."

"Yes."

"And you read them."

"Yes. I wanted to know who I was. There wasn't anybody to ask."

Dumbledore looked at him over the top of the cup for a while. "No," he said. "There was not. I had seen to that." He set the cup down. "Tell me how you learned to read."

Harry told him. It took a long time, in his voice, which had got better at the longer words over the summer but was still a small voice and went at the pace small voices went at, and Dumbledore did not hurry him or finish anything for him. The Ladybird books, read to Dudley with his aunt's finger under the words. The new television, and the page with the writing on it that his aunt left on so she could follow her programme over the noise. The newspaper on the arm of the chair. The catalogue. The encyclopaedia at night, by his light.

"By your light."

"I'll show you. After."

"When was this? The subtitles."

"The summer before last. June."

"You were two."

"Nearly."

"And you had the words already. The spoken words."

"Yes. From listening. I had a long time to listen."

"How long?"

He had known the question would come, and there was no good answer to it, because any answer was a guess.

"Everything is slow," he said. "You are. Mrs Figg is. When somebody says a word it takes a long time to finish, and I have the whole time to think about it, and then I have the time until the next word. When I was in the pen there was nothing to do but listen. So I listened, and I had a long time to do it in."

"How slow?"

"I don't know. I can't count it. There's nothing to count against. Everything I could count against is slow as well." He had the one thing, and he gave it. "The television draws its picture fifty times every second. The encyclopaedia says so. I can watch the line go down the screen. It goes down the way you would watch somebody walk across a room."

Dumbledore said nothing for a while. Then he did the sum, and did not say the number.

"Some hundreds of times, then."

"I think so."

Dumbledore looked at him. He looked over the glasses rather than through them, straight into Harry's eyes, and his own were very blue and very steady, and Harry looked back because he had always looked back, and felt something.

It was like a page being turned, inside, where there were no pages, and then another, faster, as if someone were leafing through a book they could not read to find the picture.

"What are you doing?"

Dumbledore stopped at once. He looked away, at the fire, and when he looked back it was through the glasses.

"There is a way of seeing what another person is thinking," he said. "If one looks them in the eye and knows how. It is not a kind thing to do without asking, and I did it without asking. I am sorry."

"What did you see?"

"A great deal, going past very fast. I could not keep hold of any of it." He turned his cup on its saucer. "I will not do it again unless you tell me I may."

"All right."

Mrs Figg, in her chair, said, "Albus," in a voice Harry had not heard her use, and Dumbledore said, "Yes, Arabella. I know," and that was all that passed between them about it.

---

Harry had his questions in order. He had had them in order for a fortnight, since the day Mrs Figg had put her cup down on the hearth, and he had put the one he most wanted answered first, because speaking was slow and he did not know how long he would be given.

"Is Voldemort dead?"

"No. I don't believe so." Dumbledore did not look away from him. "I don't know where he is, or what he is, now. I believe that he will try to come back, and that it may take him a long time."

"Does he know where I am?"

"No. That is why you are where you are."

"The letter said that. About my mother's blood." Harry had the words from the letter and used them. "That the protection holds while I live under the roof of her blood."

"Yes. Your mother died rather than let him have you. That left something in you, and the something draws its strength from her blood, and her blood lives in your aunt. While you make your home where your aunt makes hers, he cannot touch you there, nor anyone who serves him. It's old magic. Nobody understands it well, and I know of nothing stronger."

"How much do I have to live there?"

Dumbledore's eyebrows went up a little.

"I've read my aunt's house," Harry said. "There's nothing left in it. Mrs Figg brings me six from the library every week and they last a day. I would rather be where the books are."

"It depends on you thinking of the house as home and going back to it, not on counting days. I would not like to find out how little would serve. Several weeks in every year at the least, I should think, and a good deal more than that while you are small, if it can be managed."

"I don't want to, much."

"No. I don't suppose you do." Dumbledore looked at the fire for a moment. "Where do you sleep, Harry?"

"In the cupboard under the stairs. There's a bolt on the outside. I can open it."

Nothing on Dumbledore's face moved for a time, and then he looked at Mrs Figg, and she looked back at him and gave a very small nod, and he looked at the fire again.

"I will speak to your aunt."

"She'll know I told you."

"She will know that I know. I will see that nothing comes back on you for it." He turned back to Harry. "I did not put you there to be bolted in."

"I don't mind the cupboard. I mind having nothing to read."

"Then you shall have something to read."

---

He asked for the wand next, because it was the next thing on the list, and because the books, from what he had seen of the catalogue page in the Prophet in Mrs Figg's bin, were going to want one.

"What would you do with a wand?"

"The things in the books."

"A wand in untaught hands is a dangerous object. Children are not given them until eleven, and that is not an arbitrary age." Dumbledore considered him. "Though I grant you that most of the reasons do not apply."

"I can do some things without one."

"Show me."

Harry looked at the sugar bowl on the tray. He saw it rising, and crossing, and coming to rest on the arm of Dumbledore's chair beside Mr Tibbles, and it rose, and crossed, at the walking pace that was the only pace it had, and Mr Tibbles moved up to make room for it and it settled. Then he held out his hand palm up and saw the light, and the light came, the size of an orange, steady, and sat over his palm, and he held it there and watched Dumbledore watch it.

Dumbledore watched it for a long time. He did not reach for his own wand. He did not say anything until Harry let the light go.

"How long have you been able to do that?"

"The light since last autumn. Moving things since the February before that. The first time was by accident, a bottle, when I was hungry and nobody came. After that I tried it on purpose, and it worked."

"On purpose."

"Yes."

"Every time?"

"Small things, every time, if I see the whole of it first. Big things never. I don't know why."

"Nor do I. I have an idea about it, but I'm not sure enough of it to tell you yet." Dumbledore took the sugar bowl off the arm of his chair and put it back on the tray, with his hand, as though returning something borrowed. "Most children do something of the kind once or twice before they are eleven. When they are frightened, or very angry. Afterwards they cannot do it again when they try, and they stop trying. You do it when you try. I have never heard of that in a child your age, and I need to think about it."

"Can I have a wand?"

"Yes. Not today. Before Christmas, I think, and with someone beside you when you use it until I am satisfied that you need nobody beside you. I will think about who." He reached into the pocket of the red suit and brought out a paper bag and held it out. "Sherbet lemon?"

Harry took one. It was the first sweet he had ever had that was not a biscuit, and it was very sour and then very sweet, and he kept it in his cheek for the rest of the conversation.

"There is a thing I must ask of you in return," Dumbledore said. "What you told me about the television, and how fast you think. I'd like that kept between the three of us for now."

"Why?"

"Because people are frightened by what they can't do themselves, and you'll have enough of that as it is. And because there are people who would want to make use of you if they knew. You'll be clever in front of people whatever we do, and I don't want you to hide it. I'm asking that they don't know the reason."

"I won't tell people. If somebody asks me straight out, I won't say it isn't true."

"No. I would not ask you to say that."

---

They talked for another hour. Mrs Figg made a second pot. Harry asked about his mother and father, and Dumbledore told him some things, plainly, the way he would have told them to a grown man: that James had been reckless and loyal and very good on a broomstick, and Lily had been kind and quick and did not suffer fools, and that they had been twenty-one. He said there was a great deal more, and that he would tell it over several visits rather than all at once. Harry asked who the big man was. Dumbledore said his name was Hagrid, and that he would very much like to see Harry again one day, and that he would cry when he did.

Then Harry asked the question he had kept back, because it was not on the list and he had not been sure he wanted the answer.

"There was another man. That night. Before the big man took me away on the motorbike. He had black hair all over the place. He took me from the big man and held me."

Dumbledore's cup stopped on its way to his mouth.

"What did he do?"

"He cried. Not out loud. He held me very hard against his chest, and his heart was going fast." Harry had heard a good many hearts since, his own and Dudley's and Mrs Figg's when she lifted him, and knew what their ordinary pace was. "Then he gave me back and said a lot to the big man, and they went out."

Dumbledore put the cup down.

"His name is Sirius Black," he said. "He was your father's closest friend from the age of eleven. He is in prison, in Azkaban, which you have read about in Arabella's newspaper."

"Why?"

"Because it was he who told Voldemort where to find you."

"He was crying," Harry said.

"Yes. I expect he was."

Harry did not know what to make of that, and did not try to then.

"All right," he said.

---

Dumbledore left at two, by the back door, as he had come. Before he went he took from inside the red suit, which did not look as though it had room for them, four books, and put them on the low table one at a time. A History of Magic. Magical Theory. The Standard Book of Spells, Grade One. Hogwarts: A History. They were thick, and old, and smelled of a different house.

"There are others. I will bring them a few at a time, because I want to hear what you make of these first, and because Arabella's shelf is not large." He looked at Mrs Figg. "I will speak to Petunia Dursley this week. I would like him here more than Tuesdays, Arabella, if you will have him."

"Whenever she'll let him go," said Mrs Figg. "She'll let him go."

"I expect she will." He looked down at Harry. "A fortnight. Read those and think about what you want to ask me. If there is something I won't tell you, I'll say so rather than make something up."

He went out through the kitchen. Harry heard the back door, and his feet on the path, and then the sound from the garden again, the car door a street away, drawn out long enough for him to hear that it had a crack in the middle of it, and then nothing.

Mrs Figg sat down. She had got up to see him out and she sat down again in her chair and Snowy got back onto her knees, and she looked at the four books on the table, and then at Harry.

"Well," she said.

Harry picked up the top one. It was the History, and it was heavy, and he got it onto his knees on the hearthrug with both hands and opened it, and the first page said that magic had been practised in the British Isles for as long as there had been anyone in them to practise it, and he read on.
