# Chapter Four

*September to October 1983*

Dumbledore came on the second Tuesday in September, at eleven in the morning, by the back door.

Harry heard him before he saw him. Out in the garden there was a sound like a car door shutting a street away, drawn out as every sound was for him, so that he had the whole length of it to wonder about. Feet came up the path. Mrs Figg got out of her chair faster than he had ever seen her move. The back door opened, and a voice in the kitchen said that it was very good of Arabella to have him, that the cats looked well, and that he would not say no to a cup of tea.

Harry knew the voice. He had heard it once before, two years ago, on a pavement in the dark, talking at length to a woman who was about to become a cat. He had not understood a word of it then. He understood every word now as it came through the kitchen wall, long and slow and clear. Then the kitchen door opened and the man from the step walked into the front room.

He was tall and old, with the silver beard and the half-moon glasses, in a suit of some dark red stuff. He stopped in the doorway to look at Harry on the hearthrug. Harry looked back, as he had on the pavement, and did not cry.

"You put the lights out," Harry said.

For a moment the old man said nothing. Then he crossed to the chair that was not Mrs Figg's and lowered himself into it with his hands on his knees. Mr Tibbles jumped onto the arm and looked up at him. The old man considered the cat, then Harry.

"I did. And on again afterwards. Do you remember that as well?"

"Yes. And the woman who turned into a cat. And the big man who cried."

"You were fifteen months old."

"Yes."

"I had thought you were asleep."

"You made me go to sleep. After the lights."

The old man took that in. Mrs Figg came through with the tea on a tray and a plate of the better biscuits, set it on the low table between the chairs, and sat down in her own chair with Snowy. She said nothing. The old man picked up a cup and held it without drinking.

"My name is Albus Dumbledore."

"I know. You wrote the letters."

"Which letters?"

"The ones in the tin in my aunt's bureau. The one to her when she was a girl, and the one she read on the step. I put them back."

"I see." Dumbledore drank some tea. "Did you read anything else of your aunt's?"

"There wasn't anything else. I'd read the rest of the house."

"Her letters were hers, Harry."

"Yes."

"And you read them."

"Yes. I wanted to know who I was, and there wasn't anybody to ask."

Dumbledore looked at him over the cup for a while. "No," he said. "There wasn't. I had seen to that." He put the cup down. "Tell me how you learned to read."

Harry told him. It took a long time in his voice, which had improved over the summer on the longer words but was still a small voice going at a small voice's pace, and Dumbledore neither hurried him nor finished anything for him. The Ladybird books, read to Dudley with his aunt's finger under the words. The new television, and the page of writing his aunt left on so that she could follow her programme over the noise. The newspaper on the arm of the chair. The catalogue. The encyclopaedia at night, by his light.

"By your light."

"I'll show you. After."

"When was this? The subtitles."

"The summer before last. June."

"You were two."

"Nearly."

"And you already had the spoken words."

"Yes. From listening. I had a long time to listen."

"How long?"

He had known the question would come, and there was no good answer, since any answer was a guess.

"Everything is slow," he said. "You are. Mrs Figg is. When somebody says a word it takes a long time to finish, and I have the whole of that time to think about it, and then the time until the next word. In the pen there was nothing to do except listen. So I listened, and I had a long time to do it in."

"How slow?"

"I don't know. I can't count it; there's nothing to count against. Everything I could count against is slow as well." There was one thing he could offer, and he offered it. "The television draws its picture fifty times every second. The encyclopaedia says so. I can watch the line go down the screen. It goes down the way you'd watch somebody walk across a room."

Dumbledore was quiet. Then he did the sum, and did not say the number.

"Some hundreds of times, then."

"I think so."

Dumbledore looked at him over the glasses rather than through them, straight into his eyes. His own were very blue and very steady. Harry looked back, as he always had, and felt something.

It was like a page being turned inside him, where there were no pages, and then another, faster, as though someone were leafing through a book they could not read in search of the picture.

"What are you doing?"

Dumbledore stopped at once. He looked away, into the fire, and when he looked back it was through the glasses.

"There's a way of seeing what another person is thinking, if you look them in the eye and know how. I shouldn't have done it without asking. I'm sorry."

"What did you see?"

"A great deal, going past very fast. I couldn't keep hold of any of it." He turned the cup on its saucer. "I won't do it again unless you tell me I may."

"All right."

From her chair Mrs Figg said, "Albus," in a voice Harry had not heard her use. Dumbledore said, "Yes, Arabella. I know." That was all that passed between them about it.

---

Harry had his questions in order. He had put them in order a fortnight ago, the day Mrs Figg set her cup down on the hearth, with the one he most wanted answered first, because speaking was slow and he did not know how long he would be given.

"Is Voldemort dead?"

"No. I don't believe so." Dumbledore held his eye. "I don't know where he is, or what he is, now. I believe he'll try to come back, and that it may take him a long time."

"Does he know where I am?"

"No. That's why you are where you are."

"The letter said that. About my mother's blood." Harry used the letter's words. "That the protection holds while I live under the roof of her blood."

"Yes. Your mother died rather than let him have you. That left something in you, and the something draws its strength from her blood, and her blood lives in your aunt. While you make your home where your aunt makes hers, he can't touch you there, nor can anyone who serves him. It's old magic. Nobody understands it well, and I know of nothing stronger."

"How much do I have to live there?"

Dumbledore's eyebrows went up a little.

"I've read my aunt's house," Harry said. "There's nothing left in it. Mrs Figg brings me six books from the library every week and they last a day. I'd rather be where the books are."

"It depends on your thinking of the house as home and going back to it, not on counting days. I'd rather not find out how little would serve. Several weeks in every year at the least, I should think, and a good deal more than that while you're small, if it can be managed."

"I don't want to, much."

"No. I don't suppose you do." Dumbledore watched the fire for a moment. "Where do you sleep, Harry?"

"In the cupboard under the stairs. There's a bolt on the outside. I can open it."

For a time nothing on Dumbledore's face moved. Then he looked at Mrs Figg, who gave him a very small nod, and he looked back at the fire.

"I'll speak to your aunt."

"She'll know I told you."

"She'll know that I know. I'll see that nothing comes back on you for it." He turned to Harry. "I didn't put you there to be bolted in."

"I don't mind the cupboard. I mind having nothing to read."

"Then you shall have something to read."

---

He asked for the wand next. It was the next thing on his list, and from what he had seen of the advertisements in Mrs Figg's Prophet, the books were going to want one.

"What would you do with a wand?"

"The things in the books."

"A wand in untaught hands is a dangerous object. Children aren't given them until eleven, and there are reasons for that." Dumbledore considered him. "Though I grant you most of the reasons don't apply."

"I can do some things without one."

"Show me."

Harry looked at the sugar bowl on the tray. He saw it rise, cross the room, and settle on the arm of Dumbledore's chair beside Mr Tibbles; and it rose, and crossed, at the walking pace that was the only pace it had, and Mr Tibbles shifted to make room, and it settled. Then Harry held out his hand palm up and saw the light. The light came, the size of an orange, steady above his palm, and he held it there and watched Dumbledore watch it.

Dumbledore watched for a long time. He did not reach for his own wand. He said nothing until Harry let the light go.

"How long have you been able to do that?"

"The light since last autumn. Moving things since the February before that. The first time was an accident, a bottle, when I was hungry and nobody came. After that I tried it on purpose, and it worked."

"On purpose."

"Yes."

"Every time?"

"Small things, every time, if I see the whole of it first. Big things never. I don't know why."

"Nor do I. I have an idea about it, but I'm not sure enough of it to tell you yet." Dumbledore lifted the sugar bowl off the arm of his chair with his hand and set it back on the tray. "Most children do something of the kind once or twice before they're eleven, when they're frightened or very angry. Afterwards they can't do it again when they try, and they stop trying. You do it when you try. I've never heard of that in a child your age, and I need to think about it."

"Can I have a wand?"

"Yes. Not today. Before Christmas, I think, with someone beside you when you use it, until I'm satisfied you need nobody beside you. I'll think about who." He paused. "One more thing, since you'll read it somewhere and wonder. The Ministry keeps a watch on magic done by children, so that it can scold them for it. The watch is put on a child when the school's letter goes out, at eleven. Until then nobody is watching you but Arabella and me. I'd like it to stay that way, which is one more reason to do nothing in your aunt's house." He reached into the red suit, brought out a paper bag and held it out. "Sherbet lemon?"

Harry took one. It was the first sweet he had ever had that was not a biscuit; it was very sour and then very sweet, and he kept it in his cheek for the rest of the conversation.

"There's a thing I must ask of you in return," Dumbledore said. "What you told me about the television, and how fast you think. I'd like that kept between the three of us for now."

"Why?"

"Because people are frightened by what they can't do themselves, and you'll have enough of that as it is. And because there are people who'd want to make use of you if they knew. You'll be clever in front of people whatever we do, and I don't want you to hide it. I'm asking that they don't know the reason."

"I won't tell people. If somebody asks me straight out, I won't say it isn't true."

"No. I wouldn't ask you to say that."

There were four more things on the list, and he gave them in order.

Were there other children like him, who could do what he could do, and could he meet them? There were, Dumbledore said, a good many, and no, not yet; the reason Harry was where he was depended on very few people knowing it, and children told their parents. He would say when.

Was there a way to make his eyes go faster? He explained about the eyes: that the reading was not the slow part, the looking was. Dumbledore said that he knew of charms for people who saw badly and none for people who saw well and wanted to see quicker, and that he would ask Professor Flitwick, who knew more charms than anyone alive.

Was there a way to need less sleep? No, Dumbledore said, not one that he would let a child near, and he had seen what the others did to grown men.

And the gold in the vault, which Harry had read about in the Prophet's advertisements for Gringotts: did it do anything, sitting there? Dumbledore said that it did not, and that this was a conversation for when Harry had seen it.

---

They talked for another hour, and Mrs Figg made a second pot. Harry asked about his mother and father. Dumbledore told him some things plainly, as he might have told a grown man: that James had been reckless and loyal and very good on a broomstick; that Lily had been kind and quick and did not suffer fools; that they had both been twenty-one. There was a great deal more, he said, and he would tell it over several visits rather than all at once. Harry asked who the big man was. His name was Hagrid, Dumbledore said. He would very much like to see Harry again one day, and he would cry when he did.

Then Harry asked the question he had kept back. It was not on the list, and he had not been sure he wanted the answer.

"There was another man. That night. Before the big man took me away on the motorbike. He had black hair all over the place. He took me from the big man and held me."

Dumbledore's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"What did he do?"

"He cried. Not out loud. He held me very hard against his chest, and his heart was going fast." Harry had heard a good many hearts since, his own and Dudley's and Mrs Figg's when she lifted him, and knew their ordinary pace. "Then he gave me back and said a lot to the big man, and they went out."

Dumbledore set the cup down.

"His name is Sirius Black. He was your father's closest friend from the age of eleven. He's in prison, in Azkaban, which you've read about in Arabella's newspaper."

"Why?"

"Because it was he who told Voldemort where to find you."

"He was crying," Harry said.

"Yes. I expect he was."

Harry did not know what to make of that, and did not try to then.

"All right," he said.

---

Dumbledore left at two by the back door, as he had come. Before he went he took four books from inside the red suit, which did not look as if it had room for them, and laid them on the low table one at a time. A History of Magic. Magical Theory. The Standard Book of Spells, Grade One. Hogwarts: A History. They were thick and old, and they smelled of a different house.

"There are others. I'll bring them a few at a time, because I want to hear what you make of these first, and because Arabella's shelf isn't large." He turned to Mrs Figg. "I'll speak to Petunia Dursley this week. I'd like him here more than Tuesdays, Arabella, if you'll have him."

"Whenever she'll let him go," said Mrs Figg. "She'll let him go."

"I expect she will." He looked down at Harry. "A fortnight. Read those, and think about what you want to ask me. If there's something I won't tell you, I'll say so rather than make something up."

He went out through the kitchen. Harry heard the back door, then feet on the path, then the sound from the garden again, the car door a street away, long enough this time for him to hear the crack in the middle of it. Then nothing.

Mrs Figg had got up to see him out. She sat down again, and Snowy climbed back onto her knees, and she looked at the four books on the table and then at Harry.

"Well," she said.

Harry picked up the top one. It was the History, and heavy; he got it onto his knees on the hearthrug with both hands and opened it. The first page said that magic had been practised in the British Isles for as long as there had been anyone in them to practise it. He read on.
