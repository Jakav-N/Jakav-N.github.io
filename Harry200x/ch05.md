# Chapter Five

*September 1983 to February 1984*

On the Wednesday morning the bolt was gone from the cupboard door. Nobody said why.

His aunt had had a visitor the day before, while Harry was at Mrs Figg's. When he came home at four the front room smelled of a tobacco that was not his uncle's, and his aunt was at the sink with her back to the kitchen door, washing cups. She did not turn round. Speaking to the window, she told him that he would go to Mrs Figg's every weekday from now on, that he was not to imagine this was for his benefit, and that he was to be in by half past four. That was all she ever said about it. Next morning the bolt was off, the screw-holes filled and painted over. His uncle read the newspaper through breakfast without looking at the door or at Harry. Dudley asked where the bolt had gone, and nobody answered him.

Harry did not ask either. Dumbledore had promised that nothing would come back on him, and nothing had. The house spoke to him a little less than before, which left very little, and he had five days a week at Wisteria Walk with the four books.

---

He had read all four by the end of the first Tuesday.

The History was long, the Theory short, the Standard Book of Spells a list. Hogwarts: A History was the one he kept thinking about afterwards. It had a chapter on the school's library, and the chapter put the library at some eleven thousand volumes.

From the History he learned that there had been witches and wizards in Britain since before the Romans. For the last three hundred years they had hidden themselves from everyone else by law, partly because of the burnings and partly because hiding was easier than the alternative. There had been wars he had never heard of, and a prison he had. Voldemort appeared once, near the end, in a short paragraph which said that he had risen and fallen and did not say how.

The Theory explained that a spell needed a wand, a word and a movement, and that the word and the movement were what made one spell differ from another. It said nothing about bottles, or socks, or light that arrived without any of the three. He read it looking for the sentence that would account for him, and the sentence was not there.

The Standard Book gave him the words. That evening on the hearthrug he borrowed one of Mrs Figg's knitting needles for a wand, said the word for light, and made the movement the diagram showed. His own light came, as it always came, over his palm rather than at the needle's tip. He could not tell whether the word had done anything at all or whether he had simply been picturing light while he said it. He gave the needle back.

Dumbledore came on the second Tuesday, as promised, and sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's and asked what Harry had made of the books.

"I've read them," Harry said. "I read them the Tuesday you brought them. Since then there's only been the library." He had tried to find a way of saying the next part that was not a complaint, and had not found one. "A book takes me about as long as Mrs Figg's ironing. I had six from the library this week. They lasted a day."

Dumbledore looked at Mrs Figg, who nodded; then at the four books on the low table; then, for a while, at nothing in particular.

"I had thought," he said, "to bring them a few at a time, so that we could talk about each. That was a mistake. I was thinking of a child with a book, and a book lasts you about as long as a cup of tea." He drew out his wand, which Harry had never seen him use, and touched the low table with it. A wooden crate stood there now, about the size of the one the encyclopaedia had come in, lid off, full to the brim. "That is the rest of what a student at my school reads in seven years, with some of what the staff read, and a dictionary of the words you'll find in them that are not in your aunt's. The Prophet will come to this house every morning from tomorrow. Arabella has agreed to the owl."

"I've agreed to the owl," said Mrs Figg.

Harry looked at the crate and did the sum. "That's about three weeks."

"Then in three weeks we shall see about more." Dumbledore sat back. "Now. You've read the History. Tell me what it left out."

Harry told him. When he had finished he asked the thing he had been holding since the first Tuesday: whether he could go to the library the other book had described, the one with eleven thousand volumes.

"Not while there are students in the building. In the summer, perhaps. I haven't decided."

---

The Prophet arrived at seven every morning. The owl waited on the kitchen windowsill until Mrs Figg paid it, then left, watched off the premises by Mr Tibbles from the draining board.

Harry read the paper on the kitchen floor before Mrs Figg had finished her tea. It took him twenty minutes. The photographs moved, a few seconds each and then over again from the beginning, and he watched each one through once. The paper taught him that there was a Ministry of Magic with departments and a Minister named Bagnold; that there was a court, a hospital, a bank and a school, and that these were very nearly the whole of the wizarding state. It gave him prices. A Galleon was seventeen Sickles. The sport played in the air was called Quidditch and filled a quarter of every issue. Two years after Voldemort had gone, the paper still did not print his name, and wrote You-Know-Who where it would have been.

In the third week of November the paper turned out to know Harry's name as well.

The piece was small and on an inside page, beneath a photograph of a street he recognised from the History as Godric's Hollow. Two years and some weeks after that night, it said, the whereabouts of the Boy Who Lived remained unknown. The Ministry declined to comment. Albus Dumbledore declined to comment. A woman from Ottery St Catchpole had written in to say that the boy ought to be brought up among his own kind, where people could see he was being properly looked after, and the facing page carried two letters, one agreeing with her at length and one not.

He showed it to Mrs Figg, who read it, said, "Yes, well," and handed it back. He kept it for Dumbledore, who read it standing at the window that Tuesday and set it down.

"How many people know my name?"

"Everyone who reads that paper, and most of the people who don't." Dumbledore sat. "You're the only person who has ever lived through what was done to you that night. Everyone who was afraid of him knows it, and a good many of them think you did it, rather than that it happened to you. I put you where I did partly so that you wouldn't grow up being looked at for it. That wasn't the main reason, but it was one of them, and it still is."

"They want to see I'm all right."

"Some of them do."

"Am I?"

Dumbledore did not answer at once. "Not yet, I think. You are a good deal better off than you were in September. Nobody bolts you in, you have books, and you have Arabella. I want it better still by the spring, and I am working out how."

---

At the end of November Mrs Figg took him to the library in person, because the woman at the desk had asked to meet the boy the lists were for.

The library was a low brick building on the High Street with a ramp to the door and a radiator that knocked. It smelled the way the encyclopaedia had smelled under its plastic. Harry stopped inside the door to look at the shelves running back into the room, and Mrs Figg let him look for a while before she took his hand and walked him to the desk.

The woman there was younger than Mrs Figg and wore her glasses on a chain. She looked from Harry to Mrs Figg and back.

"This is him?"

"This is him."

"He can't be more than three."

"He's three."

The woman took one of his lists from a drawer. It was October's, by which time his writing had improved, and she held it up as if to check it against him. "You wrote this."

"Yes."

"And you read them. All six, every week."

"Yes. By Tuesday night. Then I have the week to think about them."

She set the list down and asked what he had thought of the one on the stars. He told her, standing on the far side of a desk that came up to his eyes. It took a while. She heard him to the end, went into the back, and returned with a form: if Mrs Figg would sign for him, she said, she would make out a ticket in his own name, six books. Mrs Figg signed. From then on he had twelve a week and chose them himself while Mrs Figg sat in the chair by the radiator. The woman at the desk, whose name was Mrs Dennison, stamped them without comment, except that now and then, when he brought up something she had read herself, she told him what she had thought of it before he could tell her.

---

Dumbledore took him to London in the second week of December.

He arrived at Mrs Figg's at nine in an overcoat that nearly reached the ground, with a knitted hat for Harry in one pocket. He pulled the hat down over the scar himself. In the kitchen he took Harry's hand and warned him that the next part would be unpleasant for a moment. Then the kitchen was gone.

Apparition, the books called it, and said it was uncomfortable. Harry was pulled through a tube smaller than he was. It pressed on him from every side and from inside; his eyes were pushed back into his head and his chest had no room for breath. For his body this lasted a moment. For him it lasted as long as such things did, and there was nothing to do inside the tube but be pressed. When it ended he was standing on cobbles in cold air, Dumbledore's hand still round his. He stood where he was for a little, though nothing was wrong with him.

Dumbledore looked down. "I shall find another way next time."

They were in a yard behind a pub, with a dustbin. Dumbledore counted bricks up the wall and tapped one with his wand, and the bricks began to turn. Harry saw each one go: they drew back from their neighbours, swung, and settled, until an arch stood where the wall had been and a street ran away beyond it.

The street was narrow and crooked, and the buildings leaned over it. People walked in it wearing cloaks and pointed hats. There were cauldrons stacked on the pavement outside one shop, owls in cages outside another, and a window full of broomsticks with a crowd of boys pressed against the glass. Harry knew the names of these things from the Prophet. Nobody looked at him. Several people looked at Dumbledore; two called him Headmaster, and a woman asked whether the boy was his grandson. "A young friend," Dumbledore said, and walked on, and Harry went beside him under the brim of the hat, looking at all of it.

Gringotts was a white building, taller than its neighbours and not quite straight, and goblins ran it. The History had told him that much without describing a goblin. They were small, with long fingers and clever faces, and the one who took them down to the vault spoke only to Dumbledore. The cart he put them in ran on rails into the rock, and it went fast enough that Harry, for the first time in two years, had to attend to keep up with the walls.

The vault had his name on the key. Dumbledore showed him the key, let him hold it, and gave it to the goblin, who opened the door. Inside there was gold.

Harry had thought about what a heap of gold would look like, and it looked like this: dull rather than bright, heaped loosely, coins the size of a two-pence piece but thicker, with silver piled to one side and bronze beyond. He stood in the doorway and looked, at the height of the heap and how far back it ran and the size of a single coin. By the time Dumbledore had finished saying that this was a good deal of money, enough for school and some way past, Harry had an estimate.

"How much is a Galleon in pounds?"

"About five, at the goblins' rate."

"Then that's about five houses in Privet Drive."

Dumbledore looked at the heap, then at Harry.

"I had not thought of it in those terms. Yes, about that. It isn't a great fortune, but it's a comfortable one, and it's yours. I hold the key until you come of age. I'll spend from it for you when it seems wise, and I'll tell you when I do."

"Books."

"Books, today. Yes."

They filled a bag. The goblin shut the door.

In the cart on the way up Harry asked whether some of the gold could be turned into pounds and put somewhere it would grow. He had read the book about money from the High Street library, and a year of share prices on the television, and the gold in the vault did nothing but lie there. Dumbledore said that gold had been lying in that vault since Harry's grandfather's day and had come to no harm by it. Harry said that was his point. Dumbledore was quiet for the length of the tunnel. Then he said that he would find out what a wizard's gold could lawfully become and who could hold it for a child, that it would not be much and would not be soon, and that he would like Harry's reasons on paper first. Harry wrote them that week, two pages in pencil, which took him an evening.

---

Ollivander's was a narrow shop with a single wand on a faded cushion in the window. Inside it was dark and smelled of dust, and the man who came out of the back was old in a way that had nothing to do with Dumbledore's age. His eyes were pale and he did not blink often enough.

He knew Dumbledore. He knew Harry too, hat or no hat; he looked at him for a long moment, then at the hat, and said, "Ah." He had wondered, he said, when he would be seeing Mr Potter, and had not expected it quite so soon. Then he described Lily's wand and James's, length and wood and core and temperament, as if he had sold them last week. He measured Harry with a tape that moved by itself, went into the back, and returned with an armful of boxes.

The first wand was in Harry's hand for a second before Ollivander took it back. The second did nothing either. With the third Ollivander said, "Try," and Harry, who did not know what trying meant, waved it; a vase went off the counter and broke on the floor, and that wand went back in its box. Thirty-one more followed. Harry held each one and waved it as he had waved the first, and watched Ollivander's face, which grew more interested rather than less as the pile of rejected boxes rose.

Then one was warm.

It was warm before he moved it, the moment it touched his palm, the way a cup is warm. When he waved it red and gold sparks poured from the tip, lit the shop, hung in the dust, and came down slowly enough for him to watch each one land.

"Well," said Mr Ollivander. "Well, well."

Dumbledore had stood by the door with his hands folded through all of it. He said nothing now.

"Holly. Eleven inches. The core is phoenix feather, which is unusual in itself." Ollivander took the wand back gently and began wrapping it in brown paper without taking his eyes off Harry. "The phoenix that gave this feather gave one other, and I remember both. The other went into a wand I sold a long time ago to a young man who did a great many things with it. The last I know of is the mark under your hat. I find it curious that this wand should have chosen you. I don't say it means anything."

Harry took the parcel. He looked up at Dumbledore. Dumbledore's face had not changed, and Harry kept that as well.

"Seven Galleons," said Mr Ollivander.

---

In Flourish and Blotts the books went to the ceiling, up a staircase, and across the floor in piles, and all of them were about magic. Harry stopped inside the door as he had at the library. Dumbledore put a hand on his shoulder and said that they had some time but not all day, and that he had better begin.

He went along the shelves reading spines. Dumbledore followed, took down what Harry pointed to, and left what he did not. After an hour the stack on the counter was tall enough that the assistant looked at it twice, then at the small boy beside it. Dumbledore paid from the bag, touched the stack with his wand, and put the matchbox it had become into his pocket.

They did not go into the apothecary. Harry looked through its window at the jars and asked.

"Not at Arabella's," Dumbledore said.

"Where, then?"

"We shall see." Harry had learned by then that this was not a refusal.

They went home through the fireplace at the Leaky Cauldron, since Dumbledore had promised another way. This one was green fire, a spoken address, and a long turning that was less bad than the tube. Harry came out of Mrs Figg's gas fire onto the hearthrug with soot on the hat and the parcel in both hands. Mr Tibbles got down off the chair and left the room.

---

At first he could not use it.

The Standard Book assumed a hand of eleven, and said nothing about any other kind. Harry's forearm was shorter than the wand. The movement the book gave for the Levitation Charm, a swish and a flick, turned the wand through an angle his wrist could manage but his fingers could not keep hold through; the wand went one way, his grip went another, and the feather on Mrs Figg's hearthrug stayed where it was. He could see the movement perfectly well. Seeing it was no more use than seeing a word had been when his mouth could not form it. His hand would have to learn, at the speed hands learn.

Light was easier. On the first evening, at the kitchen table, with Mrs Figg beside him as Dumbledore had required, he said the word and made the small movement the book showed, and light appeared at the tip of the wand. It was not his light. His came over his palm, orange, and wavered when he was tired; this one was white and steady and sat at the end of the wood where the book said it should, and holding it cost him nothing. He held it for an hour to find out whether that would change. It did not. "That'll do, I think," said Mrs Figg at the end of the hour, over her tea, and he put it out.

The Levitation Charm took him until January. He practised the movement with the wand for an hour each afternoon, and without it the rest of the day, in the pushchair and in the cupboard, until his hand had it. Then, on a Thursday, he said the word and swished and flicked, and the feather went up. It went up faster than the bottle had, faster than anything had ever moved for him, to the height he had meant, and stopped there. He brought it down. He picked up the Standard Book, which was heavier than anything he had ever lifted by magic, said the word again, and the book rose.

He held it there, a foot above the hearthrug. Nothing heavier than a thin book had moved for him before, and that only along the floor. This one hung in the air of Mrs Figg's front room.

"Well," said Mrs Figg from her chair.

He set it down. While the feel of the book was still in his arm he tried one more thing. He looked at the feather, made the movement, and pictured the feather rising, as he had always pictured things, but said no word. The feather rose. It did not rise as fast or as high; still, it rose, on the wand and the picture alone. He brought it down and left it there for the day, since one thing at a time was how he had done everything in the cupboard and it had kept him out of trouble. He did not know what it meant. He went back to the book and the word, which worked every time, and by February he had every charm in the first year and had begun on the second.
