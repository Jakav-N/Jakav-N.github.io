# Chapter Five

*September 1983 to February 1984*

The bolt was gone from the cupboard door on the Thursday morning, and nobody said why.

His aunt had had a visitor on the Tuesday. Harry had been at Mrs Figg's, and had come home at four to find the front room smelling of a different tobacco and his aunt at the sink with her back to the kitchen door, washing cups. She did not turn round. She said, to the window, that he was to go to Mrs Figg's in the week from now on and that he was not to think it was for his benefit, and that he was to be in by half past four, and that was all she said about it then or afterwards. The bolt was off the door the next morning, and the screw-holes had been filled with something and painted over, and his uncle at breakfast read his newspaper and did not look at the door or at Harry, and Dudley asked where the bolt had gone and nobody answered him.

Harry did not ask either. He had been told that nothing would come back on him and nothing had, and what had come instead was a house that spoke to him less than before, which was not much less, and five days a week at Wisteria Walk with the four books.

---

He had the four books read by the end of the first Tuesday.

The History was long and the Theory was short and the Standard Book of Spells was a list, and Hogwarts: A History was the one he thought about most, because it had a chapter on the library, and the chapter said that the library held some eleven thousand volumes.

The History told him that there had been witches and wizards in Britain since before the Romans, and that for the last three hundred years they had hidden themselves from everyone else by law, because of the burnings and because it was easier. It told him about wars he had not heard of and a prison he had, and it mentioned Voldemort once, near the end, in a short paragraph that said he had risen and fallen and did not say how. The Theory told him that magic was done with a wand and a word and a movement, and that the word and the movement were what made one spell different from another, and said nothing at all about bottles or socks or light that came without any of the three. He read it looking for the sentence that would explain him, and it was not there. The Standard Book told him the words. He tried them, that evening, on the hearthrug, with one of Mrs Figg's knitting needles for a wand, saying the word for light and making the movement the diagram showed, and his own light came as it always came, over his palm and not at the end of the needle, and he could not tell whether the word had done anything or whether it was only that he had been picturing light while he said it. He put the needle back.

Dumbledore came on the second Tuesday, as he had said, and sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's, and asked what Harry had made of the books.

"I've read them," Harry said. "I read them the Tuesday you brought them. I've had nothing since except the library." He had thought about how to say the next part without it sounding like a complaint, and had not found a way, and said it anyway. "I can read a book in about the time it takes Mrs Figg to do the ironing. I had six from the library this week and they lasted a day."

Dumbledore looked at Mrs Figg, who nodded, and then at the four books on the low table, and then for a while at nothing.

"I had thought," he said, "to bring them a few at a time, so that we could talk about each. That was a mistake. I was thinking of a child with a book, and a book lasts you about as long as a cup of tea." He reached into the red suit and brought out his wand, which Harry had not seen him use, and touched the low table with it, and the table had on it a wooden crate, the size of the one the encyclopaedia had come in, with the lid off and books in it to the brim. "Those are the rest of what a student at my school reads in seven years, and some of what the staff read, and a dictionary of the kind of thing you will find in them that is not in your aunt's. The Prophet will come to this house every morning from tomorrow. Arabella has agreed to the owl."

"I've agreed to the owl," said Mrs Figg.

Harry looked at the crate. He did the sum, which was a short one.

"That's about three weeks."

"Then in three weeks we shall see about more." Dumbledore sat back. "Now. You have read the History. Tell me what it left out."

Harry told him. At the end of it he asked the thing he had been holding since the first Tuesday, which was whether he could go to the library the other book had described, the one with eleven thousand volumes in it.

"Not while there are students in the building. In the summer, perhaps. I have not decided."

---

The Prophet came at seven every morning, with an owl, and the owl sat on the kitchen windowsill until Mrs Figg paid it and then went, and Mr Tibbles sat on the draining board and watched it go.

Harry read the paper on the kitchen floor before Mrs Figg had finished her tea. It took him twenty minutes. The photographs moved, a few seconds each and then over again from the beginning, and he watched each one through once. He learned that there was a Ministry with departments and a Minister called Bagnold, and a court, and a hospital, and a bank, and a school, and that those were nearly the whole of it. He learned the prices of things, and what a Galleon was, and that it was seventeen Sickles. He learned that the sport played in the air was called Quidditch and took up a quarter of every issue. He learned that the paper did not print Voldemort's name, two years after he had gone, and wrote You-Know-Who where it would have been.

In the third week of November he learned that the paper knew his name.

It was a small piece on an inside page, under a photograph of a street he recognised from the History as Godric's Hollow, and it said that two years and some weeks after that night the whereabouts of the Boy Who Lived remained unknown, that the Ministry declined to comment, that Albus Dumbledore declined to comment, and that a woman from Ottery St Catchpole had written to say that the boy should be brought up among his own kind where people could see he was all right. There was a letter on the facing page saying the same thing at greater length, and another saying the opposite.

He showed it to Mrs Figg, who read it and said, "Yes, well," and gave it back. He kept it for Dumbledore, who came that Tuesday and read it standing up by the window and put it down.

"How many people know my name?"

"Everyone who reads that paper, and most of the people who don't." Dumbledore sat down. "You are the only person who has ever lived through what was done to you that night, and everyone who was afraid of him knows it, and a good many of them think you did it, rather than that it happened to you. I put you where I did partly so that you would not grow up being looked at for it. It was not the main reason, but it was one of them, and it still is."

"They want to see I'm all right."

"Some of them do."

"Am I?"

Dumbledore did not answer at once. "Not yet," he said. "Better than in September. You are not bolted in, and you have books, and Arabella. I want it better again by the spring, and I am working out how."

---

Mrs Figg took him to the library in person at the end of November, because the woman at the desk had asked to meet the boy the lists were for.

It was a low brick building on the High Street with a ramp and a radiator that knocked, and it smelled of the same thing the encyclopaedia had smelled of under the plastic. He stood inside the door and looked at the shelves going back into the room for as long as Mrs Figg let him, and then she took his hand and walked him to the desk.

The woman at the desk was younger than Mrs Figg and had glasses on a chain. She looked at Harry, and at Mrs Figg, and at Harry again.

"This is him?"

"This is him."

"He can't be more than three."

"He's three."

The woman took one of his lists out of a drawer. It was the one from October, and his writing had been better by then, and she held it up as though she were checking it against him. "You wrote this."

"Yes."

"And you read them. All six. Every week."

"Yes. By Tuesday night. Then I have the week to think about them."

She put the list down. She asked him what he had thought about the one on the stars, and he told her, standing on the other side of a desk that came up to his eyes, and it took some time, and she listened to the end of it and then went into the back and came out with a form, and said that if Mrs Figg would sign for him she would make a ticket out in his own name, and that it was six books. Mrs Figg signed. He had twelve books a week after that, six on each ticket, and he chose his own from the shelves while Mrs Figg sat on the chair by the radiator, and the woman at the desk, whose name was Mrs Dennison, stamped them without comment and sometimes, when he brought her something she had read herself, told him what she had thought of it before he could tell her.

---

Dumbledore took him to London in the second week of December.

He arrived at Mrs Figg's at nine in an overcoat that nearly reached the ground, with a knitted hat for Harry in one pocket. He pulled the hat down over the scar himself. In the kitchen he took Harry's hand and warned him that the next part would be unpleasant for a moment. Then the kitchen was gone.

Apparition, the books called it, and said it was uncomfortable. Harry was pulled through a tube smaller than he was. It pressed on him from every side and from inside; his eyes were pushed back into his head and his chest had no room for breath. For his body this lasted a moment. For him it lasted as long as such things did, and there was nothing to do inside the tube but be pressed. When it ended he was standing on cobbles in cold air, Dumbledore's hand still round his. He stood where he was for a little, though nothing was wrong with him.

Dumbledore looked down. "I shall find another way next time."

They were in a yard behind a pub, with a dustbin. Dumbledore counted bricks up the wall and tapped one with his wand, and the bricks began to turn. Harry saw each one go: they drew back from their neighbours, swung, and settled, until an arch stood where the wall had been and a street ran away beyond it.

The street was narrow and crooked, and the buildings leaned over it. People walked in it wearing cloaks and pointed hats. There were cauldrons stacked on the pavement outside one shop, owls in cages outside another, and a window full of broomsticks with a crowd of boys pressed against the glass. Harry knew the names of these things from the Prophet. Nobody looked at him. Several people looked at Dumbledore; two called him Headmaster, and a woman asked whether the boy was his grandson. "A young friend," Dumbledore said, and walked on, and Harry went beside him under the brim of the hat, looking at all of it.

Gringotts was a white building, taller than its neighbours and not quite straight, and goblins ran it. The History had told him that much without describing a goblin. They were small, with long fingers and clever faces, and the one who took them down to the vault spoke only to Dumbledore. The cart he put them in ran on rails into the rock, and it went fast enough that Harry, for the first time in two years, had to attend to keep up with the walls.

The vault had his name on the key. Dumbledore showed him the key, let him hold it, and gave it to the goblin, who opened the door. Inside there was gold.

Harry had thought about what a heap of gold would look like, and it looked like this: dull rather than bright, heaped loosely, coins the size of a two-pence piece but thicker, with silver piled to one side and bronze beyond. He stood in the doorway and looked, at the height of the heap and how far back it ran and the size of a single coin. By the time Dumbledore had finished saying that this was a good deal of money, enough for school and some way past, Harry had an estimate.

"How much is a Galleon in pounds?"

"About five, at the goblins' rate."

"Then that's about five houses in Privet Drive."

Dumbledore looked at the heap, then at Harry.

"I had not thought of it in those terms. Yes, about that. It isn't a great fortune, but it's a comfortable one, and it's yours. I hold the key until you come of age. I'll spend from it for you when it seems wise, and I'll tell you when I do."

"Books."

"Books, today. Yes."

They filled a bag. The goblin shut the door.

---

Ollivander's was a narrow shop with a single wand on a faded cushion in the window. Inside it was dark and smelled of dust, and the man who came out of the back was old in a way that had nothing to do with Dumbledore's age. His eyes were pale and he did not blink often enough.

He knew Dumbledore. He knew Harry too, hat or no hat; he looked at him for a long moment, then at the hat, and said, "Ah." He had wondered, he said, when he would be seeing Mr Potter, and had not expected it quite so soon. Then he described Lily's wand and James's, length and wood and core and temperament, as if he had sold them last week. He measured Harry with a tape that moved by itself, went into the back, and returned with an armful of boxes.

The first wand was in Harry's hand for a second before Ollivander took it back. The second did nothing either. With the third Ollivander said, "Try," and Harry, who did not know what trying meant, waved it; a vase went off the counter and broke on the floor, and that wand went back in its box. Thirty-one more followed. Harry held each one and waved it as he had waved the first, and watched Ollivander's face, which grew more interested rather than less as the pile of rejected boxes rose.

Then one was warm.

It was warm before he moved it, the moment it touched his palm, the way a cup is warm. When he waved it red and gold sparks poured from the tip, lit the shop, hung in the dust, and came down slowly enough for him to watch each one land.

"Well," said Mr Ollivander. "Well, well."

Dumbledore had stood by the door with his hands folded through all of it. He said nothing now.

"Holly. Eleven inches. The core is phoenix feather, which is unusual in itself." Ollivander took the wand back gently and began wrapping it in brown paper without taking his eyes off Harry. "The phoenix that gave this feather gave one other, and I remember both. The other went into a wand I sold a long time ago to a young man who did a great many things with it. The last I know of is the mark under your hat. I find it curious that this wand should have chosen you. I don't say it means anything."

Harry took the parcel. He looked up at Dumbledore. Dumbledore's face had not changed, and Harry kept that as well.

"Seven Galleons," said Mr Ollivander.

---

In Flourish and Blotts the books went to the ceiling, up a staircase, and across the floor in piles, and all of them were about magic. Harry stopped inside the door as he had at the library. Dumbledore put a hand on his shoulder and said that they had some time but not all day, and that he had better begin.

He went along the shelves reading spines. Dumbledore followed, took down what Harry pointed to, and left what he did not. After an hour the stack on the counter was tall enough that the assistant looked at it twice, then at the small boy beside it. Dumbledore paid from the bag, touched the stack with his wand, and put the matchbox it had become into his pocket.

They did not go into the apothecary. Harry looked through its window at the jars and asked.

"Not at Arabella's," Dumbledore said.

"Where, then?"

"We shall see." Harry had learned by then that this was not a refusal.

They went home through the fireplace at the Leaky Cauldron, since Dumbledore had promised another way. This one was green fire, a spoken address, and a long turning that was less bad than the tube. Harry came out of Mrs Figg's gas fire onto the hearthrug with soot on the hat and the parcel in both hands. Mr Tibbles got down off the chair and left the room.

---

He could not use it at first.

That was the thing he had not read in the Standard Book, which assumed a hand of eleven. His arm from elbow to wrist was shorter than the wand, and the movement the diagram showed for the Levitation Charm, which the book called a swish and a flick, needed the wand to turn at the wrist through an angle that his wrist could do and his fingers could not hold the wand through, so that the wand went one way and his grip went another and the feather on Mrs Figg's hearthrug lay where it was. He could see what the movement was. He could see it exactly, the way he could see a page. His hand could not do it, as it had not been able to do the pencil, and the only cure for that was the same, which was time at the pace hands learned at.

The light was easy. He said the word on the first evening, in the kitchen with Mrs Figg beside him at the table as Dumbledore had required, and made the small movement the book showed, which his hand could manage, and light came at the end of the wand. It was not his light. His light came over his palm and was orange and wavered when he was tired. This was white and steady and at the end of the wood where the book said it should be, and it cost him nothing to hold, and he held it for an hour to find out whether it would cost him anything over time and it did not. Mrs Figg sat and watched it and drank her tea and said, when the hour was up, "That'll do, I think," and he put it out.

The Levitation Charm took him until January. He practised the movement with the wand an hour every afternoon at Mrs Figg's and without it the rest of the day, in the pushchair, in the cupboard, until his hand had it, and then on a Thursday in January he said the word and made the movement and the feather rose. It rose faster than the bottle had. It rose faster than anything had ever moved for him, and went up to the height he had meant and stopped there and hung, steady, and he brought it down, and picked up the Standard Book, which was heavier than any book had ever been for him, and said the word again, and the book rose.

He held it there, a foot above the hearthrug, and looked at it. Nothing heavier than a thin book had ever moved for him, and that only along the floor, and this one hung in the air of Mrs Figg's front room.

"Well," said Mrs Figg from her chair.

He set the book down. There was a thing he wanted to try and he tried it then, while the feeling of the book was still in his arm. He looked at the feather, and made the movement, and did not say the word, and pictured the feather rising as he had always pictured things, and it rose. Not as fast. Not as high. But it rose, with the wand and the picture and no word, and he brought it down and did not try it again that day, because one thing at a time was how he had done everything in the cupboard and it had kept him out of trouble. He did not know what it meant. He went back to the book and the word, which worked every time, and by February he had the first year's charms, every one, and had begun the second.
