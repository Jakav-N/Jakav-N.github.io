# Chapter Five

*September 1983 to February 1984*

On the Wednesday morning the bolt was gone from the cupboard door, and nobody said why.

His aunt had had a visitor the day before, while Harry was at Mrs Figg's. When he came home at four the front room smelled of a tobacco that was not his uncle's, and his aunt was at the sink with her back to the door, washing cups. She told the window that he was to go to Mrs Figg's every weekday from now on, that he was not to think it was for his benefit, and that he was to be in by half past four. That was all she ever said about it. The next morning the bolt was off and the screw-holes had been filled and painted. His uncle read the paper through breakfast without looking at the door. Dudley asked where the bolt had gone, and nobody answered him.

---

He had read the four books by the end of the first Tuesday, and on the second Tuesday Dumbledore came and asked what he had made of them.

"I've read them," Harry said. "I read them the day you brought them. Since then there's been the library."

"And?"

"The History is good and leaves things out. The Theory says a spell needs a wand and a word and a movement, and doesn't say anything about what I do, which has none of those. The Standard Book is a list. The one about the school says the library has eleven thousand books in it." He had been waiting a fortnight to say the next part. "A book takes me about as long as Mrs Figg's ironing. Six from the library last a day. Four of yours lasted an afternoon."

Dumbledore looked at the four books on the table, and at Mrs Figg, who nodded.

"I was thinking of a child with a book," he said. "A book lasts you about as long as a cup of tea. That was my mistake." He took out his wand, which Harry had not seen him use, and touched the table, and a wooden crate stood on it with the lid off and books to the brim. "That's what a pupil at my school reads in seven years, with some of what the staff read, and a dictionary of the words you'll find that aren't in your aunt's. The Prophet will come here every morning from tomorrow. Arabella has agreed to the owl."

"I've agreed to the owl," said Mrs Figg.

Harry looked at the crate. "That's about three weeks."

"Then in three weeks we'll see about more."

"And the other kind. Books from my aunt's side. The library on the High Street has a shelf and a half on the sciences and I've read it. There'll be shops in London with more."

"There will. I know one. Write me a list."

"Can I go to the library at the school? The one with eleven thousand."

"Not while there are pupils in the building. In the summer, perhaps. I haven't decided."

The list went the following Tuesday. A fortnight later Dumbledore carried the first box up Wisteria Walk himself, in the rain, in a Muggle coat: mathematics for sixteen-year-olds, the physics that went with it, a dictionary of the kind Muggles used, and a history of England in one volume. Harry had asked for those first because everything else he wanted leaned on them. The mathematics was done by the end of October, and he had a second list.

---

The Prophet came at seven every morning with an owl that waited on the kitchen sill to be paid, and Harry read it on the floor before Mrs Figg had finished her tea.

It took him twenty minutes. The photographs moved, a few seconds each and then over again, and he watched each one through once. The paper told him that there was a Ministry of Magic with departments and a Minister called Bagnold; that there was a court, a hospital, a bank and a school, and that those were nearly the whole of the wizarding state. It told him prices. It told him that a Galleon was seventeen Sickles. It told him that the sport played in the air was called Quidditch and took up a quarter of every issue. It did not print Voldemort's name, two years after he had gone, and wrote You-Know-Who where the name would have been.

In the third week of November it printed Harry's.

The piece was small, on an inside page, under a photograph of Godric's Hollow. Two years and some weeks after that night, it said, the whereabouts of the Boy Who Lived remained unknown. The Ministry declined to comment. Albus Dumbledore declined to comment. A woman from Ottery St Catchpole had written in to say that the boy ought to be brought up among his own kind, where people could see he was being looked after, and the facing page carried two letters, one agreeing with her and one not.

He showed it to Mrs Figg, who read it and said, "Yes, well," and gave it back. He kept it for Dumbledore, who read it standing at the window and put it down.

"How many people know my name?"

"Everyone who reads that paper, and most of the people who don't. You're the only person who has ever lived through what was done to you that night. Everyone who was afraid of him knows it, and a good many of them think you did it rather than that it happened to you. I put you here partly so that you wouldn't grow up being looked at for it. That wasn't the main reason. It was one of them."

"They want to see I'm all right."

"Some of them do."

"Am I?"

Dumbledore thought about it. "Not yet. Better than you were in September. Nobody bolts you in, you have books, and you have Arabella. I want it better still by the spring, and I'm working out how."

---

Mrs Figg took him to the library in person at the end of November, because the woman at the desk had asked to meet the boy the lists were for.

The library was a low brick building on the High Street with a ramp to the door and a radiator that knocked. He stopped inside the door to look at the shelves going back into the room, and Mrs Figg let him look for a while before she took his hand and walked him to the desk.

The woman there was younger than Mrs Figg and wore her glasses on a chain. She looked at Harry, and at Mrs Figg, and at Harry again.

"This is him?"

"This is him."

"He can't be more than three."

"He's three."

The woman took one of his lists out of a drawer. It was October's. She held it up as if to check it against him. "You wrote this."

"Yes."

"And you read them. All six, every week."

"By Tuesday night. Then I have the week to think about them."

She asked what he had thought of the one about the stars. He told her, standing on the far side of a desk that came up to his eyes. It took a while. She heard him out, went into the back, and came back with a form: if Mrs Figg would sign for him, she said, she would make out a ticket in his own name, six books. Mrs Figg signed. From then on he had twelve a week and chose them himself while Mrs Figg sat in the chair by the radiator. The woman at the desk was called Mrs Dennison. She stamped his books without comment, except that now and then, when he brought up something she had read herself, she told him what she had thought of it before he could tell her.

---

Dumbledore took him to London in the second week of December.

He came to Wisteria Walk at nine in an overcoat that nearly reached the ground, with a knitted hat for Harry in one pocket, and pulled the hat down over the scar himself. In the kitchen he took Harry's hand and warned him that the next part would be unpleasant for a moment. Then the kitchen was gone.

The books called it Apparition and said it was uncomfortable. Harry was pulled through a tube smaller than he was. It pressed on him from every side and from inside; his eyes were pushed back into his head and his chest had no room for breath. For his body it lasted a moment. For him it lasted as such things did, and there was nothing to do inside it but be pressed. When it ended he was standing on cobbles in cold air with Dumbledore's hand round his. He stood still for a little, though nothing was wrong with him.

"I'll find another way next time," Dumbledore said.

They were in a yard behind a pub, with a dustbin. Dumbledore counted bricks up the wall and tapped one with his wand, and the bricks began to turn. Harry watched each one go: they drew back from their neighbours, swung, and settled, until an arch stood where the wall had been and a street ran away beyond it.

The street was narrow and crooked and the buildings leaned over it. People in cloaks and pointed hats walked in it. There were cauldrons stacked on the pavement outside one shop, owls in cages outside another, and a window full of broomsticks with boys pressed against the glass. Nobody looked at Harry. Several people looked at Dumbledore; two called him Headmaster, and a woman asked whether the boy was his grandson. "A young friend," Dumbledore said, and walked on.

Gringotts was white and taller than its neighbours and not quite straight, and goblins ran it. The one who took them down to the vault spoke only to Dumbledore. The cart ran on rails into the rock, fast enough that Harry, for the first time in two years, had to attend to keep up with the walls.

The vault had his name on the key. Inside there was gold, heaped loosely, dull rather than bright, coins the size of a two-pence piece but thicker, with silver to one side and bronze beyond. He stood in the doorway and looked at the height of the heap and how far back it ran and the size of one coin, and by the time Dumbledore had finished saying that it was a good deal of money, enough for school and some way past, he had an estimate.

"How much is a Galleon in pounds?"

"About five, at the goblins' rate."

"Then that's about five houses in Privet Drive."

Dumbledore looked at the heap, and then at Harry. "I hadn't thought of it in those terms. Yes, about that. It isn't a great fortune. It's a comfortable one, and it's yours. I hold the key until you're of age, and I'll spend from it for you when it seems wise, and tell you when I do."

"Does it do anything? Sitting there."

"No."

"Could some of it be pounds, in a bank, where it would?"

Dumbledore did not answer at once. He said, in the cart going up, that he would find out what a wizard's gold could lawfully become and who could hold it for a child, that it would not be much and would not be soon, and that he would like Harry's reasons on paper first. Harry wrote them that week, two pages in pencil, which took him an evening.

Ollivander's was a narrow shop with a single wand on a faded cushion in the window. Inside it was dark and smelled of dust. The man who came out of the back was old in a way that had nothing to do with Dumbledore's age, with pale eyes that did not blink often enough, and he knew Harry under the hat; he looked at him, and at the hat, and said, "Ah." He had wondered, he said, when he would be seeing Mr Potter, and had not expected it so soon. He described Lily's wand and James's, wood and length and core, as if he had sold them last week. Then he measured Harry with a tape that moved by itself, and went into the back, and came out with boxes.

The first wand was in Harry's hand for a second before Ollivander took it back. The second did nothing either. With the third Ollivander said, "Try," and Harry, who did not know what trying meant, waved it; a vase went off the counter and broke on the floor. Thirty-one more followed. Harry held each one and waved it as he had the first, and watched Ollivander's face, which grew more interested rather than less as the pile of boxes rose.

Then one was warm. It was warm the moment it touched his palm, the way a cup is warm. When he waved it, red and gold sparks poured from the tip and lit the shop and hung in the dust and came down slowly enough for him to watch each one land.

"Well," said Mr Ollivander. "Well, well."

Dumbledore had stood by the door with his hands folded through all of it, and said nothing now.

"Holly. Eleven inches. Phoenix feather, which is unusual in itself." Ollivander took the wand back gently and began wrapping it in brown paper without taking his eyes off Harry. "The phoenix that gave this feather gave one other, and I remember both. The other went into a wand I sold a long time ago to a young man who did a great many things with it. The last I know of is the mark under your hat. I find it curious that this wand should have chosen you. I don't say it means anything."

Harry took the parcel and looked up at Dumbledore. Dumbledore's face had not changed. Harry kept that too.

"Seven Galleons," said Mr Ollivander.

In Flourish and Blotts the books went to the ceiling and up a staircase and across the floor in piles, and all of them were about magic. Harry stopped inside the door as he had at the library. Dumbledore put a hand on his shoulder and said they had some time but not all day. Harry went along the shelves reading spines; Dumbledore followed and took down what he pointed to; after an hour the stack on the counter was tall enough that the assistant looked at it twice and then at the small boy beside it. Dumbledore paid from the bag, touched the stack with his wand, and put the matchbox it had become in his pocket.

Then they went back through the wall and out of the pub into Charing Cross Road, where Dumbledore, with the hat pulled down over his own hair as well, walked Harry two hundred yards to a bookshop with five floors. Nobody in it was surprised to see a child. Dumbledore changed forty Galleons' worth of pounds at a desk on the ground floor, which took some time and some explaining, and found a basket, and Harry went along the shelves with his second list: mathematics for undergraduates, physics in three volumes, chemistry in two, a thick biology, an economics textbook with a blue cover, a history of England in four, a book on how the law worked, a dictionary of medicine, a book on statistics. The till came to a hundred and ninety pounds. Dumbledore paid in notes, touched the bag with his wand inside his coat, and put the result in his pocket beside the other matchbox.

They went home through the fireplace at the Leaky Cauldron, which was green fire and a spoken address and a long turning that was less bad than the tube. Harry came out of Mrs Figg's gas fire onto the hearthrug with soot on the hat and the parcel in both hands. Mr Tibbles got down off the chair and left the room.

---

At first he could not use the wand.

The Standard Book assumed a hand of eleven and said nothing about any other kind. Harry's forearm was shorter than the wand. The movement the book gave for the Levitation Charm, a swish and a flick, turned the wand through an angle his wrist could manage and his fingers could not keep hold through, so that the wand went one way and his grip went another and the feather on the hearthrug stayed where it was. He could see the movement perfectly well. Seeing it was no more use than seeing a word had been when his mouth could not form it. His hand would have to learn at the speed hands learn.

Light was easier. On the first evening, at the kitchen table, with Mrs Figg beside him as Dumbledore had required, he said the word and made the small movement the book showed, and light came at the tip of the wand. It was not his light. His came over his palm, orange, and wavered when he was tired; this was white and steady and sat at the end of the wood, and holding it cost him nothing. He held it for an hour to find out whether that would change, and it did not. "That'll do, I think," said Mrs Figg at the end of the hour, and he put it out.

The Levitation Charm took him until January. He practised the movement with the wand for an hour each afternoon and without it the rest of the day, in the pushchair and in the cupboard, until his hand had it. Then, on a Thursday, he said the word and swished and flicked, and the feather went up, faster than the bottle had, faster than anything had ever moved for him, to the height he had meant, and stopped there. He brought it down. He picked up the Standard Book, which was heavier than anything he had ever lifted by magic, said the word again, and the book rose and hung a foot above the hearthrug.

"Well," said Mrs Figg from her chair.

---

The nights at Privet Drive were a different matter, because he still slept there, and could bring nothing home.

His aunt would have found a book and said so. From the time the house settled until the first bird he had six hours, a light, and nothing to read, and he had had that since September. In December in London he had read eleven of the books that were sold to sixteen-year-olds to help them pass their examinations, and they had all been bad in the same ways. So in January he began to write one. He sat on the mattress with the pencil from the bureau and a pad Mrs Figg had bought him, and wrote the mathematics that sixteen-year-olds were examined on, laid out so that someone could learn it from the book alone, with the places where people went wrong marked. His hand managed three pages an hour and gave out after two. It was done by the middle of February: two hundred and six pages in a hand that had improved a good deal and was still a child's. He began the chemistry the next night. Mrs Figg kept the finished pages in her sideboard, and asked once who they were for, and he said whoever would pay for them.

The other thing he had been carrying since October was Mrs Figg's owls.

She bred cats, Kneazle crosses, and had sold them by owl for thirty years. Her customers wrote to her from all over the country about kittens and tempers and sent their Galleons in little bags, and she wrote back in a careful hand. He had read the letters in October, with her permission, because they were the only picture he had of what ordinary wizards wanted. One letter in four, somewhere in the business about the cat, mentioned a Muggle thing: a torch somebody's cousin had brought back, a pen that needed no inkwell, a pair of trousers with a zip. They mentioned these the way his uncle mentioned a new car. Mrs Figg shopped on the High Street every Friday and sent owls every week already. In December he had asked Dumbledore for twenty pounds in notes from the vault, and got them.

On the second Tuesday in January he asked her whether she would put a sheet in with her next letters.

"Saying what?"

"That you can get Muggle things, and what they cost. Pens that don't need ink. Torches, with the batteries. Sticky tape. Flasks that keep tea hot all day. Notebooks with lines. Nothing with a plug and nothing anyone's enchanted, so there's nothing for the Ministry to mind. I've read the law on it. I'd give you the money, you'd buy them on Friday with the shopping, and they'd go out with the kittens' letters. I'd pay you for the owls and the trouble, and whatever's left is for the list."

Mrs Figg looked at him over her cup. "What list?"

"The one I keep. Of what wants doing."

"And what does a pen that doesn't need ink sell for, to a wizard?"

"I don't know. That's why I'd start with your customers. They'll tell me by what they buy."

She thought about it until the cup was empty. Then she said that she had been sending kittens by owl for thirty years and supposed she could send a pen, and that she would want it in writing what was hers and what was his, because she had seen what happened in families over less. He wrote it that night in the cupboard, a page in pencil, and she read it twice on the Wednesday and signed it, and Mr Tibbles sat on it.

The first sheet went out with eleven letters in the third week of January, priced by guess: a pen at two Sickles, a torch at a Galleon with its batteries, a roll of tape at a Sickle. The pen had cost twelve pence. Nine of the eleven wrote back with orders, and one of them ordered twelve pens and asked whether there were other colours. By March the sheet went to every customer Mrs Figg had. She came back from the High Street on Fridays with two bags instead of one and wrapped parcels at the kitchen table on Mondays while Harry read her the Prophet, and the Galleons came back in little bags that she kept in a biscuit tin. There were sixty in it by the end of March.

In January, too, he asked Dumbledore a question that had been waiting since the crate. There was a potion, new, that let a werewolf keep his mind through the night of the moon, and the book said it was expensive in those words. He asked whether Dumbledore knew anyone who could brew it and needed the work.

"Nobody has ever asked me that," Dumbledore said.

"I'm asking."

Dumbledore said he would think, and Harry had learned by then that this meant he would.
