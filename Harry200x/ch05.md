# Chapter Five

*September 1983 to February 1984*

The bolt was gone from the cupboard door on the Thursday morning, and nobody said why.

His aunt had had a visitor on the Wednesday. Harry had been at Mrs Figg's, which was where he was now on every weekday, and had come home at four to find the front room smelling of a different tobacco and his aunt at the sink with her back to the kitchen door, washing two cups that had already been washed. She did not turn round. She said, to the window, that he was to go to Mrs Figg's in the week from now on and that he was not to think it was for his benefit, and that he was to be in by half past four, and that was all she said about it then or afterwards. The bolt was off the door the next morning, and the screw-holes had been filled with something and painted over, and his uncle at breakfast read his newspaper and did not look at the door or at Harry, and Dudley asked where the bolt had gone and was given a second sausage.

Harry did not ask either. He had been told that nothing would come back on him and nothing had, and what had come instead was a house that spoke to him less than before, which was not much less, and five days a week at Wisteria Walk with the four books.

---

He had the four books read by the end of the first Tuesday.

He had known he would, and had read them slowly, for him, which made no difference, since slowly for him was the pace his eyes went at and no other. The History was long and the Theory was short and the Standard Book of Spells was a list, and Hogwarts: A History was the one he went back to, because it had a chapter on the library, and the chapter said that the library held some eleven thousand volumes, and he sat on Mrs Figg's hearthrug with that number for some time.

The History told him there had been people like his mother for as long as there had been people, and that they had hidden themselves from everyone else by law for three hundred years, and why. It told him about wars he had not heard of and a prison he had, and it mentioned the man with the red eyes once, near the end, in a paragraph that had been written since the night the ceiling came off and was careful not to say very much. The Theory told him that magic was done with a wand and a word and a movement, and that the word and the movement were what made one spell different from another, and said nothing at all about bottles or socks or light that came without any of the three. He read it twice looking for the sentence that would explain him and it was not there. The Standard Book told him the words. He tried them, that evening, on the hearthrug, with one of Mrs Figg's knitting needles for a wand, saying the word for light and making the movement the diagram showed, and his own light came as it always came, over his palm and not at the end of the needle, and he could not tell whether the word had done anything or whether it was only that he had been picturing light while he said it. He put the needle back.

Dumbledore came on the second Tuesday, as he had said, and sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's, and asked what Harry had made of the books.

"I've read them twice," Harry said. "There was nothing else."

"Twice."

"The first time on the Tuesday you brought them. The second on the Wednesday. I've had them since." He had thought about how to say the next part without it sounding like a complaint, and had not found a way, and said it anyway. "I can read a book in about the time it takes Mrs Figg to do the ironing. I had six from the library this week and they lasted a day."

Dumbledore looked at Mrs Figg, who nodded, and then at the four books on the low table, and then for a while at nothing.

"I had thought," he said, "to bring them to you a few at a time, so that we might talk about each. That was a mistake of the ordinary kind. I was thinking of a child with a book, and you are a child with a book for about the time it takes to make tea." He reached into the red suit and brought out his wand, which Harry had not seen him use, and touched the low table with it, and the table had on it a wooden crate, the size of the one the encyclopaedia had come in, with the lid off and books in it to the brim. "Those are the rest of what a student at my school reads in seven years, and some of what the staff read, and a dictionary of the kind of thing you will find in them that is not in your aunt's. The Prophet will come to this house every morning from tomorrow. Arabella has agreed to the owl."

"I've agreed to the owl," said Mrs Figg. "I haven't agreed to what the cats will make of it."

Harry looked at the crate. He did the sum, which was a short one.

"That's about three weeks."

"Then in three weeks we shall see about more." Dumbledore sat back. "Now. You have read the History. Tell me what it left out."

Harry told him. At the end of it he asked the thing he had been holding since the first Tuesday, which was whether he could go to the library the other book had described, the one with eleven thousand volumes in it.

"Not while there are students in the building. In the summer, perhaps. I have not decided."

---

The Prophet came at seven every morning, with an owl, and the owl sat on the kitchen windowsill until Mrs Figg paid it and then went, and Mr Tibbles sat on the draining board and watched it go and did nothing, which Mrs Figg said was the most she had hoped for.

Harry read the paper on the kitchen floor before Mrs Figg had finished her tea. It took him most of an hour, because the pictures moved and he watched them. He learned that there was a Ministry with departments and a Minister called Bagnold, and a court, and a hospital, and a bank, and a school, and that those were nearly the whole of it. He learned the prices of things, and what a Galleon was, and that it was seventeen Sickles. He learned that the sport played in the air was called Quidditch and took up a quarter of every issue. He learned that the man with the red eyes was not named in the paper, ever, by anyone, and that there were several ways of not naming him and the paper used all of them, two years after he had gone.

In the third week of November he learned that the paper knew his name.

It was a small piece on an inside page, under a photograph of a street he recognised from the History as Godric's Hollow, and it said that two years and some weeks after that night the whereabouts of the Boy Who Lived remained unknown, that the Ministry declined to comment, that Albus Dumbledore declined to comment, and that a Mrs somebody of Ottery St Catchpole had written to say that the boy should be brought up among his own kind where people could see he was all right. There was a letter on the facing page saying the same thing at greater length, and another saying the opposite.

He showed it to Mrs Figg, who read it and said, "Yes, well," and gave it back. He kept it for Dumbledore, who came that Tuesday and read it standing up by the window and put it down.

"How many people know my name?"

"Everyone who reads that paper. Everyone who does not." Dumbledore sat down. "You are the only person who has ever lived through what was done to you that night, and everyone who was afraid of him knows it, and most of them think you did it on purpose. I put you where I did partly so that you would not grow up being looked at for it. That was one reason among several, and it was not the one that mattered most, but it was a reason, and it still is."

"They want to see I'm all right."

"Some of them do."

"Am I?"

Dumbledore did not answer at once. "You are reading at three," he said. "You are not bolted in. You have a crate of books and a woman who argues with you about them. I would not call it all right. I would call it better than it was in September, and I would like it to be better again by the spring, and I am working on how."

---

Mrs Figg took him to the library in person at the end of November, because the woman at the desk had asked to meet the boy the lists were for.

It was a low brick building on the High Street with a ramp and a radiator that knocked, and it smelled of the same thing the encyclopaedia had smelled of under the plastic. He stood inside the door and looked at the shelves going back into the room for as long as Mrs Figg let him, and then she took his hand and walked him to the desk.

The woman at the desk was younger than Mrs Figg and had glasses on a chain. She looked at Harry, and at Mrs Figg, and at Harry again.

"This is him?"

"This is him."

"He's three."

"He's three."

The woman took one of his lists out of a drawer. It was the one from October, and his writing had been better by then, and she held it up as though she were checking it against him. "You wrote this."

"Yes."

"And you read them. All six. Every week."

"Yes. By Tuesday night. Then I have the week to think about them."

She put the list down. She asked him what he had thought about the one on the stars, and he told her, standing on the other side of a desk that came up to his eyes, and it took some time, and she listened to the end of it and then went into the back and came out with a form, and said that if Mrs Figg would sign for him she would make a ticket out in his own name, and that it was six books. Mrs Figg signed. He had twelve books a week after that, six on each ticket, and he chose his own from the shelves while Mrs Figg sat on the chair by the radiator, and the woman at the desk, whose name was Mrs Dennison, stamped them without comment and sometimes, when he brought her something she had read herself, told him what she had thought of it before he could tell her.

---

Dumbledore took him to London in the second week of December.

He came to Mrs Figg's at nine in the morning in an overcoat that nearly reached the ground, and put a knitted hat on Harry's head and pulled it down over the scar, and took his hand in the kitchen, and said that this would be unpleasant and brief, and that he was sorry for the first and could do nothing about it, and Harry said, "All right," and the kitchen went.

It was like being pulled through a tube that was smaller than he was. The History had a word for it, and he had read the word and not understood it, and now he understood it and would have given a good deal not to. It pressed on him from every side at once and from inside, and his eyes were pushed into his head and his chest had no room in it for breath, and it went on. Dumbledore had said brief. For Dumbledore it was. Harry had time, inside the tube, to notice that he was not going to be able to breathe until it ended and to be afraid of that and to tell himself that Dumbledore had done this a thousand times and to not believe himself, and then it ended and he was standing on cobbles in cold air with Dumbledore's hand still round his, bent over, pulling breath in.

"That was longer for you than for me, I think."

"Yes."

"I shall find another way next time."

They were in a yard behind a building with a dustbin in it, and Dumbledore counted bricks in the wall and tapped one with his wand, and the wall opened. Harry watched it open. The bricks turned and drew back from one another at a pace that would have been quick for a wall, and that he could see every part of, and beyond them there was a street.

It was narrow and went crooked and the buildings leaned over it, and there were people in it in cloaks and hats, and shops with things in the windows he knew the names of from the Prophet and had not seen: cauldrons stacked on the pavement, owls in cages, a window full of brooms with a crowd of boys at it. Nobody looked at him. Several people looked at Dumbledore, and two of them said, "Headmaster," and one asked whether the boy was his grandson and Dumbledore said, "A young friend," and walked on. Harry walked beside him with his hand in Dumbledore's and his hat over his eyes and looked at everything.

Gringotts was white and crooked and run by goblins. He knew that from the History, and the History had not said what they looked like, and they looked like nothing he had read about. The one who took them down to the vault did not speak to Harry and did not look at him, and the cart it put them in went down into the rock very fast, by anyone's measure, so that even Harry felt it, and the walls went by him at the pace of a train, which was the first time in two years anything had gone by him at the pace it was meant to.

The vault was his. Dumbledore said so, and showed him the key, and put the key in his hand afterwards and let him hold it, and the goblin opened the door, and inside was money.

He had expected money. He had read about Galleons in the Prophet and thought about what a pile of gold would look like. It looked like this: heaped, dull, not shining much in the lamplight, coins the size of a two-pence piece and thicker, with silver heaped to one side and bronze to the other. He stood in the doorway and did what he did with everything, which was look, and he looked at the heap and at its height and at how far it went back, and at the size of one coin, and he had an estimate before Dumbledore had finished saying that it was a good deal of money and would see him through school and some way past.

"How much is a Galleon in pounds?"

"About five, at the goblins' rate, which is not a rate anyone else gets to set."

"Then that's about five houses in Privet Drive."

Dumbledore looked at the heap, and at Harry, and Harry saw him do the sum and come out near the same place.

"I had not thought of it in those terms. Yes. About that. It is not a great fortune, as these things go. It is a comfortable one, and it is yours, and until you are of age I hold the key and will spend it on your behalf when I think it wise and tell you when I do."

"Books."

"Books, today. Yes."

They took a bag of it. The goblin shut the door.

---

Ollivander's was a narrow shop with one wand on a cushion in the window and dust on the cushion, and it was dark inside, and the man who came out of the back was old in a different way from Dumbledore, with pale eyes that did not blink enough.

He knew Dumbledore. He knew Harry, too, which Harry had not expected, because of the hat. He looked at Harry for a long time and then at the hat and said, "Ah," and said that he had wondered when he would be seeing him, and that he had not thought it would be quite so soon, and then he said what his mother's wand had been, and what his father's, in detail, as though he had sold them the week before. Then he measured Harry with a tape that measured by itself, and went into the back, and came out with boxes.

Harry held a wand, and it was taken away. He held another. The man said, "Try," and Harry did not know what to try, and waved it, and a vase on the counter went over, and the man took that one away too. There were a good many after that. He stopped counting them when it became clear the man was not going to stop, and held each one as it was given and waved it as he had waved the first and watched the man's face, which grew more interested rather than less.

Then he held one that was warm.

It was warm before he did anything, as soon as it was in his hand, the way the handle of a cup is warm, and he waved it as he had waved the others and red and gold came out of the end of it in a shower that lit the shop and hung in the dust and came down slowly enough for him to watch every spark of it land.

"Well," said Mr Ollivander. "Well, well."

Dumbledore, who had stood by the door with his hands folded through all of it, did not say anything.

"Holly," said Mr Ollivander. "Eleven inches. Phoenix feather, which is not a common core, and this is not a common feather." He took the wand from Harry, gently, and wrapped it in brown paper, and did not stop looking at him while he did it. "The phoenix whose feather is in this wand gave one other. One. I remember both. The other is in a wand I sold a very long time ago to a young man who did a great many things with it afterwards, and the last thing I know of that he did with it was to give you that mark under your hat. It is curious that this wand should have chosen you. I do not say it means anything. I say it is curious."

Harry took the parcel. He looked at Dumbledore, and Dumbledore's face had not moved, and he kept that too.

"Seven Galleons," said Mr Ollivander.

---

Flourish and Blotts was the only shop that day in which anyone had to tell him it was time to go.

He had thought, in the library on the High Street, that he had seen a lot of books in one place. Flourish and Blotts had them to the ceiling and up a staircase and in piles on the floor, and every one of them was about the thing the Theory had not explained, and he stood inside the door, as he had in the library, until Dumbledore put a hand on his shoulder and said that they would be here some time and he had better begin. He began. He went along the shelves reading spines, and Dumbledore came behind him and took down what he pointed to and did not take down what he did not, and in an hour there was a stack on the counter that the man behind it looked at twice, and Dumbledore paid for it out of the bag from the vault and touched it with his wand, and it was the size of a matchbox and went into his pocket.

They did not go into the apothecary. Harry stood outside it and looked through the window at the jars and asked, and Dumbledore said, "Not at Arabella's," and Harry said, "Where, then," and Dumbledore said, "We shall see," which Harry had learned by then was not no.

They went home by the fireplace at the Leaky Cauldron, because Dumbledore had said he would find another way, and the other way was green fire and a word and a great deal of turning, which was also long for Harry and less bad, and he came out of Mrs Figg's gas fire onto her hearthrug with soot on the hat and the wand in brown paper in both hands, and Mr Tibbles got off the chair and left the room.

---

He could not use it at first.

That was the thing he had not read in the Standard Book, which assumed a hand of eleven. His arm from elbow to wrist was shorter than the wand, and the movement the diagram showed for the Levitation Charm, which the book called a swish and a flick, needed the wand to turn at the wrist through an angle that his wrist could do and his fingers could not hold the wand through, so that the wand went one way and his grip went another and the feather on Mrs Figg's hearthrug lay where it was. He could see what the movement was. He could see it exactly, the way he could see a page. His hand could not do it, as it had not been able to do the pencil, and the only cure for that was the same, which was time at the pace hands learned at.

The light was easy. He said the word on the first evening, in the kitchen with Mrs Figg beside him at the table as Dumbledore had required, and made the small movement the book showed, which his hand could manage, and light came at the end of the wand. It was not his light. His light came over his palm and was orange and wavered when he was tired. This was white and steady and at the end of the wood where the book said it should be, and it cost him nothing to hold, and he held it for an hour to find out whether it would cost him anything over time and it did not. Mrs Figg sat and watched it and drank her tea and said, when the hour was up, "That'll do, I think," and he put it out.

The Levitation Charm took him until January. He practised the movement with the wand an hour every afternoon at Mrs Figg's and without it the rest of the day, in the pushchair, in the cupboard, until his hand had it, and then on a Thursday in January he said the word and made the movement and the feather rose. It rose faster than the bottle had. It rose faster than anything had ever moved for him, and went up to the height he had meant and stopped there and hung, steady, and he brought it down, and picked up the Standard Book, which was heavier than any book had ever been for him, and said the word again, and the book rose.

He held it there, a foot above the hearthrug, and looked at it. Nothing larger than a sock had ever moved for him in two years of trying, and the book hung in the air of Mrs Figg's front room.

"Well," said Mrs Figg from her chair.

He set the book down. There was a thing he wanted to try and he tried it then, while the feeling of the book was still in his arm. He looked at the feather, and made the movement, and did not say the word, and pictured the feather rising as he had always pictured things, and it rose. Not as fast. Not as high. But it rose, with the wand and the picture and no word, and he brought it down and did not try it again that day, because one thing at a time was how he had done everything in the cupboard and it had kept him out of trouble. He did not know what it meant. He put it with the things he did not know what they meant, which was a growing pile, and went back to the book and the word, which worked every time, and by February he had the first year's charms, every one, and had begun the second.
