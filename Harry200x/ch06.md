# Chapter Six

*March to June 1984*

Professor McGonagall came on a Thursday evening in March, after her last class, and she came by the gas fire.

Mrs Figg had been told to expect it and had moved the hearthrug back and shut the cats in the kitchen, and Harry sat on the settee with the second-year Transfiguration book on his knees and watched the fire go green and a tall woman step out of it into the front room, brushing nothing off a dark green cloak. He had seen her before. He had seen her become a cat on a pavement two years ago, and he knew her face from the last stage of that before the fur, and he looked at her now and she looked at him, and neither of them said anything about it.

"Mr Potter."

"Yes."

"I am Professor McGonagall. I teach Transfiguration at Hogwarts. The Headmaster tells me you have read the first three years of the subject and would like to be taught it. He also tells me a number of other things, which I have decided to see for myself." She sat down in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's without being asked, and took her hat off, and put it on her knee. "Mrs Figg. Thank you for having me."

"You're welcome, Professor. There's tea when you want it."

"Later, I think." She turned back to Harry. "Tell me what Transfiguration is."

He told her. He gave her the first-year book's sentence and then the third-year book's longer one, and then, because she had not stopped looking at him, what he thought the difference between them was. She asked him what the book meant by a thing's nature. He told her. She asked him why it was harder to change a large thing than a small one, and he gave her the book's answer, which was about mass, and she said, "That is what the book says. It is not quite right," and told him why, which took some minutes and was the first time anyone had told him a book was wrong about magic, and he listened to all of it and asked one question at the end, and she answered that too.

Then she took a matchstick out of the pocket of her cloak and put it on the low table between them.

"The first-year exercise. A match into a needle. You will have read the instructions. Most students take several weeks to get a point on it. Show me what you do."

He looked at the match. He had read the instructions, and the instructions said to hold the needle clearly in the mind, and he had been holding things clearly in his mind since before he had a mind to speak of, and he had besides seen a woman's face narrow to a cat's at a pace where he could watch the bones go, so that he knew what one thing becoming another looked like from the inside of the second it took. He saw the needle. He saw the whole of it, the length and the eye and the point and the colour of the steel in Mrs Figg's lamplight, and he pointed his wand, which was too long for his arm, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say for this one, and the match was a needle.

It did not go through stages. It had been a match and it was a needle, and it lay on the low table with the lamp on it.

Professor McGonagall did not move for a moment. Then she picked up the needle and turned it in her fingers, and looked at the point, and the eye, and ran her thumb down the length of it, and put it down.

"Again," she said, and put another match beside it.

He did it again. She picked that one up as well.

"Mrs Figg," she said, "I will have that tea now, if you please."

Mrs Figg went to the kitchen. Professor McGonagall sat with the two needles in her hand and looked at Harry for some time, and when she spoke her voice was the same as before, dry and exact, and he could not have said from it what she was thinking.

"That is not a first attempt. I do not mean that you have practised. I mean that a first attempt does not look like that. A first attempt is a match with a silver point, or a needle with a wooden end, because the student cannot hold the whole of the needle at once and the magic does what it is given." She put the needles in her pocket. "You held the whole of it."

"Yes."

"Do you know how?"

"I can see things. All of a thing, for as long as I want. I don't have to hurry." He had decided what he would say to her about that and he said it. "The Headmaster knows how. He asked me to keep it to the three of us."

"Then I shall not ask." She looked at the Transfiguration book on his knees. "You will not attempt anything living. Not a beetle, not a mouse, not one of Mrs Figg's cats. You will not attempt anything on yourself or on any other person. You will not attempt anything you have not read the whole chapter on, including the part about what goes wrong. Those are not the Headmaster's conditions. They are mine, and if I find they have been broken I shall not come again. Do I have your word?"

"Yes."

"Good. I will come on Thursdays, once a fortnight, at this hour. Read the fourth year before I come again, and the fifth."

---

Remus Lupin was Dumbledore's idea, and Dumbledore told Harry about him a week before he came, in the chair by the fire, in order.

He was a friend of James and Lily's from school, one of three, and the only one of the three who was neither dead nor in Azkaban. He was twenty-four. He was poor, and had been out of work more than in it, and the reason for that was something Harry needed to know before he met him and not after: when Remus was a small boy he had been bitten by a werewolf, and had been one since. One night in the month, at the full moon, he became something dangerous, and he shut himself away for it and was ill for a day on either side. The rest of the month he was a man like any other, and a kind one, and very good at explaining things. Most wizards would not have him in the house. Dumbledore would, and had at school, and proposed to have him here, on weekdays, as the person who sat beside Harry while he used the wand, and taught him what the books left out, and answered his questions about his parents, which Dumbledore had been answering a little at a time and which Remus could answer better.

"He will not come on the day of the moon or the day after. Arabella knows and has agreed. I am telling you so that you know what the gaps are, and so that you hear it from me and not from the Prophet, which has opinions about werewolves."

"Does he want to come?"

"Very much."

He came on a Monday in April, at nine, by the back door, in a Muggle overcoat with the hem gone and robes under it that had been mended at the elbows more than once. He was thin, and younger than Harry had pictured from twenty-four, and he stood in the kitchen doorway and looked at Harry on the hearthrug for a long time without saying anything, and Harry looked back and waited, which he was good at.

"You've got her eyes," Lupin said. "I knew that. Everyone says it. It's different seeing it."

"Everyone says I've got his hair."

"You have. It did that at eleven and it was still doing it at twenty-one." He came in and sat down, not in Dumbledore's chair but on the floor, with his back against it, so that his eyes were level with Harry's. "I'm Remus. I last saw you when you were about a year old. You were sick on me." He looked at the stack of books by the hearth, and at the wand on top of them, and then back at Harry. "Professor Dumbledore says I'm to sit with you while you practise, and teach you what I can, and answer what you ask. I thought we might start with the last one. What do you want to know?"

Harry had a list. He had had it since September, and had given parts of it to Dumbledore a little at a time and kept the parts that Dumbledore could not have known, and he began at the top.

What his mother said when she was angry. What his father did on a Sunday. Whether they had read much, and what. Whether his mother had liked her sister. Whether his father had been afraid of anything. What the house at Godric's Hollow had looked like before the roof came off, because he remembered only the one room. Whether there had been a cat, because he remembered a cat.

Lupin answered all of it. Some of it he answered at once and some of it he had to go and find, sitting with his hands round his knees looking at the gas fire, and once he stopped in the middle of an answer and did not go on for a while, and Harry waited. There had been a cat. It had been called something stupid and Lupin could not for the moment remember what. His mother, when she was angry, went very quiet and said people's full names. His father on Sundays played a game with a ball that Lupin described and Harry recognised from the Prophet, in the garden, badly, alone, because nobody would play with him, and then came in and read the paper aloud to whoever would listen, doing the voices. They had both read a great deal. Lily had liked her sister and had not understood what had gone wrong between them, and had stopped trying to mend it a year or so before she died, and had minded that. James had been afraid of very little and should have been afraid of more.

It took the whole morning. Mrs Figg brought tea at eleven and went away again. Harry sat on the hearthrug and listened, and found, somewhere in the middle of it, that he was not doing anything else while he listened. He always did something else. There was so much room in a sentence, when someone else was saying it, that he had never in his life not used the room, and he sat and let Lupin's sentences come one after another at their own pace and did nothing in the gaps but wait for the next one.

---

Lupin came every weekday after that except the two around the moon, and the work changed.

He sat beside Harry at Mrs Figg's kitchen table while Harry went through the second-year charms and then the third, and he did not teach them, because the books taught them and Harry had read the books, but he watched, and after a week of watching he said what he had seen.

"You get it right the first time if you can see what it's supposed to do. I've never seen anyone do that. You get it wrong when the wand movement wants a bigger hand than yours, and then you get it right about a fortnight later when your hand catches up, and nothing I say makes any difference to either." He turned a teacup round on the table. "So I'm not much use to you for the spells. I can tell you what they're for. The book tells you what the Softening Charm does. It doesn't tell you that everyone uses it on their boots in winter, or that if you put it on a floor somebody will fall over, or that Filch has a list of people who've done that going back forty years."

"Who's Filch?"

Lupin told him who Filch was. He told him, over the next weeks, a great deal of that kind of thing, which was in no book: how a wizarding house was run, what people did for a living and how they got the jobs, what the Ministry was for and what it actually did, what a Howler was, why nobody said the name, what it had been like at school and what it had been like after. Harry asked questions and Lupin answered them, and some of the answers were short and some took an afternoon, and Harry found that the afternoons were the better use of the time, which surprised him, since an afternoon was a very long time.

Lupin also took him to the library on the High Street, when Mrs Figg's knees were bad, and stood inside the door the first time looking at the shelves the way Harry had, and said, "They just let you take them?" and Mrs Dennison, who had been told to expect him, said that they did if you brought them back.

---

In the first week of May, Harry asked about the potion.

He had read about it in a book from the crate, a paragraph near the end of a chapter on recent work, and he had read about it again in the Prophet, in a piece about the man who had made it. It did not cure anything. It let a werewolf keep his mind through the night of the moon, so that he was an animal in a locked room and not a danger, and it had to be taken every day for a week before, and it was new, and it was expensive, and the book said so in those words.

"Do you take it?"

Lupin did not answer at once. "No."

"Why not?"

"Because a month of it costs more than I earn in three. And I've been earning for about a fortnight."

Harry thought about that for the length of time it took Lupin to pick his cup up, which was long enough.

"I've got five houses of money and nothing to spend it on but books," he said. "I'd like it to be part of what you're paid. For teaching me."

"No."

"Why not?"

Lupin put the cup down. He looked at Harry for a while, and Harry could see him looking for the way to say it, and let him.

"Because you're three," he said at last, "and I'm meant to be the one looking after you. And because it's your parents' money, and your father would have offered the same thing in the same words, and I said no to him as well."

"Did he mind?"

"Yes."

"I'll mind too. I don't see why that should settle it." Harry had thought about this and said it plainly, because plainly was the only way he had of saying things. "You're hurt once a month and you don't have to be. I've got the money. It's what the money is for."

Lupin did not say yes that day. He said he would think about it, and he came back on the Monday and said that he had spoken to Dumbledore, and that Dumbledore had said it was Harry's money and that he would have approved the spending of it on this before Harry had finished asking, and that if Remus wanted to call it a wage he could call it a wage and Dumbledore would draw up the paper. So it was a wage. The potion came by owl from an apothecary in London in a stoppered flask, a week before the May moon, and Lupin took it at the kitchen table every morning making a face, and went away for his two days, and came back on the Thursday less grey than Harry had ever seen him, and did not say anything about it, and neither did Harry.

---

The quill was Harry's own idea and it did not work.

His letters to Dumbledore were the slowest thing he did. He wrote one a week, in the fortnight between visits, with what he had read and what he wanted to ask, and they took him an hour each in pencil, his hand going at the pace hands went, with his mind sitting inside each letter waiting for the stroke to finish. He had a wand now and a book of charms, and it seemed to him that a quill could be made to go faster than a hand.

He tried the Levitation Charm first. He floated a quill above a sheet of paper and steered it, seeing each letter before the quill made it, and the quill made the letters, and made them well, in a better hand than his. It did not make them faster. He could see the next letter as soon as he liked, but the quill went where he saw it at the pace the charm moved things, and the pace the charm moved things was the pace of a hand. He tried seeing a whole word at once. The quill wrote the word, in one movement, and then stopped and waited for the next, and he could not give it the next until the first was done, and he had gained a little and not much.

So he tried two charms. There was a charm in the third-year book for setting an object moving along a path, and he put that on the quill with the Levitation Charm already on it, and saw the path, which was a line of writing, and let go.

The quill wrote the first letter. Then it wrote it again. It wrote it a third time, in the same place, and went on writing it, pressing harder each time, until the paper was through and the nib was scoring the table, and Harry said the word that ended charms and it stopped and fell over, and there was a hole in Mrs Figg's tablecloth the shape of a capital D.

Lupin, who had watched the whole thing without moving, picked up the quill.

"Everybody tries that at school," he said. "Fourth year, mostly, and mostly on a quill. Two charms on the one thing pull against each other and you get something neither of them meant." He put the quill down. "The books don't say so because the books assume you'll learn one thing at a time."

"Does anyone do it on purpose? Put two together."

"Some. Not many. The people who make things for a living, and it takes them years, and it goes wrong on them too." He looked at the hole in the tablecloth. "I'd try the writing another way, if I were you. I don't know what way."

Harry did not know either, yet. He paid Mrs Figg for the tablecloth out of the money in the vault, which Dumbledore said was a correct use of it, and went back to the pencil, and kept the quill with the other things he did not understand.

---

Dumbledore came on the last Tuesday in June and said that the Dursleys had agreed.

Harry would spend the first three weeks of July at Privet Drive, all day and every day, as he had not since September, and that was the condition and Dumbledore would not move on it. Then, in the last week of July, Lupin would come for him, and he would go to the school for the rest of the summer, while there were no students in it, and sleep in a room there, and eat with the staff who stayed, and have the library.

"All of it?"

"The part of it that is not kept behind a grille, which is most of it. You will not go behind the grille. Madam Pince, who keeps the library, has been told to expect you. She is not pleased about it. She is not pleased about most things, and you will get used to her." Dumbledore took a sherbet lemon from the bag and held the bag out. "Eleven thousand books, Harry. I am told you have done the sum."

He had done it in September, on the hearthrug, with the chapter open in front of him. At the pace his eyes went, with a book a little over an hour and the days as long as he could make them, it came to something between three years and four.

"Yes," he said. "It's a long time."
