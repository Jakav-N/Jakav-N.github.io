# Chapter Six

*March to June 1984*

Professor McGonagall came on a Thursday evening in March, after her last class, through the gas fire.

Mrs Figg had been warned. She had moved the hearthrug back and shut the cats in the kitchen, and Harry sat on the settee with the second-year Transfiguration book on his knees while the fire turned green and a tall woman stepped out of it, brushing soot from a dark green cloak. He knew her. He had watched her become a cat on a pavement two years before, and her face now was the face from the last stage before the fur. She looked at him. Neither of them mentioned it.

"Mr Potter."

"Yes."

"I'm Professor McGonagall. I teach Transfiguration at Hogwarts. The Headmaster tells me you've read three years of it and want to be taught. He's told me a good deal else besides, and I'd rather see for myself." She sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's without being asked, took off her hat and laid it on her knee. "Mrs Figg. Thank you for having me."

"You're welcome, Professor. There's tea when you want it."

"Later, I think." To Harry: "Tell me what Transfiguration is."

He gave her the first-year book's sentence, then the third-year book's longer one, and then, since she was still looking at him, what he thought the difference between them was. She asked what the book meant by a thing's nature. She asked why a large object was harder to change than a small one. He gave her the book's answer, which was about mass. "That's what the book says," she said. "It's not quite right," and she told him why. It took several minutes. Nobody had ever told him before that a book about magic was wrong, and he listened to the end and asked one question, which she answered.

Then she took a matchstick from her cloak and laid it on the low table.

"This is the first-year exercise. Match into needle. You'll have read how it's done. Most of my first-years take a few weeks to get so much as a point on it. Let's see what you do."

The instructions said to hold the needle clearly in the mind. He had besides watched a woman's face narrow into a cat's at a speed where he could follow the bones, so he knew what one thing becoming another looked like from inside the second it took. He saw the needle whole: its length, the eye, the point, the colour of steel in Mrs Figg's lamplight. The book gave no word for this, so he said none. He pointed the wand, which was too long for his arm, and the match was a needle.

There were no stages. One moment a match lay under the lamp; the next, a needle.

Professor McGonagall did not move. Then she picked the needle up, turned it in her fingers, examined the point and the eye, ran her thumb along it, and set it down.

"Again," she said, and placed a second match.

He did it again. She picked that one up too.

"Mrs Figg, I will have that tea now, if you please."

Mrs Figg went to the kitchen. Professor McGonagall sat with the two needles in her palm and studied Harry. When she spoke her voice was as dry and exact as before, and he could not have told from it what she thought.

"That wasn't a first attempt." She turned the needles over in her palm. "I don't mean you've been practising. I mean it didn't look like one. When a student does this for the first time I get a match with a silver point, or a needle with a wooden end, because they can't hold the whole needle in their head at once, and the magic does half the job and stops." She put the needles in her pocket. "You held all of it."

"Yes."

"Do you know how?"

"I can see things. All of a thing, for as long as I want. I don't have to hurry." He had decided beforehand what he would tell her about that. "The Headmaster knows how. He asked me to keep it to the three of us."

"Then I shan't ask." Her eyes went to the book on his knees. "Three conditions, and they're mine, not the Headmaster's. Nothing living. Not a beetle, not a mouse, not one of Mrs Figg's cats. Nothing on yourself, or on anybody else. And you read the whole chapter first, every time, right through to what happens when it goes wrong. If I find you've broken any of them, I don't come back. Do I have your word?"

"Yes."

"Good. Thursdays, then, every other week, about this time. Read the fourth year before I'm back. And the fifth."

---

Dumbledore came on the first Tuesday in March with an answer about the gold.

He had spoken to the goblins, who did not object to a wizard's gold leaving them in the form of pounds, provided it left as pounds and did not come back as gold, and who had charged him a fee for the information. He had spoken to a man called Hadley.

"He was at Hogwarts some years after Remus Lupin, whom you'll meet next month. Muggle-born. He went back to his father's world after school and qualified as a solicitor, and he's one of perhaps a dozen people in the country who understand both kinds of money. I've asked him to hold a sum for you in trust, in the Muggle way, and to put it where you ask, within reason. He said he'd need to meet you. I said he would find you unusual."

"How much?"

"Two thousand Galleons. Ten thousand pounds at the goblins' rate; a tenth of what's in the vault, more or less. If it grows, we may talk about more. If it doesn't, you'll have learned something at a price I can justify to myself."

"If it grows, I'd like it to be half."

"If it grows, you may ask me for half, and I shall want to see the figures, and we'll talk." Dumbledore took a sherbet lemon. "I read your two pages. They were very clear. You might have left out the part where you explained to me what a bank was."

"I didn't know what you knew."

"No. That was fair."

Mr Hadley came to Wisteria Walk the following week, in a grey suit with a briefcase, and sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's with his knees together. Harry asked him, before anything else, how a child could own things in the Muggle world, and be paid, and pay others. Mr Hadley said that a child could not, and that this was what trustees were for, and explained trusts for a quarter of an hour while Harry listened and asked four questions, the last of which Mr Hadley said he would have to look up. Then Mr Hadley asked what Harry wanted done with ten thousand pounds.

Harry told him. He had read the blue economics textbook in December and thought about it since, and he had read the share prices on the television every night for two years, and what he wanted was dull: a spread of large companies that paid dividends, the dividends put back in, nothing sold without a reason, and the reasons written down. Mr Hadley wrote this on a pad. He asked where Harry had learned the word dividend. Harry told him. Mr Hadley wrote that down too, put his pen away, looked at Harry for a while, and said that he would send the papers for Professor Dumbledore to sign.

---

A week before Remus Lupin first came, Dumbledore sat in the chair by the fire and told Harry about him.

"I've found you someone. His name is Remus Lupin. He was at school with your parents, one of your father's three close friends, and he's the only one of the three who isn't dead or in Azkaban. He's twenty-four. He's very poor, and he's been out of work more than in it, and you ought to know why before you meet him rather than after."

Harry waited.

"When he was a small boy he was bitten by a werewolf. He's been one ever since. One night a month, at the full moon, he turns into a wolf, and on that night he's dangerous, so he locks himself away. He's ill for a day on either side of it. The rest of the month he's a man like anyone else. A kind one, as it happens, and better at explaining things than most people I know. Most wizards wouldn't have him in the house. I had him at school for seven years, and I'd like to have him here, on weekdays. He'd sit with you while you use the wand. He'd tell you the things that aren't in the books. And he can answer your questions about your mother and father a good deal better than I can."

"When does he not come?"

"The day of the moon and the day after. Arabella knows, and she's agreed." Dumbledore turned his cup. "I wanted you to hear it from me, not from the Prophet. The Prophet has opinions about werewolves."

"Does he want to come?"

"Very much."

He came on a Monday in April, at nine, by the back door, in a Muggle overcoat with the hem gone and, under it, robes mended at the elbows more than once. He was thin and looked younger than twenty-four. He stopped in the kitchen doorway and looked at Harry on the hearthrug without speaking, and Harry looked back and waited.

"You've got her eyes," Lupin said at last. "I knew that. Everyone says so. It's different, seeing it."

"Everyone says I've got his hair."

"You have." Lupin came in. He did not take Dumbledore's chair; he sat on the floor with his back against it, so that his eyes were level with Harry's. "I'm Remus. I last saw you when you were about a year old. You were sick on me." He glanced at the stack of books by the hearth and the wand lying on top. "Professor Dumbledore says I'm to sit with you while you practise, teach you what I can, and answer whatever you ask. I thought we'd start with the last one. What do you want to know?"

Harry had a list. He had been keeping it since September, and he had given Dumbledore the questions Dumbledore could answer and saved the rest. He began at the top.

What his mother said when she was angry. What his father did on a Sunday. Whether they had read much, and what. Whether his mother had liked her sister. Whether his father had been afraid of anything. What the house at Godric's Hollow had looked like before the roof came off, since he remembered only one room of it. Whether there had been a cat, because he remembered a cat.

Lupin answered everything. Some answers came at once; for others he had to go and find them, sitting with his arms round his knees and his eyes on the gas fire, and once he stopped in the middle of one and was quiet for a while, and Harry waited. There had been a cat, with a stupid name that Lupin could not for the moment recall. Lily, angry, went very quiet and used people's full names. On Sundays James played a game with a ball that Harry recognised from the Prophet, alone in the garden because nobody would play with him, badly, and then came in and read the newspaper aloud to anyone who would listen, doing the voices. They had both read a great deal. Lily had been fond of her sister and had never understood what went wrong between them; about a year before she died she had stopped trying to mend it, and minded. James had been afraid of very little.

It took the whole morning. Mrs Figg brought tea at eleven and went away again. Somewhere in the middle of it Harry noticed that he was not doing anything else while he listened. There was always room in a sentence when someone else was saying it, and he had always used the room. Now he let Lupin's sentences arrive at their own pace, one after another, and did nothing in the gaps but wait for the next.

---

After that Lupin came every weekday except the two around the moon, and the work changed.

He sat beside Harry at Mrs Figg's kitchen table while Harry worked through the second-year charms and then the third. He did not teach them; the books did that, and Harry had read the books. He watched, and after the first week he kept the counts, on paper, with a watch Dumbledore had lent, because Harry had explained about the twenties and Lupin had said that a man with a pencil could do that part and leave Harry's attention for the casting. After a week of watching he said what he had seen.

"You get a spell right first time if you can see what it's meant to do. I've never watched anyone do that. And you get it wrong when the wand movement wants a bigger hand than you've got, and then there's nothing to be done about it, by you or me, until your hand's grown. Some of these you won't manage for years, whatever you do." He turned his teacup on the table. "So I'm not much use to you on the spells themselves. What I can tell you is what they're for. The book says what the Softening Charm does. It doesn't say that everyone puts it on their boots in winter, or that if you put it on a floor somebody will fall over."

"Who's Filch?"

Lupin explained who Filch was. Over the following weeks he explained a great deal else of that kind, none of it in any book: how a wizarding household ran, what people did for a living and how they came by the work, what the Ministry was for and what it actually did, what a Howler was, why nobody said Voldemort's name aloud, what school had been like and what had come after. Harry asked and Lupin answered, sometimes in a sentence and sometimes over a whole afternoon.

When Mrs Figg's knees were bad, Lupin took him to the library instead. The first time, he stood inside the door looking at the shelves the way Harry once had. "They just let you take them?" he said, and Mrs Dennison, who had been told to expect him, said that they did.

Lupin could also carry, and shop, and address an owl, and Harry had been waiting since the autumn for someone who could do all three.

Mrs Figg bred cats, Kneazle crosses, and sold them by owl, and had done for thirty years. Her customers wrote to her from all over the country about kittens and tempers and sent their Galleons in little bags, and she wrote back in a careful hand. Harry had read the correspondence in October, with her permission, because it was the only picture he had of what ordinary wizards wanted and what they paid for it. One letter in four, somewhere in the business about the cat, mentioned a Muggle thing: a torch somebody's cousin had brought back, a pen that needed no inkwell, a pair of trousers with a zip. They mentioned these the way his uncle mentioned a new car. He had kept that since October, with the one thing it needed written against it, which was a pair of hands.

So on the second Tuesday in April he asked Mrs Figg whether she would put a sheet in with her next letters.

"Saying what?"

"That you can get Muggle things, and what they cost. Pens that don't need ink. Torches, with the batteries. Sticky tape. Flasks that keep tea hot all day. Notebooks with lines in. Nothing with a plug and nothing anyone's enchanted, so there's nothing for the Ministry to mind; Remus has checked. I'd buy the things in the High Street with my own money, Remus would wrap them, and you'd send them with the kittens' letters. I'd pay you for the owls and the trouble, and whatever's left after that is for the list."

Mrs Figg looked at him over her cup for a long moment. "What list?"

"The one I keep. Of what wants doing."

"And what does a pen that doesn't need ink sell for, to a wizard?"

"I don't know yet. That's why I'd start with your customers. They'll tell me by what they buy."

She thought about it until the cup was empty. Then she said that she had been sending kittens by owl for thirty years and supposed she could send a pen, and that she would want it in writing what was hers and what was his, because she had seen what happened in families over less. Harry wrote it that evening, a page in pencil, which took him most of the evening, and she read it twice and signed it, and Mr Tibbles sat on it.

The first sheet went out with eleven letters in the third week of April, priced by guess: a pen at two Sickles, a torch at a Galleon with its batteries, a roll of tape at a Sickle. The pen had cost twelve pence in the High Street. Nine of the eleven wrote back with orders, and one of them ordered twelve pens and asked whether there were other colours. By June the sheet went to every customer Mrs Figg had and to the people they had told. Lupin spent Monday mornings in the High Street with a shopping list and Monday afternoons at Mrs Figg's table wrapping parcels, and the owls went out heavy, and the Galleons came back in little bags that Mrs Figg kept in a biscuit tin.

Harry asked Dumbledore, in the same month, for two afternoons a week of Lupin's time that were his and not the school's: not teaching, finding things out. There were questions whose answers were in the Ministry's reading room or in somebody's shop, places a man could walk into and a boy of three could not. How many names the Ministry had on its register of werewolves, and whether anyone believed it. Who owned the Prophet. What a shop in Diagon Alley paid in rent at the good end and the bad. He would pay for the afternoons out of the vault, on top of the wage. Dumbledore said that Remus was to tell him what he had been asked, since a young man asking at the Ministry about werewolves would be noticed, and agreed. Lupin's first report came in the second week of May, four pages in his own hand: two hundred and eleven names on the register, believed by nobody who knew anything, Lupin's own not among them; the Prophet owned by a family called Cuffe for a century; forty Galleons a month for a small shop at the wrong end of the Alley and two hundred at the right. Harry did not believe the two hundred and eleven either. He put the four pages with the rest of what he knew and noticed that the biscuit tin had taken forty Galleons in May.

---

In the first week of May, Harry asked about the potion.

He had read of it in a book from the crate, in a paragraph at the end of a chapter on recent work, and again in the Prophet, in a piece about the man who had invented it. It cured nothing. It let a werewolf keep his mind through the night of the moon, so that he was an animal in a locked room rather than a danger to anyone. It had to be taken every day for the week before. It was new, and it was expensive; the book said so in those words.

"Do you take it?"

Lupin did not answer at once. "No."

"Why not?"

"Because a month of it costs more than I earn in three. And I've been earning for about a fortnight."

"I've got five houses of money and nothing to spend it on but books. I'd like it to be part of what you're paid, for teaching me."

"No."

"Why not?"

Lupin set his cup down. He looked at Harry while he found the words, and Harry let him.

"Because you're three," he said eventually, "and I'm supposed to be the one looking after you. And because it's your parents' money. Your father offered me the same thing in nearly the same words, and I said no to him as well."

"Did he mind?"

"Yes."

"I'll mind too. I don't see why that should settle it. You're hurt once a month and you don't have to be. I've got the money. That's what the money is for."

Lupin did not say yes that day. He said he would think about it. On the Monday he came back and said he had spoken to Dumbledore, and that Dumbledore had said the money was Harry's and that he would have approved this use of it before Harry finished asking; and if Remus preferred to call it a wage, Dumbledore would draw up the paper. So it was a wage. The potion came by owl from an apothecary in London, in a stoppered flask, a week before the May moon. Lupin drank it at the kitchen table each morning, pulling a face, went away for his two days, and came back on the Thursday less grey than Harry had ever seen him. Neither of them said anything about that.

---

The quill was Harry's own idea, and it did not work.

The slowest thing he did was write to Dumbledore. One letter a week, between visits, with what he had read and what he wanted to ask: an hour each, in pencil, his hand moving at the pace hands move while his mind sat inside every letter waiting for the stroke to finish. He had a wand now and a book of charms. A quill, he thought, could surely be made to go faster than a hand.

He tried the Levitation Charm first. A quill hung over a sheet of paper, and he steered it, seeing each letter before the quill made it. The quill made them, and in a better hand than his own; but it made them no faster. He could see the next letter as soon as he liked. The quill still went where he saw it at the pace the charm moved things, and that was the pace of a hand. He tried seeing a whole word. The quill wrote the word in one movement, stopped, and waited for the next, which he could not give it until the first was done. A little gained, not much.

So he tried two charms. The third-year book had one for setting an object moving along a path. He laid it on the quill over the Levitation Charm, saw the path as a line of writing, and let go.

The quill wrote the first letter. Then it wrote the same letter again, in the same place, and again, pressing harder each time, until the nib had gone through the paper and was scoring the table. Harry said the word that ends charms. The quill stopped and fell over. There was a hole in Mrs Figg's tablecloth the shape of a capital D.

Lupin had watched the whole thing without moving. He picked up the quill.

"Everybody tries that at school. Fourth year, mostly, and mostly on a quill. Two charms on the one object pull against each other, and you get something neither of them meant." He put the quill down. "The books don't say so, because the books assume you'll learn one thing at a time."

"Does anyone do it on purpose? Two together?"

"Some. The people who make things for a living. It takes them years, and it goes wrong on them too." Lupin looked at the hole. "I'd try the writing another way, if I were you. I don't know what way."

Harry did not know either, yet. He paid Mrs Figg for the tablecloth out of the vault, which Dumbledore called a correct use of the money, and went back to the pencil. There was a book he wanted to write, and could not at a pencil's pace: the mathematics sixteen-year-olds were examined on, laid out so that someone could learn it from the book alone, with the places people went wrong marked. He had read eleven revision books in London in March and they were all bad in the same ways. Two hundred pages in pencil was seventy hours of his hand. He put it on the list against the day the ink was faster.

He came at it again in June from the other side. The quill had been the wrong thing to move. What he wanted on the page was ink, and ink was a small, light thing, and small light things had been coming to him along paths he saw since the night of the sock. So he set a sheet of paper on the kitchen table, uncorked the bottle, and saw a word on the page: the ink lifting from the bottle in a thread, crossing the inch of air, and lying down on the paper in the shape of his own name, every letter of it, as he had seen the bead cross the room.

The ink came. It came in a thread as he had seen it, and lay down, and the result was a blot the size of a penny with a tail on it.

"That's new," said Lupin, who had sat up.

"It's not finished." Harry looked at the blot. He had seen the word; what he had not seen was the ink as ink, which did not stay where it was put but spread, and ran where the paper was damp, and wanted to be a drop rather than a line. The next attempt he saw the ink spread as well, and allowed for it, and got a letter that could be read and three that could not. Lupin stopped reading his own book and watched. By the end of the afternoon there was a word on the paper, blurred at the edges and too heavy in the downstrokes, and it was Harry, and it had taken no longer to arrive than the bead had taken to cross the room.

It was slower than a pencil, that first day, because he could do a word and then had to look at what the ink had done before he saw the next. Lupin said the pencil was quicker and he should stick to it. Harry said he would, for now.

---

Dumbledore came on the last Tuesday in June and said that the Dursleys had agreed.

Harry would spend the first three weeks of July at Privet Drive, all day and every day, as he had not since September. That was the condition, and Dumbledore would not move on it. In the last week of July Lupin would come for him, and he would go to the school for the rest of the summer, while there were no students in it. He would sleep in a room there, eat with the staff who stayed, and have the library.

"All of it?"

"Everything that isn't behind the grille, which is nearly all of it. The grille you will leave alone. Madam Pince keeps the library, and I've told her to expect you; I won't pretend she was delighted. She is very protective of her books, and she will be slow to come round. Be patient with her and keep your hands clean, and she will." Dumbledore took a sherbet lemon from the bag and held the bag out. "Eleven thousand books, Harry. I'm told you've done the sum."

He had done it in September, on the hearthrug, with the chapter open in front of him. At the pace his eyes went, with a book a little over an hour and the days as long as he could make them, it came to something between three years and four.

"Yes," he said. "It's a long time."
