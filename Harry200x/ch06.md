# Chapter Six

*March to June 1984*

Professor McGonagall came on a Thursday evening in March, after her last class, through the gas fire.

Mrs Figg had been warned. She had moved the hearthrug back and shut the cats in the kitchen, and Harry sat on the settee with the second-year Transfiguration book on his knees while the fire turned green and a tall woman stepped out of it, brushing soot from a dark green cloak. He knew her. He had watched her become a cat on a pavement two years before, and her face now was the face from the last stage before the fur. She looked at him. Neither of them mentioned it.

"Mr Potter."

"Yes."

"I'm Professor McGonagall. I teach Transfiguration at Hogwarts. The Headmaster tells me you've read three years of it and want to be taught. He's told me a good deal else besides, and I'd rather see for myself." She sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's without being asked, took off her hat and laid it on her knee. "Mrs Figg. Thank you for having me."

"You're welcome, Professor. There's tea when you want it."

"Later, I think." To Harry: "Tell me what Transfiguration is."

He gave her the first-year book's sentence, then the third-year book's longer one, and then, since she was still looking at him, what he thought the difference between them was. She asked what the book meant by a thing's nature. She asked why a large object was harder to change than a small one. He gave her the book's answer, which was about mass. "That's what the book says," she said. "It's not quite right," and she told him why. It took several minutes. Nobody had ever told him before that a book about magic was wrong, and he listened to the end and asked one question, which she answered.

Then she took a matchstick from her cloak and laid it on the low table.

"This is the first-year exercise. Match into needle. You'll have read how it's done. Most of my first-years take a few weeks to get so much as a point on it. Let's see what you do."

The instructions said to hold the needle clearly in the mind. He had besides watched a woman's face narrow into a cat's at a speed where he could follow the bones, so he knew what one thing becoming another looked like from inside the second it took. He saw the needle whole: its length, the eye, the point, the colour of steel in Mrs Figg's lamplight. The book gave no word for this, so he said none. He pointed the wand, which was too long for his arm, and the match was a needle.

There were no stages. One moment a match lay under the lamp; the next, a needle.

Professor McGonagall did not move. Then she picked the needle up, turned it in her fingers, examined the point and the eye, ran her thumb along it, and set it down.

"Again," she said, and placed a second match.

He did it again. She picked that one up too.

"Mrs Figg, I will have that tea now, if you please."

Mrs Figg went to the kitchen. Professor McGonagall sat with the two needles in her palm and studied Harry. When she spoke her voice was as dry and exact as before, and he could not have told from it what she thought.

"That wasn't a first attempt." She turned the needles over in her palm. "I don't mean you've been practising. I mean it didn't look like one. When a student does this for the first time I get a match with a silver point, or a needle with a wooden end, because they can't hold the whole needle in their head at once, and the magic does half the job and stops." She put the needles in her pocket. "You held all of it."

"Yes."

"Do you know how?"

"I can see things. All of a thing, for as long as I want. I don't have to hurry." He had decided beforehand what he would tell her about that. "The Headmaster knows how. He asked me to keep it to the three of us."

"Then I shan't ask." Her eyes went to the book on his knees. "Three conditions, and they're mine, not the Headmaster's. Nothing living. Not a beetle, not a mouse, not one of Mrs Figg's cats. Nothing on yourself, or on anybody else. And nothing you haven't read the whole chapter on, including the part about what goes wrong. If I find you've broken any of them, I don't come back. Do I have your word?"

"Yes."

"Good. Thursdays, then, every other week, about this time. Read the fourth year before I'm back. And the fifth."

---

Remus Lupin was Dumbledore's idea. Dumbledore told Harry about him a week before he came, in the chair by the fire, in order.

Remus had been at school with James and Lily, one of a group of three friends; of the three he was the only one neither dead nor in Azkaban. He was twenty-four and poor, and had been out of work far more than in it. The reason belonged before the meeting, not after. As a small boy Remus had been bitten by a werewolf, and he had been a werewolf since. One night in the month, at the full moon, he became something dangerous; he locked himself away for it, and was ill for a day on either side. For the rest of the month he was a man like any other, a kind one, and unusually good at explaining things. Most wizards would not have him in the house. Dumbledore had kept him at school and proposed to have him here, on weekdays, as the person who sat beside Harry while he used the wand, taught him what the books left out, and answered his questions about his parents, which Remus could do better than Dumbledore could.

"He won't come on the day of the moon or the day after. Arabella knows, and she's agreed. I wanted you to hear it from me first, and not from the Prophet. The Prophet has opinions about werewolves."

"Does he want to come?"

"Very much."

He came on a Monday in April, at nine, by the back door, in a Muggle overcoat with the hem gone and, under it, robes mended at the elbows more than once. He was thin and looked younger than twenty-four. He stopped in the kitchen doorway and looked at Harry on the hearthrug without speaking, and Harry looked back and waited.

"You've got her eyes," Lupin said at last. "I knew that. Everyone says so. It's different, seeing it."

"Everyone says I've got his hair."

"You have." Lupin came in. He did not take Dumbledore's chair; he sat on the floor with his back against it, so that his eyes were level with Harry's. "I'm Remus. I last saw you when you were about a year old. You were sick on me." He glanced at the stack of books by the hearth and the wand lying on top. "Professor Dumbledore says I'm to sit with you while you practise, teach you what I can, and answer whatever you ask. I thought we'd start with the last one. What do you want to know?"

Harry had a list. He had kept it since September, giving Dumbledore the parts Dumbledore could answer and holding back the parts he could not, and he began at the top.

What his mother said when she was angry. What his father did on a Sunday. Whether they had read much, and what. Whether his mother had liked her sister. Whether his father had been afraid of anything. What the house at Godric's Hollow had looked like before the roof came off, since he remembered only one room of it. Whether there had been a cat, because he remembered a cat.

Lupin answered everything. Some answers came at once; for others he had to go and find them, sitting with his arms round his knees and his eyes on the gas fire, and once he stopped in the middle of one and was quiet for a while, and Harry waited. There had been a cat, with a stupid name that Lupin could not for the moment recall. Lily, angry, went very quiet and used people's full names. On Sundays James played a game with a ball that Harry recognised from the Prophet, alone in the garden because nobody would play with him, badly, and then came in and read the newspaper aloud to anyone who would listen, doing the voices. They had both read a great deal. Lily had been fond of her sister and had never understood what went wrong between them; about a year before she died she had stopped trying to mend it, and minded. James had been afraid of very little.

It took the whole morning. Mrs Figg brought tea at eleven and went away again. Somewhere in the middle of it Harry noticed that he was not doing anything else while he listened. There was always room in a sentence when someone else was saying it, and he had always used the room. Now he let Lupin's sentences arrive at their own pace, one after another, and did nothing in the gaps but wait for the next.

---

After that Lupin came every weekday except the two around the moon, and the work changed.

He sat beside Harry at Mrs Figg's kitchen table while Harry worked through the second-year charms and then the third. He did not teach them; the books did that, and Harry had read the books. He watched. After a week of watching he said what he had seen.

"You get a spell right first time if you can see what it's meant to do. I've never watched anyone do that. You get it wrong when the wand movement wants a bigger hand than yours, and about a fortnight later your hand catches up and you get it right, and nothing I say makes any difference either way." He turned his teacup on the table. "So I'm not much use to you on the spells themselves. What I can tell you is what they're for. The book says what the Softening Charm does. It doesn't say that everyone puts it on their boots in winter, or that if you put it on a floor somebody will fall over."

"Who's Filch?"

Lupin explained who Filch was. Over the following weeks he explained a great deal of that kind, none of it in any book: how a wizarding household ran, what people did for a living and how they came by the work, what the Ministry was for and what it actually did, what a Howler was, why nobody said the name, what school had been like and what had come after. Harry asked and Lupin answered. Some answers were short. Some took an afternoon, and Harry found, to his surprise, that the afternoons were the better use of the time.

When Mrs Figg's knees were bad, Lupin took him to the library instead. The first time, he stood inside the door looking at the shelves the way Harry once had. "They just let you take them?" he said, and Mrs Dennison, who had been told to expect him, said that they did.

---

In the first week of May, Harry asked about the potion.

He had read of it in a book from the crate, in a paragraph at the end of a chapter on recent work, and again in the Prophet, in a piece about the man who had invented it. It cured nothing. It let a werewolf keep his mind through the night of the moon, so that he was an animal in a locked room rather than a danger to anyone. It had to be taken every day for the week before. It was new, and it was expensive; the book said so in those words.

"Do you take it?"

Lupin did not answer at once. "No."

"Why not?"

"Because a month of it costs more than I earn in three. And I've been earning for about a fortnight."

"I've got five houses of money and nothing to spend it on but books. I'd like it to be part of what you're paid, for teaching me."

"No."

"Why not?"

Lupin set his cup down. He looked at Harry while he found the words, and Harry let him.

"Because you're three," he said eventually, "and I'm supposed to be the one looking after you. And because it's your parents' money. Your father offered me the same thing in nearly the same words, and I said no to him as well."

"Did he mind?"

"Yes."

"I'll mind too. I don't see why that should settle it. You're hurt once a month and you don't have to be. I've got the money. That's what the money is for."

Lupin did not say yes that day. He said he would think about it. On the Monday he came back and said he had spoken to Dumbledore, and that Dumbledore had said the money was Harry's and that he would have approved this use of it before Harry finished asking; and if Remus preferred to call it a wage, Dumbledore would draw up the paper. So it was a wage. The potion came by owl from an apothecary in London, in a stoppered flask, a week before the May moon. Lupin drank it at the kitchen table each morning, pulling a face, went away for his two days, and came back on the Thursday less grey than Harry had ever seen him. Neither of them said anything about that.

---

The quill was Harry's own idea, and it did not work.

The slowest thing he did was write to Dumbledore. One letter a week, between visits, with what he had read and what he wanted to ask: an hour each, in pencil, his hand moving at the pace hands move while his mind sat inside every letter waiting for the stroke to finish. He had a wand now and a book of charms. A quill, he thought, could surely be made to go faster than a hand.

He tried the Levitation Charm first. A quill hung over a sheet of paper, and he steered it, seeing each letter before the quill made it. The quill made them, and in a better hand than his own; but it made them no faster. He could see the next letter as soon as he liked. The quill still went where he saw it at the pace the charm moved things, and that was the pace of a hand. He tried seeing a whole word. The quill wrote the word in one movement, stopped, and waited for the next, which he could not give it until the first was done. A little gained, not much.

So he tried two charms. The third-year book had one for setting an object moving along a path. He laid it on the quill over the Levitation Charm, saw the path as a line of writing, and let go.

The quill wrote the first letter. Then it wrote the same letter again, in the same place, and again, pressing harder each time, until the nib had gone through the paper and was scoring the table. Harry said the word that ends charms. The quill stopped and fell over. There was a hole in Mrs Figg's tablecloth the shape of a capital D.

Lupin had watched the whole thing without moving. He picked up the quill.

"Everybody tries that at school. Fourth year, mostly, and mostly on a quill. Two charms on the one object pull against each other, and you get something neither of them meant." He put the quill down. "The books don't say so, because the books assume you'll learn one thing at a time."

"Does anyone do it on purpose? Two together?"

"Some. The people who make things for a living. It takes them years, and it goes wrong on them too." Lupin looked at the hole. "I'd try the writing another way, if I were you. I don't know what way."

Harry did not know either, yet. He paid Mrs Figg for the tablecloth out of the vault, which Dumbledore called a correct use of the money, and went back to the pencil.

---

Dumbledore came on the last Tuesday in June and said that the Dursleys had agreed.

Harry would spend the first three weeks of July at Privet Drive, all day and every day, as he had not since September. That was the condition, and Dumbledore would not move on it. In the last week of July Lupin would come for him, and he would go to the school for the rest of the summer, while there were no students in it. He would sleep in a room there, eat with the staff who stayed, and have the library.

"All of it?"

"Everything that isn't behind the grille, which is most of it. You stay out from behind the grille. Madam Pince keeps the library, and she's been told you're coming. She's not pleased. She's not pleased about most things. You'll get used to her." Dumbledore took a sherbet lemon from the bag and held the bag out. "Eleven thousand books, Harry. I'm told you've done the sum."

He had done it in September, on the hearthrug, with the chapter open in front of him. At the pace his eyes went, with a book a little over an hour and the days as long as he could make them, it came to something between three years and four.

"Yes," he said. "It's a long time."
