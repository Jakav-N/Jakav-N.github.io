# Chapter Seven

*July to August 1984*

The three weeks at Privet Drive began on the first of July, a Sunday, and he spent the first of them finding out what the house was like with him in it all day.

It had been nine months. In that time he had been home for the evenings and the nights, which the house had got used to, and now he was there at breakfast and at lunch and through the long afternoons when his aunt had the ironing on and Dudley had the television. Nobody had said what he was to do with himself. He read the newspaper when his uncle had finished with it, and the local paper on Thursdays. Lupin had brought him twelve library books on the Saturday before, with Mrs Dennison's blessing and his aunt's silence, and they were gone by the Tuesday. After that there was the wireless, and the garden, and Dudley.

Dudley was four, five weeks older than Harry and nearly twice his weight. He had not had Harry about the place in the daytime since they were both in the pen, and he looked at him for most of the first morning as though he had come with the furniture. By the afternoon he had worked out that Harry would fetch things. By the second day he had worked out that Harry would read to him, which his mother did only at bedtime, and he brought a book across the carpet and sat down on Harry's feet and said, "Do that one," and Harry did that one. His aunt came in from the kitchen while it was happening, stood in the doorway with a tea towel in her hands, said nothing, and went out again.

His uncle came home at six and found the two of them on the carpet with the book between them.

"What's this?"

"Harry's reading," said Dudley. "He does the voices."

His uncle looked at Harry for a while. "Dinner," he said, to nobody in particular, and went to wash.

That was how the three weeks went. Harry read to Dudley when Dudley wanted it and fetched when Dudley wanted it and otherwise stayed out of the way. At night in the cupboard, with the door shut and his light going, he wrote: the fifth book, the economics that sixteen-year-olds sat, two hours a night in pencil, and it was the last thing he ever wrote in pencil. In the second week his aunt went down with a headache that kept her in her room for two days with the curtains drawn. Harry made Dudley's breakfast both mornings, which was cereal, and his own, and washed the bowls. Dudley, with nobody else to ask, asked what the shapes in his book were, and Harry began teaching him his letters. By the time his aunt came down on the third day, Dudley could find P and D and H anywhere on a page. He showed her. She looked at Harry over Dudley's head, said nothing, and did not stop it.

On the last evening, after Dudley was in bed, she stopped in the hall outside the cupboard with the landing light behind her.

"You're not to do anything in this house," she said. "Not in front of him. Whatever you do at hers."

"I won't."

She stood a moment longer, and then went up, and he heard her door.

---

Lupin came for him on the Monday with a bag over his shoulder and no car. They walked together to the end of Wisteria Walk, where Lupin stepped to the kerb and held out his wand arm at the empty road.

The bus arrived out of nowhere. It was purple and three decks high, and it stopped so hard that its windows rattled. A young man in a purple uniform leaned out of the door and began a speech about emergency transport for the stranded witch or wizard; Lupin cut it short with eleven Sickles. Inside there were beds instead of seats, brass bedsteads that slid about the floor as the bus moved, and a chandelier. The conductor looked at Harry's hat, then at Harry, and said nothing. Lupin chose a bed near the back, sat Harry on it, and took hold of the frame.

The bus did not so much drive as leap. It would stand in one street and then, with a bang, be standing in another, and in between the lamp-posts and pillar boxes of the first street flung themselves out of its way. Harry watched a pillar box bend aside and straighten again behind them, and a good many more between Surrey and the north. He was not sick. Lupin, holding the bed frame with both hands, looked at him once and seemed to decide not to ask.

They got down in the early afternoon on a road between hills, with a lake below them and a castle above, and the bus went off with a bang and left them in silence.

Hogwarts: A History had not said what the castle looked like from the road with the sun on it, or that there would be so much of it, towers behind towers, or that the lake would be that colour. He stood and looked.

"Yes," Lupin said after a while. "It does that to everyone. Come on. There's someone at the gate who's been waiting since breakfast."

Hagrid was at the gate. He was as big as Harry remembered and his beard was as black, and when he saw them coming up the road he made the sound Harry had last heard from him in a broken house in the dark.

"Yeh won't remember me," Hagrid said, when he could say anything.

"I do. You carried me out. You cried then as well."

Hagrid looked at Lupin. Lupin said, "He remembers a good deal, Hagrid. I did warn you." Hagrid sat down on the grass by the gatepost, which brought his face nearly to Harry's level, and looked at him for a long time, and then laughed, and wiped his face on his sleeve, and said that in that case Harry could help him with the pumpkins when he had a minute, because he had been meaning to get a word in before the school did.

---

Dumbledore met them on the steps and took them up through the castle himself.

The staircases were the first thing. One of them moved while they were on it, swinging out from its landing towards another with a grinding Harry could feel through his shoes. "They do that," said Dumbledore, and Harry watched the stone slide and said yes. The portraits were the second. He had read that they moved and had pictured the Prophet's photographs; what he saw, looking up at a knight who was looking down at him, was paint rearranging itself, every brushstroke of the face shifting to make the next expression, in the time it took Dumbledore to climb three steps. The knight told him it was rude to stare. Harry said he was sorry and went on staring.

His room was on the second floor, two doors from the hospital wing, with a bed and a desk and a window over the lake. Dumbledore had thought he might want the lake. Madam Pomfrey came in while he was still looking at it, introduced herself, sat him on the bed, looked in his eyes and ears, listened to his chest, and asked how he slept.

About ten hours, he said. Then he asked whether it had to be ten. He had read what the Muggle books said about sleep and found them vague on why, and ten hours was nearly half of everything.

"Yes, it has to be ten," said Madam Pomfrey. "You're four. Your body does its mending while you sleep and your brain does its filing, and there isn't a potion in this castle that will do either for you, and if there were I wouldn't give it to a child. Ask me again when you're grown."

"I will."

"I don't doubt it." She looked at the scar for a while without touching it, and went out to speak to Dumbledore in the corridor in a voice that was not quite low enough.

Then there was the library.

It was on the fourth floor and took up most of it. The doors were tall, and the room beyond them went back further than Harry could see from the threshold, between shelves that went up into dimness, and it smelled of what the encyclopaedia had smelled of under its plastic, concentrated. Madam Pince stood behind a desk inside the door. She was thin and tight-mouthed. She held Dumbledore's letter in both hands and read it twice while the three of them waited, and then looked over it at Harry.

"He's four."

"He is," said Dumbledore.

"The letter says he's to have the run of the general collection."

"It does."

"Children in this library leave jam on the pages, Headmaster. They leave the pages themselves, in some cases."

"I'm told he's careful."

Madam Pince looked at Harry a little longer. Then she gave him the rules: no book left the room without her stamp; no food or drink came into it; no ink came within an arm's length of a page; he would sit at the table nearest her desk and nowhere else; he would return each book to her and not to the shelf; and if she found a single turned-down corner he would not come back. Harry said he understood. He asked where the catalogue was. There was a ledger, Madam Pince said, which he might look at under her eye.

The ledger ran by the year the school had acquired each book, which told him where a book was and nothing about what was in it. He asked whether there was a list by subject. There was not, she said; there never had been; she knew where everything was.

He did not start at the beginning of the ledger. He had thought on the bus about what the library was for, and the answer was the list he kept, of what was wrong and what each thing needed, and the library was where the needs might be met. So he asked first for Charms, because charms were what he could do; then for Potions and for Healing, because those were what Lupin and the people in the Prophet's hospital pages needed; then for the law and the Ministry's own publications, because he would have to know what was allowed; then history, which told him why things were as they were. Divination he left until last, after reading one book of it. He asked for the first book at half past three and the second at a quarter to five, and when the bell rang for dinner he had four on the return pile and Madam Pince was looking at him rather than at the pile.

On the third day he asked her whether, since there was no list by subject, she would like one. She said she had managed for thirty-one years. He said he was going to read the books anyway, that writing a card for each one afterwards would cost him very little, and that the cards would be hers to burn if she did not want them. She said he was not to bring ink into her library. He said he would write them in his room. By the end of the summer there were three hundred and ninety cards in a shoebox on her desk, one for every book he had read there, each with the title and the shelf and six lines on what the book was about, in a clerk's hand. She said nothing about them. She did not burn them. In the second week of August he saw her take one out to answer a question of Professor Sprout's, and put it back.

---

He ate with the staff who stayed through the summer, at a single table set crosswise in the Great Hall under a ceiling that showed the sky. There were eight of them most evenings: Dumbledore in the middle; McGonagall, when she was not away; a tiny man with a shock of white hair who was Professor Flitwick and taught Charms; a round woman with earth under her nails who was Professor Sprout; Madam Pomfrey; Madam Pince, who did not speak at meals; Hagrid, at the end, on a chair of his own; and Lupin, whom Dumbledore had put in a room along the corridor from Harry's. Professor Snape, who taught Potions and lived in the dungeons, came to the table on the third evening and not before.

He came in while the soup was going round and took the empty chair at the far end from Hagrid. He looked down the table at Lupin, and then at Harry, and Harry looked back and saw a thin man in black with a sallow face and hair that needed washing, who was perhaps twenty-four, like Lupin.

"Severus," said Dumbledore. "This is Harry."

"I know who it is."

"Harry would like to learn something of Potions this summer. I've told him the second-year room is free and that he may use it under Remus's eye, if you've no objection."

"The second-year room is full of second-year equipment."

"Which he won't damage."

Snape looked at Harry again. Harry had read every Potions text in the crate and three more from Flourish and Blotts, and he had a list of questions for the first person who could answer them. He had not expected the first person to look like this. He asked the first one anyway.

"Why does it matter which way you stir?"

Snape did not answer at once. Then he said, "Because the brew is not a soup. Which text have you read?"

Harry told him. Snape's eyebrows went up a fraction at the third title. He asked another question, about the order of adding two ingredients that the texts disagreed on, and Harry gave both texts' reasons and said which he thought was right and why. Snape listened without his face moving. At the end of it he turned to Dumbledore.

"He may use the room. He is to touch nothing of mine. Lupin is to be in the room when there is a fire under anything, and if there is an accident I will hear about it from Lupin and not from the hospital wing." He picked up his spoon. "The stirring is in Borage, third chapter. He has not read Borage."

"No," said Harry. "I'll read it tomorrow."

He read it the next morning, by nine. It answered the question and raised four others, which he kept, since Snape did not come to dinner again for a week.

---

Professor Flitwick found him on the fifth day, on the floor of the library between two shelves with a book about the charms on the library itself.

Madam Pince had not meant him to have that book. It had been on her desk, and he had asked, and she had looked at him and at the book and pushed it across without a word, which he had learned by then was the nearest she came to approval. It was eighty years old. It explained how the shelves kept their order, how a stolen book knew it was stolen, and how the Restricted Section's grille told the difference between Madam Pince's hand and anyone else's, and Harry had three questions it could not answer. Professor Flitwick, who had come in for something else, sat down on the floor across from him without being asked and answered all three.

Flitwick was the first person at Hogwarts who seemed pleased rather than startled. He talked fast and with his hands, and when Harry asked a question he looked delighted to have been asked. Harry asked about the shelving charms. He asked how a photograph in the Prophet held a few seconds of movement and gave them back. He asked about Omnioculars, which the Prophet's Quidditch pages advertised, and which could slow down and replay what they had seen.

"They can. Clever things. The charm's a cousin of the one on the photographs, with a rate on it, so you can turn the handle and have it slower or faster." Flitwick looked at him. "Why?"

Harry had decided on the bus that he would tell the staff what was true and leave Dumbledore to tell them why.

"I read faster than I can see. The reading isn't the slow part. My eyes are. They can only jump so often and take in so much at once, and a page takes as long as it takes however much I'd like it quicker. I wondered if there was a way of showing me the words faster than I can look at them."

Flitwick was quiet, which Harry had not seen him be.

"The eyes," he said. "Not the understanding."

"No. The understanding's all right."

"There are charms for people who see badly. Lenses and so on. They make the thing bigger or sharper. They don't make it come faster." He thought. "The Omniocular charm shows you what it's already seen, as fast or slow as you like. If it had seen a page... no. It would show you the page, and you'd still read it with the eyes you've got. You'd want something that gave you the words one at a time, where you're already looking, so the eye needn't move at all. I don't know anything that does that. I don't know that anything can't." He got up off the floor, which did not take him far. "Let me think. Ask me again in a week."

---

On the thirty-first of July he was four, and for the first time somebody knew.

Hagrid knew first, because he had asked Lupin. He arrived at breakfast with a cake under a cloth and set it in front of Harry's place with great seriousness, and it was heavier than it looked and tasted of treacle and something Harry could not name, and Harry ate a slice and said it was good. Mrs Figg had sent a card with a cat on the front and nothing inside but her name and the cats' names, in order. Dumbledore gave him a book he had not read, which had been Dumbledore's own at school, with Dumbledore's notes in the margins. McGonagall, who was away, had left a parcel: a box of matches and a note saying she would expect to see the same number of needles in September. Flitwick gave him a brass thing with a handle that he said was an Omniocular with the works taken out. "For looking at. Not for using. The using part is what I'm thinking about."

Lupin's present was a photograph.

It was small, with a white border, and it showed two people in a garden with a pushchair between them. The man had Harry's hair and was laughing at something to the left of the picture. The woman had red hair and was not laughing; she was looking at the pushchair, and then she looked up at the man, and then back at the pushchair, and the man went on laughing. It took about four seconds and then began again. There was somebody in the pushchair under a sun hat who could not be seen.

"That's you," said Lupin. "Under the hat. It was the only one I had with all three of you in it. I've been meaning to get it copied. You may as well have the original."

Harry looked at it for a long time. The others at the table had stopped eating, and then, one by one, started again, and Hagrid blew his nose. Harry watched his mother look at the pushchair and up at his father and down again, four seconds at a time, and after a while he put the photograph in his shirt pocket, where it stayed for the rest of the summer, and said, "Thank you," to Lupin, and ate another slice of Hagrid's cake.

---

Hester kept the owls going through the summer from Luton, buying in Luton on Fridays, and wrote on Sundays to say what had gone out and what had come in and what the customers asked for that she could not supply. By August the thing they asked for most, after pens, was books: Muggle novels, which they had heard of and could not buy without going where they did not go. Harry wrote back in ink that she should lend them, a penny a week by owl, that he had checked the law and it was legal, and that she should start with the ones Mrs Dennison recommended.

The ink had gone on in his room in the evenings, where Madam Pince's rule did not reach, and the castle had parchment and ink in quantities Mrs Figg's kitchen had not. By the end of July he could lay down a line of writing at a time, seeing the ink as ink and the letters as letters together, and the line arrived whole. By the middle of August it was a paragraph. The letters came out heavy and the edges ran, and Lupin said it looked like a note written in a moving carriage, which was fair. But a letter to Dumbledore, which had been an hour in pencil, took a quarter of that.

He wrote to Bathilda Bagshot the same way. She had written the History, and the History had left things out, and he had a page of questions about what it had left out. Dumbledore said she was alive, very old, living at Godric's Hollow, and likely to answer. Lupin addressed the owl. The reply came in a fortnight, nine pages in a hand worse than Harry's, and it answered four of his questions and raised eleven more, and finished by saying that she had known his grandparents and that he was to come and see her when somebody could bring him.

And he finished the costing. He had Lupin's report and the apothecary's reply from June, and he had the three books and Belby's paper, and in August he asked Professor Sprout in the greenhouse which of the Wolfsbane ingredients were dear and why. She said that two were dear because they were rare and three because one apothecary in London was the only stockist, and that she had told the Ministry so twice. He asked Snape, at the fourth dinner, whether the potion could be brewed in a large batch without spoiling. Snape said that Belby had not tried, that nobody had asked, and that it was not a stupid question, and named no book, because there was none. Harry wrote the costing out, two pages, and gave it to Dumbledore with a note asking again for the name of someone who could brew, since the first batch would need hands that were not Snape's. Dumbledore wrote back that he had a name and would bring it in September.

---

The summer had a shape by the second week of August.

He woke at six, which Madam Pomfrey had opinions about and Dumbledore did not. He was in the library when Madam Pince unlocked it at seven, and he read at the table by her desk, a book in something over an hour, with the return pile growing at his right hand until she came and took it away. At eleven Lupin came for him and they went down to the dungeons, where the second-year room was cold and smelled of old fires, and Harry brewed what the second-year texts said to brew while Lupin sat on a stool by the door and read, and at one they went up to eat. In the afternoon he was in the library again until the light went, except on Tuesdays and Fridays, when Hagrid had him for an hour among the pumpkins and told him things about the Forest that Dumbledore would not have told him. After dinner Dumbledore sometimes sent for him, and they talked in the round office with the portraits pretending to sleep and a red and gold bird on a perch by the door watching Harry the way Mr Tibbles did.

Snape came to dinner three times more that summer. Each time Harry asked him one question, and each time Snape answered it, shortly, and named a book, and the book was in the library and Harry had read it by the following morning. On the last occasion, in the final week of August, Snape looked at him after answering and said, "You ask things the way your mother did. As if you were owed the answer." Harry waited. "She usually was," Snape said, and went back to his plate.

Madam Pince stopped looking at the return pile altogether. By the middle of August she had taken to leaving a book or two on his table before he arrived, with no comment, and he read those first.

On the last night, lying in the room with the window over the lake, he did the arithmetic with the real numbers. A book took him an hour and ten minutes, taking the thick ones with the thin. He had the library from seven to seven. At that rate, with the terms taken out, eleven thousand books came to a little under four years, and he would be eight.

Flitwick had not come back to him with an answer in a week, or in three. He came to the library most days instead and sat on the floor, talking about lenses and photographs and the rate on an Omniocular, and went away shaking his head, and came back. Harry had not expected an answer in a summer.
