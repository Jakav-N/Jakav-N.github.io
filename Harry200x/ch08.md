# Chapter Eight

*September to December 1984*

He came back from Hogwarts on the first of September with a box, and the box was the whole of what he owned.

It held the wand in its brown paper, the four books Dumbledore had brought in the first week and a dozen more he had not finished with, the gutted Omniocular, the matches, a card with a cat on it, and a photograph in a shirt pocket that was in the box only because the shirt was. Lupin carried it up Wisteria Walk from where the Knight Bus had left them, and Mrs Figg opened the door before they reached it, and said that his room was the back bedroom and that Tufty had been sleeping on the bed and would have to be spoken to.

The back bedroom was his because Dumbledore had arranged it over the summer, in a conversation at Privet Drive that Harry had not been present for and nobody described to him. He would sleep at Mrs Figg's from now on, on weekdays and Saturdays. On Sundays Lupin would walk him round to Privet Drive after breakfast and collect him before tea, and he would sit in his aunt's front room with Dudley and a book and be in the house, which Dumbledore said was the thing that mattered, and which his aunt accepted. The cupboard under the stairs went back to being a cupboard; Harry saw the vacuum cleaner standing in it on the first Sunday. The back bedroom at Mrs Figg's had a window and a shelf, and he liked it.

Dudley, on the first Sunday, brought his book across the carpet without being asked and sat down on Harry's feet and said, "Do the letters." He had kept P and D and H through August. He had also, on his own, worked out O, because it was a circle, and was proud of it, and Harry told him he was right to be.

---

Lupin took him to London on the second Saturday, by train, which Harry had never been on.

They went for books, but not the wizarding kind. Harry had read what the High Street library had on the sciences, which was a shelf and a half, and had come to the end of it in the spring. He had written a list over the summer, in ink, of what he wanted next, and Dumbledore had read it at the staff table and passed it to Lupin with his eyebrows up, and had agreed that it should be paid for from the vault at the goblins' rate, which turned sixty Galleons into three hundred pounds and a bag of notes that Lupin kept in his inside pocket with his hand on it.

He spent the hour on the train watching back gardens go past and reading the advertisements over the seats. At Waterloo Lupin bought a map and studied it on the concourse for some time, and they walked up over the river and into Charing Cross Road, where there was a bookshop with five floors.

He had thought Flourish and Blotts large. This shop was larger, and nobody in it was surprised to see a child, and nobody asked whose he was. Lupin found a basket. Harry went along the shelves at a walk, reading the spines of a floor at a time, and took down what he wanted, and the basket filled: a mathematics textbook for sixteen-year-olds and another for undergraduates, three volumes of physics, two of chemistry, a thick biology, an economics textbook with a blue cover, a history of England in four volumes, a book on how the law worked, a dictionary of medicine. The assistant at the till looked at the basket and at Harry and at Lupin and said nothing beyond the total, which was two hundred and eighty pounds, and Lupin counted it out in notes.

They carried the books back to Waterloo in two bags. On the train Harry read the first volume of the physics, and Lupin read the economics, and at Little Whinging Lupin said that he had understood about a third of it.

That was the autumn. Five books a day on weekdays, from a stack that went down and was replaced, with the library's twelve on top: mathematics first, because the physics needed it and so did the economics, and then the physics, and then everything else at once. He did not go in order after the first week. He had read enough by then to see where each book leaned on the others, and he read whichever one the last one had made necessary. Lupin, who sat with him for the wand work in the mornings and read his own books in the afternoons, said at the end of October that he had never watched anyone read like that.

---

Dumbledore came on the first Tuesday in October with an answer about the gold.

He had spoken to the goblins, who did not object to a wizard's gold leaving them in the form of pounds, provided it left as pounds and did not come back as gold, and who had charged him a fee for the information. He had spoken to a man called Hadley.

"He was at Hogwarts, some years after Remus. Muggle-born. He went back to his father's world after school and qualified as a solicitor, and he is one of perhaps a dozen people in the country who understand both kinds of money. I've asked him to hold a sum for you in trust, in the Muggle way, and to put it where you ask, within reason. He said he would need to meet you. I said that he would find you unusual."

"How much?"

"Two thousand Galleons. Ten thousand pounds, at the goblins' rate. A tenth of what's in the vault, more or less. If it grows, we may talk about more. If it doesn't, you will have learned something at a price I can justify to myself." Dumbledore took a sherbet lemon. "I read your two pages. They were very clear. You might have left out the part where you explained to me what a bank was."

"I didn't know what you knew."

"No. That was fair."

Mr Hadley came to Wisteria Walk the following week, in a grey suit, with a briefcase, and sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's with his knees together and asked Harry what he wanted to do with ten thousand pounds. Harry told him. He had read the economics textbook and thought about it for a month, and he had read the share prices on the television every night for two years, and what he wanted was dull: a spread of large companies that paid dividends, the dividends put back in, nothing sold without a reason, and the reasons written down. Mr Hadley wrote this on a pad. He asked where Harry had learned the word dividend. Harry told him. Mr Hadley wrote that down as well, and then put his pen away and looked at Harry for a while, and said that he would send the papers for Professor Dumbledore to sign.

---

In the third week of October Lupin took him to Godric's Hollow.

Bathilda Bagshot had written again, twice, and the second letter had said that she was ninety-one and did not propose to wait indefinitely. So they went, on a Saturday, by the Knight Bus, which set them down at the edge of a village in the West Country with a church and a square and a war memorial in the middle of the square.

It was not a war memorial when Harry looked at it. He had read about this in the second of Bathilda's letters and had not quite believed it, and he stood in the square with Lupin's hand on his shoulder and watched the stone obelisk become a statue of a man and a woman and a baby, the way the bricks had become an arch in the yard behind the Leaky Cauldron, one piece after another. The man had his hair. The woman had a long face and her hair down. The baby in her arms had no scar, because the sculptor had not known about it, or had not wanted to.

"Only wizards see it," Lupin said. "Muggles see the memorial. It was put up the year after."

Harry looked at the statue for a while. Then he said, "Where's the house?"

The house was at the end of the lane past the church, behind a hedge grown wild, and it was still a house as far as the first floor and after that it was not. The right-hand side of the upper storey was open to the sky, and ivy had got in, and the roof over that part had gone entirely. A sign had grown up out of the long grass by the gate, on a post, and it said in gold letters what had happened here and when, and underneath, in a great many hands and inks, people had written things. Harry read them all. Most said thank you. Some said good luck. One, near the bottom, in green, said that if he ever read this he should know that he was not alone, and was signed with a name he did not know.

He did not go in. There was nothing in there he needed to see; he had been in there, and he had the room exactly, and the window and the cot and the floor. He stood at the gate and looked at the open roof from the outside for as long as it took the clouds behind it to move the width of a hand, and Lupin stood beside him and said nothing at all, and then they went to the churchyard.

The grave was white marble, at the back, with two names and two sets of dates and a line beneath that Harry read and did not understand, and asked about. Lupin said that it was the kind of thing people put on graves, and that Dumbledore had chosen it, and that he had never been sure what Dumbledore meant by it either. Harry stood and read the dates. His father had been twenty-one years and seven months old. His mother had been twenty-one years and nine. He had known both numbers since the letter in the tin. Seeing them cut in stone was different, and he had not expected it to be.

"I don't know what you're meant to do," he said.

"Nobody does," said Lupin. "People stand here. Some of them talk to it. I used to. I stopped, because I didn't think they could hear, and then I started again, because it didn't seem to matter whether they could."

Harry stood a while longer. He did not talk to it. He told Lupin that he would like to come back, and Lupin said yes, and they went to find Bathilda Bagshot.

She lived in a cottage that smelled of old books and older cat, and she was very old indeed, with a face like a crumpled bag and eyes that were still quick in it, and she opened the door and looked down at Harry and said, "You've got your grandmother's chin. Come in. Don't touch the owl, it bites."

They were there until the light went. She answered his eleven questions and the four more he had brought, in a voice that wandered off and came back, and she got some things wrong that he knew from the Prophet to be wrong and he said so, and she said that at ninety-one one was permitted to misremember the Ministers of Magic, and that he was not to be pert. She told him about his grandparents, who had lived in the village and whom she had known for forty years, and about his father as a boy, which she remembered better than she remembered last week. She did not mention the night the roof came off, and neither did he. When they left she gave him a book from her own shelf, which was the first edition of her History with her notes in the margins about what she had got wrong, and said that he was to send it back when he had read it, and that she expected him in the spring.

On the bus Lupin asked whether he was all right. Harry said yes. Then, because it was true and Lupin had asked, he said that he had not expected the statue, and that he would need to think about the statue, and Lupin said that he had always found it a bit much himself, and that was the whole of what either of them said about Godric's Hollow for some time.

---

Dumbledore read Harry's letter about the Wolfsbane ingredients at Mrs Figg's table on the first Tuesday in November and set it down.

"You've written to Damocles Belby."

"Yes. In September. He wrote back. He said he hadn't tried a large batch because there was no demand for a large batch, and that he supposed there was no reason it shouldn't work, and that I was to stop writing to him. I've written to the apothecary in London instead, the one that's the only stockist. I asked what three of the ingredients would cost by the pound rather than the ounce."

"And?"

"Half. At most. One of them, a tenth. They don't sell by the pound because nobody asks."

Dumbledore folded his hands. "What is it you want to do, Harry?"

He had been asked that before, by himself, more often than by anyone else. He kept a kind of account of it: the things that were wrong that he knew about, and against each one what it would take to put it right, and which of those things he had. Most of the list needed hands he did not have, or money he could not reach, or someone to listen to a four-year-old, and those stayed on the list and waited. The potion was different. The thing missing from it was arithmetic, and he had arithmetic. That was why it had come first.

"I want the potion to cost less. There are more people like Remus than there are people who can pay for it, and the price is high because nobody's bothered, not because it has to be. I can't brew it. Professor Snape won't let me near anything with aconite in it for years, and he's right. But somebody could brew a month's worth for ten people at once and sell it at what it costs, and the costing is the part I can do. I've done it. It's two pages. I'd like you to give them to someone who can brew."

Dumbledore read the two pages. He read them more slowly than Harry would have liked, and when he had finished he put them inside his robe.

"I'll give them to Severus," he said. "He may say no. He may say yes and not tell me. Either way he will read them properly, which is more than most people would." He looked at Harry. "You know that if this works it will not be you who gets the credit."

"I don't want the credit. I want it to cost less."

---

The ink was a page at a time by November.

He had got there by degrees, a line and then three and then a paragraph, and the step from a paragraph to a page had been the hardest, because a page of ink was a great many drops and all of them wanted to be somewhere other than where he put them. He saw the ink and the letters together now, as one thing, and when he had seen a page the ink left the bottle in a thread and went to the paper and lay down and was a page, in a hand that was not his, since his own hand had never been good, but was a hand he had made up and could keep steady. Lupin said it looked like a clerk's.

A page took him the time it took the ink to cross the inch of air and settle, which was a few seconds, and then the time it took him to look at what the ink had done before he saw the next, which was no time at all, and then a few seconds more for the next page. His letters to Dumbledore were ten minutes. His letters to Bathilda Bagshot, who wrote nine pages and expected nine back, were an hour, which was the length of one of her paragraphs. He wrote, that autumn, to Dumbledore and Bathilda and Mrs Dennison and Mr Hadley and the apothecary and the man at the council, who had written to Mrs Figg to say the fence was up; and to Dudley, once, a letter with the alphabet in it, which his aunt found on the Monday and did not throw away.

McGonagall watched him do it on the second Thursday in November. She had come for the Transfiguration and found him finishing a letter, and she stood in the door of Mrs Figg's kitchen and watched a page arrive out of a bottle and said nothing until it had dried.

"What do you call that?"

"I don't call it anything. It's the ink. I move it."

"That is not a charm I know."

"It isn't a charm. There's no word and no movement. I see where it goes and it goes. I've done it since I was two, with socks."

McGonagall came and picked up the page and held it to the light.

"It is not Transfiguration either," she said. "Nothing has been changed. It has only been moved." She put the page down. "I shall have to think about what it is. In the meantime, Mr Potter, the matches."

He had forty needles for her by then, in a tin. She counted them.

---

Christmas at Hogwarts was eleven students and the staff, and one of the eleven saw him.

He went up on the twentieth with Lupin, by the Knight Bus, and Dumbledore had given him his old room and a note to Madam Pince, and he was in the library by eight on the twenty-first with the return pile growing. The students who had stayed were mostly older, and mostly kept to their common rooms, and Dumbledore had thought that a small boy in the library and at the far end of the staff table would be noticed by nobody who mattered. He was noticed on the twenty-third by a girl of about fifteen with a Ravenclaw scarf, who came into the library for a book and stopped between the shelves and looked at him for a while, at the stack beside him and at his forehead, which he had not thought to cover, and went out again without the book.

She did not speak to him. He learned afterwards from Flitwick, who was her Head of House, that she had written home that evening, and that her mother had written to a cousin, and that the cousin's wife worked at the Prophet. That was in January. In December what happened was Christmas dinner, at one long table with the students at one end and the staff at the other and Harry between Hagrid and Lupin, and crackers that went off with a bang he could watch, and a hat from one of them that Hagrid wore all afternoon, and Madam Pince, at the far end, eating a mince pie.

Flitwick found him in the library on Boxing Day, and sat on the floor.

"I haven't got it," he said. "I want to tell you where I've got to, so you don't think I've stopped. The Omniocular charm will hold a page. I've done that; I've a pair that have looked at a page of Borage and will show it back as long as you like. The trouble is what I said in the summer. They show you the page, and you read the page with your eyes, and your eyes are the trouble. What I want is something that gives you a word, and then the next word, in the same place, so the eye needn't move. Nothing I know of does that. I've been through everything in the Charms section and some things that aren't, and I've written to two people who know more than I do." He rubbed his face. "I'm not giving it up. I'm telling you it's a long job."

"How long?"

"I don't know. A year. Years. It may be that it can't be done and I'm the wrong man to find that out." He looked at the stack beside Harry. "How many is that?"

"Since July? Seven hundred and three. Three hundred and ninety of them from here."

Flitwick was quiet for a moment.

"Well," he said. "You've time, then. That's one way of looking at it."

Harry had looked at it that way. He said he did not think much of it. Flitwick laughed, got up off the floor, said he would be back in the New Year, and was.
