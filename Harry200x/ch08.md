# Chapter Eight

*September to December 1984*

He came back from Hogwarts on the first of September with a box, and the box held everything he owned: the wand in its brown paper, the books he had not finished with, the gutted Omniocular, the matches, a card with a cat on it, a shoebox of blank cards, and a photograph in a shirt pocket. Lupin carried it up Wisteria Walk from where the Knight Bus had left them. Mrs Figg opened the door before they reached it and said that his room was the back bedroom, and that Tufty had been sleeping on the bed and would have to be spoken to.

The back bedroom was his because Dumbledore had arranged it with his aunt over the summer, in a conversation nobody described. He would sleep at Mrs Figg's on weekdays and Saturdays. On Sundays Lupin would walk him round to Privet Drive after breakfast and collect him before tea, and he would sit in his aunt's front room with Dudley and a book and be in the house, which Dumbledore said was what mattered. The cupboard under the stairs went back to being a cupboard. The back bedroom had a window and a shelf.

---

On the second Tuesday in September he asked Dumbledore for ten names.

He had the briefs written already, a page each in ink. Ten people who had left Hogwarts with good marks and found nothing, or found something they hated, and would take fifteen Galleons a week to read and brew and count and write down what they found, in rooms above the newsagent's in Luton that Hester's father would let for almost nothing. One to read everything ever printed about warding and write down where the books agreed and where they stopped. One to do the same for the charms that held pictures and moved them, so that Flitwick had somebody to argue with who was not Harry. One to brew the ordinary potions the apothecaries sold dear, Pepperup and burn paste and the rest, which wanted no licence, and sell them from the Alley room's door at what they cost plus a tenth. One to go through the Prophet's back numbers in the Ministry's reading room and count the werewolves the Ministry had not counted. Two to cast the simplest charms over and over, with Lupin's watch, so that he could find out whether what was true of his casting was true of anyone's. The rest for whatever the first six turned up.

"Ten," Dumbledore said.

"Ten to start. The tin can pay six now and the vault can pay the rest, and the books in Oxford will pay all of them by Easter. The figures are on the back."

Dumbledore turned the page over and read the figures.

"You understand what you're describing. Not a boy with a tutor and a woman in Luton who sends pens. A house. With wages, and rooms, and a name on the door, and the Ministry will want to know whose name, and the apothecaries will want to know why their customers have stopped coming, and Lucius Malfoy will want to know everything."

"Hester's name on the door. She's said she'll have it. The Ministry can ask her and she'll tell them the truth, which is that she runs it and I pay for it. By the time they think to ask, I'll be in the Prophet anyway." He waited. "You said yes to Hester, and to Remus's afternoons. This is the same thing, only more of it. One person finding things out is a hobby. Ten is how things get found out."

Dumbledore looked at the figures a while longer.

"Six names by October," he said. "The other four when the first six have been paid for a month and nobody has blown anything up. And Severus is to see every potion that leaves that room before it's sold, whether it wants a licence or not, and he won't thank either of us."

---

He went to Luton for the first time on a Thursday in November, by Knight Bus with Lupin, under the hat.

The newsagent's was on a corner with a bus stop outside it, and Hester's mother was behind the counter and looked at Harry and then at Hester and said, "This is him, then," and Hester said it was, and took him through the back and up a staircase that smelled of newsprint and, higher up, of something boiling. The rooms above the shop had been a flat. Now the front room had four desks in it and the bedroom two, and the kitchen had a cauldron on the stove and Joan Tilley's assistant watching it, and the bathroom had become a cupboard for jars.

Six people stood up when he came in, which he had not expected and did not like, and he asked them to sit down, and they did.

He went round them one at a time. Ruth Marchbanks had the warding books, forty of them on the floor beside her desk in two piles, read and not; she was thirty, Muggle-born, had been a clerk at the Ministry for a year and been let go, and she said that so far every book she had read copied the one before it and added a name. Owen Pryce had the picture charms; he was twenty-two and had wanted to be a Charms teacher and been told there were no posts, and he had already written to Flitwick twice and been written back to once. The man with the Prophet's back numbers, a Muggle-born called Soames who had been a reporter for two months before the Prophet decided it did not need him, had reached 1978 and had eighty names. The two with Lupin's watch were a brother and sister from Hull who had failed to get into the Ministry for the same reason nobody put in writing, and they had a table of numbers that was two weeks old and already said something Harry had not expected, which was that a grown wizard who had cast a charm every day for ten years still failed it one time in twelve. Joan Tilley was in the kitchen and did not come out.

Harry asked each of them three questions and wrote each of them a brief for the fortnight before they had finished answering, with the ink laying down a page at a time on Hester's desk, and the brother from Hull stopped talking in the middle of a sentence to watch it, and then went on.

In the stairwell afterwards Hester said, "They'll do."

"Yes."

"Two of them didn't believe you were real until you walked in. One of them still doesn't. She'll be fine." She looked at the wages book under her arm. "You'll want the other four by Christmas."

"I'd like them by Christmas. Professor Dumbledore said a month without anything blowing up."

"Nothing's going to blow up," said Hester. "I've put the cauldron in the kitchen."

The seventh and eighth came in December. By Christmas the rooms above the newsagent's were four rooms, because Hester's father had knocked through, and the Alley room sold Pepperup at a third of the apothecaries' price to a queue that Snape inspected on Tuesdays without saying a word to anyone but Joan Tilley, and Soames's count stood at three hundred and nine and was still going. Harry read eight reports a week before breakfast and wrote eight replies before Mrs Figg had the kettle on.

---

In the third week of October Lupin took him to Godric's Hollow.

Bathilda Bagshot had written again, twice, and the second letter had said that she was ninety-one and did not propose to wait indefinitely. So they went, on a Saturday, by the Knight Bus, which set them down at the edge of a village in the West Country with a church and a square and a war memorial in the middle of the square.

It was not a war memorial when Harry looked at it. He had read about this in the second of Bathilda's letters and had not quite believed it, and he stood in the square with Lupin's hand on his shoulder and watched the stone obelisk become a statue of a man and a woman and a baby, the way the bricks had become an arch in the yard behind the Leaky Cauldron, one piece after another. The man had his hair. The woman had a long face and her hair down. The baby in her arms had no scar, because the sculptor had not known about it, or had not wanted to.

"Only wizards see it," Lupin said. "Muggles see the memorial. It was put up the year after."

Harry looked at the statue for a while. Then he said, "Where's the house?"

The house was at the end of the lane past the church, behind a hedge grown wild, and it was still a house as far as the first floor and after that it was not. The right-hand side of the upper storey was open to the sky, and ivy had got in, and the roof over that part had gone entirely. A sign had grown up out of the long grass by the gate, on a post, and it said in gold letters what had happened here and when, and underneath, in a great many hands and inks, people had written things. Harry read them all. Most said thank you. Some said good luck. One, near the bottom, in green, said that if he ever read this he should know that he was not alone, and was signed with a name he did not know.

He did not go in. There was nothing in there he needed to see; he had been in there, and he had the room exactly, the window and the cot and the floor. He stood at the gate and looked at the open roof from the outside for as long as it took the clouds behind it to move the width of a hand, and Lupin stood beside him and said nothing, and then they went to the churchyard.

The grave was white marble, at the back, with two names and two sets of dates and a line beneath that Harry read and did not understand, and asked about. Lupin said it was the kind of thing people put on graves, that Dumbledore had chosen it, and that he had never been sure what Dumbledore meant by it either. Harry stood and read the dates. His father had been twenty-one years and seven months old. His mother had been twenty-one years and nine. He had known both numbers since the letter in the tin. Seeing them cut in stone was different, and he had not expected it to be.

"I don't know what you're meant to do," he said.

"Nobody does," said Lupin. "People stand here. Some of them talk to it. I used to. I stopped, because I didn't think they could hear, and then I started again, because it didn't seem to matter whether they could."

Harry stood a while longer. He did not talk to it. He told Lupin he would like to come back, and Lupin said yes, and they went to find Bathilda Bagshot.

She lived in a cottage that smelled of old books and older cat, and she was very old indeed, with a face like a crumpled bag and eyes that were still quick in it, and she opened the door and looked down at Harry and said, "You've got your grandmother's chin. Come in. Don't touch the owl, it bites."

They were there until the light went. She answered his eleven questions and the four more he had brought, in a voice that wandered off and came back, and she got some things wrong that he knew from the Prophet to be wrong and he said so, and she said that at ninety-one one was permitted to misremember the Ministers of Magic, and that he was not to be pert. She told him about his grandparents, who had lived in the village and whom she had known for forty years, and about his father as a boy, which she remembered better than she remembered last week. She did not mention the night the roof came off, and neither did he. When they left she gave him a book from her own shelf, the first edition of her History with her notes in the margins about what she had got wrong, and said that he was to send it back when he had read it, and that she expected him in the spring.

On the bus Lupin asked whether he was all right. Harry said yes. Then, because it was true and Lupin had asked, he said that he had not expected the statue, and that he would need to think about the statue, and Lupin said that he had always found it a bit much himself, and that was all either of them said about Godric's Hollow for some time.

---

Dumbledore brought the brewer's name on the first Tuesday in September and read the finished costing at Mrs Figg's table on the second, and Joan Tilley came to Wisteria Walk in the third week.

She was thirty and short and had burns on both hands, and she had been brewing in a Muggle chemist's in Croydon since she left school because no apothecary would take her. She read the costing twice at the table while Harry and Lupin waited. Then she said that the quantity for the second ingredient was wrong by a fifth, and why, and that the rest was right, and asked when she could start.

In October Hester took a lease in her own name on a room at the wrong end of Diagon Alley, forty Galleons a month, two doors from a shop that sold second-hand cauldrons. Snape came to it once, in November, looked at everything, said four sentences to Joan Tilley and none to anyone else, and signed the licence that let aconite be kept there under his name. The first batch was brewed in the last week of November, for three. Lupin was one of the three, and his flask was larger than the others', and nobody told him why.

---

The primer he wrote in October, in a day.

He had read the first-year texts on magical theory in a day in September of the year before and found them long on ceremony and short on sense, and he wrote, in a hundred and forty pages, a plain account of what a spell was and what a wand did and why the words mattered, for a child of eleven. Dumbledore read it in an evening at Mrs Figg's table and turned back to the fourth chapter and read that again.

"You've said a thing here about why the words matter that I've been teaching the opposite of for forty years."

"Is it wrong?"

"I don't know. I'd like to argue about it in January, when I've thought of something to say." He closed the book. "Who's it for?"

"Anyone who's eleven and hasn't got it. I'd like Flourish and Blotts to sell it. Not with my name on. No name isn't a lie."

Dumbledore took it to Flourish and Blotts himself. It was in the window by the middle of November with nothing on the spine but the title, and it sold six hundred copies by Christmas, and three people wrote to the shop to ask who had written it and were told the truth, which was that the author preferred not to be named.

The nine Muggle books he had written in the first week of September, a day each, at the desk in the back bedroom with a ream of paper from the High Street and two bottles, seeing each page and letting the ink lie down and looking at it and seeing the next. Lupin read the French on the Thursday and said it was better than his. Mr Hadley collected the stack and said that the man in Oxford had begun to ask for a name, and Harry said that the man could have no name or nothing. Mr Hadley also brought the first figures from the trust, which had grown by a little under a tenth since April with the dividends put back in, and Harry said that he would be asking for the other half of the vault in the spring, with reasons, and that Mr Hadley should have the reasons first, because he would know which of them were wrong.

The rest of the autumn went by owl. Hester's cousin Ivy came in September to do the parcels, because Hester could not do the parcels and the lending library with one pair of hands, and the lending library began in October with three hundred Muggle novels bought for forty pounds from a shop in Luton that was closing, and the first week's post brought back two hundred and six requests. Colin's rucksack came on Fridays and then on Tuesdays as well. By Christmas the tin had been a Gringotts account in Mrs Figg's name for two months, and Hester's monthly letter said that it held eighteen hundred Galleons after wages, and that what the customers wanted next was bicycles.

---

He had asked Dumbledore for children at their first meeting, in September of the year before, and been told not yet. He had asked again in April. In November he asked a third time, and Dumbledore gave him an answer with a date in it. There were two or three families he would trust with the knowledge of where Harry was. He would not need to trust them for long, because a girl in Ravenclaw had seen Harry in the library over the summer, and the Prophet would have the story by the New Year, and once the whole country knew where Harry was there was no reason the Longbottoms should not. Harry put the date on the list.

Eleven pupils stayed at Hogwarts over Christmas that year. One of them saw him.

He went up on the twentieth with Lupin, by the Knight Bus, and Dumbledore gave him his old room and a note to Madam Pince, and he was in the library by eight on the twenty-first with the return pile growing and the shoebox of cards open beside her desk. The pupils who had stayed were mostly older and kept to their common rooms, and Dumbledore had thought a small boy in the library and at the far end of the staff table would be noticed by nobody who mattered. He was noticed on the twenty-third by a girl of about fifteen with a Ravenclaw scarf, who came into the library for a book and stopped between the shelves and looked at him, at the stack beside him and at his forehead, which he had not thought to cover, and went out again without the book.

She did not speak to him. He learned afterwards from Flitwick, her Head of House, that she had written home that evening, that her mother had written to a cousin, and that the cousin's wife worked at the Prophet. That was in January. In December there was Christmas dinner, at one long table with the pupils at one end and the staff at the other and Harry between Hagrid and Lupin, and crackers, and a hat from one of them that Hagrid wore all afternoon, and Madam Pince at the far end eating a mince pie.

Snape was at the dinner. He said nothing to Harry across it, but when the table broke up he stopped behind Harry's chair for the time it took to say one thing, which was that Tilley's November batch had held, that it had cost a third of what Belby charged, and that Lupin had been one of the three and had not been told why his flask was larger. Then he went out.

Flitwick found Harry in the library on Boxing Day and sat on the floor.

"I haven't got it. I want to tell you where I've got to, so you don't think I've stopped. The Omniocular charm will hold a page. I've a pair that have looked at a page of Borage and will show it back as long as you like. The trouble is what I said in the summer. They show you the page, and you read it with the eyes you've got. What I want is something that gives you a word, and then the next word, in the same place, so the eye needn't move. Nothing I know of does that. I've been through the whole Charms section and written to two people who know more than I do." He rubbed his face. "I'm not giving it up. I'm telling you it's a long job."

"How long?"

"I don't know. A year. Years. It may be that it can't be done and I'm the wrong man to find that out." He looked at the stack beside Harry. "How many is that?"

"Since July? Seven hundred and three. Three hundred and ninety from here."

Flitwick was quiet a moment. "Well. You've time, then."

Harry said he did not think much of that, and Flitwick laughed, and got up, and said he would be back in the New Year, and was.

---

On the last night of the year Dumbledore sent for him, and they sat in the round office with the fire going and the portraits asleep or pretending, and Dumbledore asked what was on the list now. The long one.

Harry told him some of it. The house in Luton wanted the other two names, and then ten more, because the first eight had each found two questions for every one they answered. The brewing room could make for thirty by Easter if the room were bigger and Joan Tilley had help, and he had asked Hester to look for a bigger room. The lending library wanted more books than Luton had, and Colin had found a warehouse. There were two hundred and eleven names on a register that everyone said was wrong, and Soames had three hundred and nine already from the back numbers, and Lupin thought he could get the real figure in a year if he was allowed to ask werewolves instead of the Ministry. There was a boy at Privet Drive who could read now and had nothing in the house to read. There was Flitwick's card, which he could not help with except by waiting. There was the other half of the vault. There were the children, and the date.

Dumbledore listened to all of it and said nothing until he had finished.

"You've left one out."

"Which?"

"The one that's been first on the list since September. The man with the red eyes, who isn't dead."

"I haven't left it out. There isn't anything on it I can do yet, so I don't say it. I think about it every night."

"Yes," said Dumbledore. "So do I." He got up and went to the window, where the lake lay black under a sky with no moon in it, and stood with his back to the room for a while. "Go to bed, Harry. It's a new year in an hour, and you'll want to have slept."
