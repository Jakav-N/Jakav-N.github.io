# Chapter Eight

*September to December 1984*

He came back from Hogwarts on the first of September with a box, and the box was the whole of what he owned: the wand in its brown paper, Dumbledore's four books and a dozen more, the gutted Omniocular, the matches, a card with a cat on it, a shoebox of blank cards, and a photograph in a shirt pocket. Lupin carried it up Wisteria Walk from where the Knight Bus had left them. Mrs Figg opened the door before they reached it and said that his room was the back bedroom and that Tufty had been sleeping on the bed and would have to be spoken to.

The back bedroom was his because Dumbledore had arranged it with his aunt over the summer, in a conversation Harry had not been present for and nobody described. He would sleep at Mrs Figg's on weekdays and Saturdays. On Sundays Lupin would walk him round to Privet Drive after breakfast and collect him before tea, and he would sit in his aunt's front room with Dudley and a book and be in the house, which Dumbledore said was the thing that mattered. The cupboard under the stairs went back to being a cupboard. The back bedroom had a window and a shelf, and he liked it.

He also had, written in ink on the night before the bus, a list of what he meant to have done by Christmas. There were eight items. He had made it in the library in the last week of August out of the longer list he kept, by asking of each thing whether it needed anything he did not now have, and eight did not. Dumbledore had read it on the last evening and handed it back and said, "Yes. Go on, then."

---

The first item was the book, and he wrote it on the Sunday.

He had carried it in his head since March: the mathematics that sixteen-year-olds in England were examined on, laid out so that someone could learn it from the book alone, with the places people went wrong marked, because he had read eleven books that claimed to do this and they had all failed in the same ways. Two hundred pages in pencil had been seventy hours of his hand. Two hundred pages in ink was a day. He sat at the desk in the back bedroom from eight until the light went, with a ream of paper from the High Street and two bottles, and saw each page and let the ink lie down, and looked at it, and saw the next. Lupin read the stack on the Monday evening and said that he wished he had had it at fifteen. On the Tuesday it went to Mr Hadley.

Mr Hadley came to Wisteria Walk himself to say what he thought of it, which was that he knew a man at a small educational publisher in Oxford, that the man would want to know who had written it, and that he would say a client who preferred not to be named, which was true. He took the manuscript away in his briefcase. In November the man in Oxford offered six hundred pounds for it outright, with no author's name on the cover. Mr Hadley advised against an outright sum. Harry took it, because six hundred pounds in November was worth more to the list than a royalty in two years, and because the chemistry one had taken him the following Sunday and the physics the one after that.

Mr Hadley also brought the first figures from the trust, which had been invested since April. It had grown by a little under a tenth, with the dividends put back in. Harry read the sheet and asked two questions about two of the companies and then said that he would like Mr Hadley to tell Professor Dumbledore the figure, and that he would be asking for the other half of the vault in the spring, with reasons.

"I'd want to see the reasons too," said Mr Hadley.

"You'll have them. You'll have them first, because you'll know which of them are wrong."

---

The second item was hands, because Lupin had run out of them.

The owls had gone out all summer from Mrs Figg's front room at a quarter of the spring's pace, and in September they went back to the spring's pace and past it, because the sheet had been passed about over the holidays and there were letters now from people Mrs Figg had never sold a kitten to. Lupin spent Mondays in the High Street and at the table with the brown paper, and his two afternoons at the Ministry and in Diagon Alley, and he taught Harry nothing on Mondays at all. The biscuit tin was full by the second week.

Harry asked Dumbledore whether he knew anyone who had left Hogwarts with good marks and could not get work.

"A great many. Why?"

"Because Remus can't wrap parcels and find things out at the same time, and I'd rather pay someone who needs it than someone who doesn't."

Dumbledore brought a name the following Tuesday, and the week after that a young woman called Hester Pye came to Wisteria Walk. She had left Hogwarts in June with seven passes and had been turned down since by the Ministry twice and by three shops, for reasons none of them had put in writing, and her parents kept a newsagent's in Luton. She looked at the biscuit tin and the pile of torches and the sheet, and at Harry, and asked who she would be working for.

"Me. Mrs Figg's name is on the letters because mine can't be. But it's my money and my list, and I'll tell you what I'd like done and why, and if you think I'm wrong you're to say so."

"You're four."

"Yes. Remus will tell you it doesn't make much difference, and you can decide for yourself in a month. Twelve Galleons a week to start."

Hester Pye looked at Lupin, who nodded, and at Mrs Figg, who said, "He's quite serious, dear. You'd better sit down," and sat down, and started the next Monday. By the end of October the owls went out from a spare room above the newsagent's in Luton, because Mrs Figg's front room had run out of floor, and the tin had become a Gringotts account in Mrs Figg's name with three hundred and twenty Galleons in it after Hester's wages. By Christmas it was five hundred and ten. Hester had taken over the pricing in her second week, because she knew what her mother's customers would pay for a thing and Harry did not, and the first thing she did was put the price of a pen up by a Sickle, and the orders did not fall.

---

The third item was a student, and Mr Hadley found him.

Harry had the library's twelve books a week and a box from Charing Cross Road a month, and by October neither was enough, because the undergraduates' books referred to journals and to books the five-floored shop did not stock, and the journals were in university libraries where nobody would issue a ticket to a four-year-old. He asked Mr Hadley for somebody at a university in London who would, for twenty pounds a week, take a list to the library on Fridays and bring back what was on it, and copy out of the journals what could not be borrowed. Mr Hadley's clerk had a younger brother at Imperial College reading engineering and short of money. The brother came to Wisteria Walk once, looked at the back bedroom with its shelf of textbooks and its four-year-old, and said that he did not need to understand the arrangement to take the twenty pounds. His name was Colin. From November he came on Fridays with a rucksack, and the rucksack was the best thing in Harry's week.

That was how the autumn read. The undergraduates' books in the six subjects he had begun with, and then their references, which Colin brought; and beside them Snape's Borage, open, while he read the chemistry, looking for where the two agreed. They agreed about heat and about nothing else, and he kept the places where they disagreed for the summer and for Snape. The statistics he read with his own counts in his head, the twenties from Mrs Figg's table, and found the book had names for things he had been doing since January and a good many things he had not thought of, and he began to do those. By Christmas he had read, by Colin's reckoning on the back of a list, about a third of what a man reading for a degree in each of six subjects would have read, and Colin had stopped being surprised and started bringing things that were not on the list because he thought Harry would want them, and was usually right.

Lupin read the second-year undergraduate economics in October, having finished the first-year, and asked questions, and Harry answered them. It was the first time he had taught anyone anything but letters.

---

In the third week of October Lupin took him to Godric's Hollow.

Bathilda Bagshot had written again, twice, and the second letter had said that she was ninety-one and did not propose to wait indefinitely. So they went, on a Saturday, by the Knight Bus, which set them down at the edge of a village in the West Country with a church and a square and a war memorial in the middle of the square.

It was not a war memorial when Harry looked at it. He had read about this in the second of Bathilda's letters and had not quite believed it, and he stood in the square with Lupin's hand on his shoulder and watched the stone obelisk become a statue of a man and a woman and a baby, the way the bricks had become an arch in the yard behind the Leaky Cauldron, one piece after another. The man had his hair. The woman had a long face and her hair down. The baby in her arms had no scar, because the sculptor had not known about it, or had not wanted to.

"Only wizards see it," Lupin said. "Muggles see the memorial. It was put up the year after."

Harry looked at the statue for a while. Then he said, "Where's the house?"

The house was at the end of the lane past the church, behind a hedge grown wild, and it was still a house as far as the first floor and after that it was not. The right-hand side of the upper storey was open to the sky, and ivy had got in, and the roof over that part had gone entirely. A sign had grown up out of the long grass by the gate, on a post, and it said in gold letters what had happened here and when, and underneath, in a great many hands and inks, people had written things. Harry read them all. Most said thank you. Some said good luck. One, near the bottom, in green, said that if he ever read this he should know that he was not alone, and was signed with a name he did not know.

He did not go in. There was nothing in there he needed to see; he had been in there, and he had the room exactly, and the window and the cot and the floor. He stood at the gate and looked at the open roof from the outside for as long as it took the clouds behind it to move the width of a hand, and Lupin stood beside him and said nothing at all, and then they went to the churchyard.

The grave was white marble, at the back, with two names and two sets of dates and a line beneath that Harry read and did not understand, and asked about. Lupin said that it was the kind of thing people put on graves, and that Dumbledore had chosen it, and that he had never been sure what Dumbledore meant by it either. Harry stood and read the dates. His father had been twenty-one years and seven months old. His mother had been twenty-one years and nine. He had known both numbers since the letter in the tin. Seeing them cut in stone was different, and he had not expected it to be.

"I don't know what you're meant to do," he said.

"Nobody does," said Lupin. "People stand here. Some of them talk to it. I used to. I stopped, because I didn't think they could hear, and then I started again, because it didn't seem to matter whether they could."

Harry stood a while longer. He did not talk to it. He told Lupin that he would like to come back, and Lupin said yes, and they went to find Bathilda Bagshot.

She lived in a cottage that smelled of old books and older cat, and she was very old indeed, with a face like a crumpled bag and eyes that were still quick in it, and she opened the door and looked down at Harry and said, "You've got your grandmother's chin. Come in. Don't touch the owl, it bites."

They were there until the light went. She answered his eleven questions and the four more he had brought, in a voice that wandered off and came back, and she got some things wrong that he knew from the Prophet to be wrong and he said so, and she said that at ninety-one one was permitted to misremember the Ministers of Magic, and that he was not to be pert. She told him about his grandparents, who had lived in the village and whom she had known for forty years, and about his father as a boy, which she remembered better than she remembered last week. She did not mention the night the roof came off, and neither did he. When they left she gave him a book from her own shelf, which was the first edition of her History with her notes in the margins about what she had got wrong, and said that he was to send it back when he had read it, and that she expected him in the spring.

On the bus Lupin asked whether he was all right. Harry said yes. Then, because it was true and Lupin had asked, he said that he had not expected the statue, and that he would need to think about the statue, and Lupin said that he had always found it a bit much himself, and that was the whole of what either of them said about Godric's Hollow for some time.

---

The fourth item was the potion, and Dumbledore read the costing at Mrs Figg's table on the first Tuesday in November and set it down.

"You've written to Damocles Belby."

"Yes. In September. He wrote back. He said he hadn't tried a large batch because there was no demand for one, and that he supposed there was no reason it shouldn't work, and that I was to stop writing to him. I've written to the apothecary in London instead, the one that's the only stockist. I asked what three of the ingredients would cost by the pound rather than the ounce."

"And?"

"Half. At most. One of them, a tenth. They don't sell by the pound because nobody asks."

Dumbledore folded his hands. "What is it you want to do, Harry?"

He had been asked that before, by himself, more often than by anyone else. The list he kept was the answer: the things that were wrong that he knew about, and against each one what it would take to put it right, and which of those he had. Most of the list needed standing nobody would give a four-year-old, and those items waited. The potion was different. What it needed was arithmetic and a brewer, and he had the arithmetic, and brewers could be paid.

"I want the potion to cost less. There are more people like Remus than there are people who can pay for it, and the price is high because nobody's bothered, not because it has to be. I can't brew it. Professor Snape won't let me near anything with aconite in it for years, and he's right. But somebody could brew a month's worth for ten people at once and sell it at what it costs, and I've done the costing; it's two pages. I'd like you to give them to someone who can brew. And I'd like a name of someone who can brew and needs the work, so that if Professor Snape says no I can pay somebody who'll say yes."

Dumbledore read the two pages. He read them more slowly than Harry would have liked, and when he had finished he put them inside his robe.

"I'll give them to Severus. He may say no. He may say yes and not tell me. Either way he'll read them properly, which is more than most people would. And I'll find you a name." He looked at Harry. "You know that if this works it won't be you who gets the credit."

"I don't want the credit. I want it to cost less."

Lupin's reports went on through the autumn, four pages a week on the two afternoons. The apothecaries bought from three growers and a man in Dorset who bred the animals; Honeydukes got its sugar from a Muggle wholesaler in Leeds through a Squib who drove a van; the Ministry's reading room would let a wizard see the register of commercial brewers, which had nine names on it, and the youngest of the nine was sixty. Harry read every page and put it with the rest. He knew more about how the wizarding world was supplied, by Christmas, than anyone who had asked Lupin why he wanted to know, and the thing he knew most clearly was how little of it anyone had looked at.

---

The ink was a page at a time by November.

He had got there by degrees, a line and then three and then a paragraph, and the step from a paragraph to a page had been the hardest, because a page of ink was a great many drops and all of them wanted to be somewhere other than where he put them. He saw the ink and the letters together now, as one thing, and when he had seen a page the ink left the bottle in a thread and went to the paper and lay down and was a page, in a hand that was not his, since his own hand had never been good, but was a hand he had made up and could keep steady. Lupin said it looked like a clerk's.

A page took him the time it took the ink to cross the inch of air and settle, which was a few seconds, and then the time it took him to look at what the ink had done before he saw the next, which was no time at all, and then a few seconds more. His letters to Dumbledore were ten minutes. His letters to Bathilda Bagshot, who wrote nine pages and expected nine back, were an hour, which was the length of one of her paragraphs. He wrote, that autumn, to Dumbledore and Bathilda and Mrs Dennison and Mr Hadley and Hester and the apothecary, and to Mrs Figg's customers in her name when she let him, and to Colin with the Friday list; and to Dudley, once, a letter with the alphabet in it, which his aunt found on the Monday and did not throw away.

McGonagall watched him do it on the second Thursday in November. She had come for the Transfiguration and found him finishing a letter, and she stood in the door of Mrs Figg's kitchen and watched a page arrive out of a bottle and said nothing until it had dried.

"What do you call that?"

"I don't call it anything. It's the ink. I move it."

"That is not a charm I know."

"It isn't a charm. There's no word and no movement. I see where it goes and it goes. I've done it since I was two, with socks."

McGonagall came and picked up the page and held it to the light.

"It is not Transfiguration either," she said. "Nothing has been changed. It has only been moved." She put the page down. "I shall have to think about what it is. In the meantime, Mr Potter, the matches."

He had forty needles for her by then, in a tin. She counted them.

---

He had asked Dumbledore, in November, whether there were other children. Wizard children, his own age, whom he might meet. He had Dudley on Sundays and nobody else under twenty, and he had noticed that, and had thought about it long enough to be sure it mattered and not only to him. Dumbledore had said that there were, that he knew of two or three families he would trust with the knowledge of where Harry was, and that he would think about how. Harry had put it on the list, with a date beside it.

Christmas at Hogwarts was eleven students and the staff, and one of the eleven saw him.

He went up on the twentieth with Lupin, by the Knight Bus, and Dumbledore had given him his old room and a note to Madam Pince, and he was in the library by eight on the twenty-first with the return pile growing and the shoebox of cards open on the desk beside hers. The students who had stayed were mostly older and mostly kept to their common rooms, and Dumbledore had thought that a small boy in the library and at the far end of the staff table would be noticed by nobody who mattered. He was noticed on the twenty-third by a girl of about fifteen with a Ravenclaw scarf, who came into the library for a book and stopped between the shelves and looked at him for a while, at the stack beside him and at his forehead, which he had not thought to cover, and went out again without the book.

She did not speak to him. He learned afterwards from Flitwick, who was her Head of House, that she had written home that evening, and that her mother had written to a cousin, and that the cousin's wife worked at the Prophet. That was in January. In December what happened was Christmas dinner, at one long table with the students at one end and the staff at the other and Harry between Hagrid and Lupin, and crackers that went off with a bang he could watch, and a hat from one of them that Hagrid wore all afternoon, and Madam Pince, at the far end, eating a mince pie.

Snape was at the dinner. He said nothing to Harry across it, but when the table broke up he stopped behind Harry's chair for the time it took to say one sentence, which was that the first batch had been brewed in November for three, that it had cost a third of what Belby charged, and that Lupin had been one of the three and had not been told why his flask was larger. Then he went out.

Flitwick found Harry in the library on Boxing Day, and sat on the floor.

"I haven't got it," he said. "I want to tell you where I've got to, so you don't think I've stopped. The Omniocular charm will hold a page. I've done that; I've a pair that have looked at a page of Borage and will show it back as long as you like. The trouble is what I said in the summer. They show you the page, and you read the page with your eyes, and your eyes are the trouble. What I want is something that gives you a word, and then the next word, in the same place, so the eye needn't move. Nothing I know of does that. I've been through everything in the Charms section and some things that aren't, and I've written to two people who know more than I do." He rubbed his face. "I'm not giving it up. I'm telling you it's a long job."

"How long?"

"I don't know. A year. Years. It may be that it can't be done and I'm the wrong man to find that out." He looked at the stack beside Harry. "How many is that?"

"Since July? Seven hundred and three. Three hundred and ninety of them from here."

Flitwick was quiet for a moment.

"Well," he said. "You've time, then. That's one way of looking at it."

Harry had looked at it that way. He said he did not think much of it. Flitwick laughed, got up off the floor, said he would be back in the New Year, and was.

On the last night of the year, in the room with the window over the lake, Harry went through the eight. The book: written, sold, six hundred pounds, and two more written. Hands: Hester, and five hundred and ten Galleons in an account with Mrs Figg's name on it. The student: Colin, and the rucksack. The trust: up a tenth, and the spring to ask for half. The potion: brewed, for three, at a third. A brewer's name: promised. Children: promised. The reading device: Flitwick's, and years. Five done, two promised, one that was not his to hurry; and the longer list behind these eight, which he went through as well, as he did every night, and found a little shorter than it had been in September, and a great deal longer than he would have liked.
