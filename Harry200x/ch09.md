# Chapter Nine

*January to March 1985*

The Prophet had him on the eleventh of January, on the front page, under a photograph of the castle in snow.

Harry read it on Mrs Figg's kitchen floor at seven, with the owl still on the sill. The headline asked whether the Boy Who Lived was at Hogwarts. The piece under it said that a pupil had seen a small dark-haired boy with a scar in the school library over Christmas, reading; that the boy had been seen at the staff table; that the Headmaster had been asked and had declined to comment; that the Ministry had been asked and had declined to comment; and that if the boy was who the pupil thought he was, then the public had a right to know how he was being brought up, and by whom, and why nobody had been told. There was a second piece on page four, by a different hand, saying that the public had no such right and should be ashamed of itself. There was a letter from a woman in Ottery St Catchpole saying that she had said so two years ago.

Mrs Figg read it after him, standing at the sink.

"Well," she said. "That's that, then."

Dumbledore came at ten, which was not a Tuesday, by the back door, with the paper folded in his coat pocket, and sat down in his chair.

"They'll have the rest of it inside a week whether I speak or not. Arabella's name is in the school's records as the person I asked to watch you, and the records aren't as private as they should be, and a reporter who knows your aunt's name will be on Privet Drive by Friday. I'd rather say something true before they print something wrong. What would you like said?"

"That I'm well. That I'm in your care for part of the year and in my aunt's for part of it. That I read. Not where I live, and not about Mrs Figg, because they'd come to the door. Nothing about how. Nothing that isn't true, and nothing more than that."

"That's very nearly what I had written." Dumbledore took a sheet from his other pocket and gave it to him. It was five sentences. Harry read it and gave it back.

"The fourth sentence says I'm advanced for my age."

"You are."

"It's true. It's also the one they'll print in big letters."

"Yes. I thought about leaving it out. Then I thought that if I did, they'd get it from someone who was at the Christmas table and print that, with trimmings. Better it comes from me, plain." He folded the sheet. "They'll write to you. A great many of them. The school will take the post if you'd rather, or Arabella will, and you needn't answer any of it."

"I'll answer the ones that ask something."

"That may be most of them."

"I know. A page is a few seconds. I'd rather people had an answer than didn't."

The statement was in the Prophet on the Saturday, in a box, with the fourth sentence in big letters above it.

---

The owls started on the Monday.

The school took the post, and Hagrid brought it down to Wisteria Walk in a sack on the Thursday, by Knight Bus. There were three hundred and forty letters in the sack. Harry sorted them on the front-room carpet with Mr Tibbles walking on them, into the ones that asked something and the ones that did not, and the first pile was two hundred and twelve high.

They asked whether he was happy. They asked whether he remembered his parents. They asked what he was reading, and whether he had a wand yet, and what his favourite subject was, and whether he had really done what everyone said he had done that night, and how. Nine of them asked whether he would come to tea. One asked whether he could recommend a book on Charms for a clever girl of eight who had read everything in the house, and he answered that one first, with three titles and why.

He answered all two hundred and twelve in two evenings. The ink lay down a page at a time, and the pages went into envelopes that Mrs Figg addressed, because her hand was neater, and the Luton owls took them out with the parcels. He said that he was well. He said that he remembered a little and that Remus Lupin, who had been his father's friend, told him the rest. He said what he was reading, which was a long list, and gave it. He said that he did not know what had happened that night, that he had been fifteen months old, and that anyone who said they knew was guessing. He said no to tea, politely, nine times, because Dumbledore had asked him to for the present. He did not say where he lived.

Two of the replies were in the Prophet the following week, sent in by their recipients, and printed without comment.

The second sack had four hundred letters in it, and the third two hundred and ninety, and after that it settled at about a hundred a week, which was an evening. In the first sack there had been a parcel done up in brown paper with a jumper in it, green, with an H on the front, and a letter from a woman called Molly Weasley in Ottery St Catchpole, who said that she had a boy his age and a girl a year younger and five more besides, and that if he was ever in Devon there was always room. Harry wore the jumper. He wrote back on the Thursday and asked what the boy and the girl were called.

In the second sack there was a letter on heavy paper with a crest, from Lucius Malfoy of Wiltshire, who wrote with great courtesy that he had known Harry's father slightly at school and had followed Harry's story with interest, that he had a son of Harry's age who would benefit from suitable company, and that the Malfoys would be honoured to receive Harry at his convenience. Harry read it, and then read what was between its sentences, and showed it to Lupin, who read it and gave it back and said nothing for a while.

"What is he?"

"He was one of them. Voldemort had followers, and Malfoy was one of the ones who was never proved to be. He said he'd been under a curse the whole time. The court believed him, or found it convenient to." Lupin turned the letter over, as if there might be more on the back. "He's very rich, and he's on the school's board of governors, and there's nothing you can say to him that won't be repeated."

Harry wrote back that evening. He thanked Mr Malfoy for his letter. He said that he was not at present visiting anyone, on the Headmaster's advice, and that he hoped Mr Malfoy's son was well. He read it to Lupin before it went, and Lupin said, "That'll do," and then, "He'll write again," and he did, in March, and got the same answer.

---

The brewing room made for twelve on the second Saturday in February, and Harry was there.

Lupin took him by Knight Bus to the Leaky Cauldron and through the wall, with the hat down, on a morning with frost on the cobbles and the Alley nearly empty, and they walked the length of it to the wrong end, past the second-hand cauldron shop, to a door with no sign that Hester had painted green. Inside was one room with a flagged floor and a window onto the back of something, three cauldrons on three fires, a table with scales and a knife and jars, and Joan Tilley in a leather apron with her sleeves rolled up over the burns, who looked round when the door opened and said, "Shut that, there's a draught," and went back to the middle cauldron.

Harry shut it, stood by the wall where he had been told to stand, and watched.

He had read everything there was about this potion, which was three books and Belby's paper and the paragraph in the Prophet, and he had done the costing twice and Joan Tilley had corrected it once, and none of that had told him what it looked like, which was blue, or what it smelled of, which was cut grass with something unpleasant underneath. Joan Tilley worked the three cauldrons in turn, with a rhythm he could see the shape of after the first round: stir, lift the lid, look, add, stir, cover, step to the next. She did not hurry and she did not stop. Lupin sat on a stool by the door with the door shut and read a book and did not look at the cauldrons at all, which Harry understood by the second hour.

Snape came at eleven. He came in without knocking, shut the door, and stood where Harry was standing, so that Harry moved, and watched Joan Tilley for a quarter of an hour without speaking. Then he went to the middle cauldron and looked into it, and lifted the lid of the left-hand one and looked into that, and said, "The third is a shade dark. You've had it a minute long."

"I've had it a minute long because the fire's uneven on that side and I've allowed for it," said Joan Tilley, without looking up. "Taste it in an hour and tell me I'm wrong."

Snape did not answer that. He looked at the jars on the table, and the scales, and the sheet of Harry's figures pinned above them with Joan Tilley's corrections in red, and at Harry, and at Lupin, who had not raised his eyes from the book.

"Twelve," Snape said.

"Twelve this month. Thirty by Easter, if the room were bigger."

"The room will not be bigger on my licence until I've seen twelve come out right." He went to the door. "I'll be back at four. If the third is dark at four it goes down the drain and you start again, and the aconite comes out of your wages, not his."

He went. At four he came back and tasted the third and said nothing, and signed the sheet on the wall. Twelve flasks went out that evening by owl to twelve names on Lupin's list, who had each paid what it cost and not a Knut more, and one of them wrote back the following week to say that she had not been able to afford it for six years and had wept in her kitchen, and did Mr Lupin's friend want anything in return. Harry wrote back that he did not.

---

Mr Hadley came on the last Tuesday in February with the figures, and for the first time in Harry's acquaintance with him he looked pleased.

The trust had grown by a sixth since April, dividends in. Oxford had taken the nine ink books at six hundred each and wanted to talk about a series with a name on it and a royalty, and had been told that the name was not available and the royalty was. Harry had written his reasons for the other half of the vault in January, four pages, and sent them to Mr Hadley first, who had found two errors, one of arithmetic and one of law. Harry had corrected both and sent the pages to Dumbledore. Dumbledore had read them and come to Wisteria Walk and said that he would not give Harry half; he would give him a further quarter, which made a little over a third, and he would like Harry to understand why.

"Because if I'm wrong you've lost a third and not half."

"Because if you're wrong, and I've let a four-year-old put half of his inheritance into a market on the strength of four pages, I'll have to explain myself to your father, in my head, and I'd rather it were a third." Dumbledore had looked at the fire. "You'll have half when you've been right for two years."

So Mr Hadley had the papers for the further quarter, and the figures, and a question.

"What do you want it in?"

"The same as before. There's nothing I know that the market doesn't, and I'm not going to pretend there is. Large companies, dividends in, nothing sold without a written reason." Harry paused. "And the publishing man in Oxford. If he wants a series, ask him what he'd want for the company."

Mr Hadley put his pen down. "You want to buy a publisher."

"I want to find out what one costs. He's small and he's good and he's been fair, and he'll be bought by somebody bigger in five years, and I'd rather it was the trust. I don't know if it's sensible. That's what you're for."

Mr Hadley said he would find out, and did. The answer in March was most of what the trust held, and Harry said no, and asked him to ask again in a year.

---

The house in Luton had twelve people in it by March, and Harry went there once a fortnight.

The other two names had come in January, when the first eight had been paid for three months and nothing had blown up, and two more in February, because Harry asked and Dumbledore had stopped arguing. Hester had taken the floor above as well. The stairs smelled of Pepperup and ink. Harry sat at the end of the long table on the second floor while the twelve told him, one at a time, what they had found, and asked each of them three questions, and wrote each a brief for the fortnight before they had finished answering.

Ruth Marchbanks had got to 1700 in the warding books and said that nobody since had added anything but names. Owen Pryce had sent Flitwick eleven pages and got back nine. The brother and sister from Hull had their first numbers on casters who were not Harry, and the numbers said what the first fortnight's had said and more: that everybody failed, and that the failures came in runs. Joan Tilley's assistant had sold four hundred flasks of Pepperup out of the Alley door in February at a third of the price along the street, and the apothecary along the street had come to the door to look and gone away again. Soames's count stood at four hundred and twelve, and he had started to get letters from werewolves who had heard of him, which was not how he had expected to find them and was working better than the back numbers.

Hester's monthly letter said that the Alley room would not be bigger by Easter because Snape would not sign for it, that she had found a second room next door instead, which Snape had not been asked about and need not be until there was something in it, that the lending library had passed six hundred borrowers and wanted a bigger warehouse, and that a wizard in Hogsmeade had asked whether the list of Muggle goods could be printed as a proper booklet with pictures. Harry wrote back yes to all of it, and asked her to find out what Flourish and Blotts would pay for a list, by subject, of every wizarding book currently in print, which nobody had ever made, and which he could make from Madam Pince's cards and the shop's own shelves in about a week.

---

On the second Thursday in March, Augusta Longbottom came to Wisteria Walk with her grandson.

Dumbledore had written to say she was coming, and who she was: that her son and his wife were in St Mungo's and had been for three years and would not be coming out, and why; that the boy was Harry's age to the day but one; and that Augusta had asked Dumbledore directly whether the Potter boy was real, and Dumbledore had said yes, and she had said that in that case Neville had better meet him before the Prophet did. Dumbledore had added that Augusta was a formidable woman.

Her hat had a stuffed vulture on it. Harry looked at it while Mrs Figg got the door shut, and then he looked at Neville.

Neville was a round, pale boy in a coat bought for him to grow into, standing half behind his grandmother with his hands in his pockets, and he looked at Harry the way Dudley had looked at the encyclopaedia salesman. Harry had Dudley, and a hundred and forty letters from children in the sacks, and none of it told him what to say to a boy who had been made to come. So he said, "There's a cat under the sideboard called Tufty who won't come out. Mrs Figg says she comes out for people she likes. Nobody's found out who that is yet."

Neville looked at the sideboard.

Augusta Longbottom took the chair that was not Mrs Figg's without being offered it, and accepted tea, and asked Harry four questions in the first ten minutes: what he was reading, who taught him, what he thought of the Prophet, and whether it was true that he had read the whole of Dumbledore's library. He answered the first three and corrected the fourth, with the number, and she looked at him over her cup and said, "I see," and then talked to Mrs Figg about cats for a quarter of an hour while Harry and Neville sat on the hearthrug and Tufty did not come out.

Neville did not say much. Harry found, after a while, that he did not need to. He told Neville about the bottle, and the sock, and the light, in order, the way he would have told Dumbledore, and Neville listened with his hands in his pockets, and at the end of it said, "I can't do anything."

"Nothing's happened? Not once?"

"Great-Uncle Algie dropped me off the pier at Blackpool to make something happen. I nearly drowned. Gran says it'll come." He looked at the hearthrug. "She says it to Great-Uncle Algie, not to me."

Harry had a pebble in his pocket that he had picked up in the garden because it was round. He put it on the hearthrug between them.

"Can you see this? Not look at it. See it. Where it is, and where it would be if it was an inch higher. Both at once, as clearly as you can. Don't want it to move. Just see it there and see it higher."

Neville looked at the pebble. His face did what Dudley's had done over the letter P. Nothing happened. Harry had not expected anything to; he had wanted Neville to be looking at the pebble rather than the floor, and he was.

"Now tell me what it looks like an inch higher. Describe it. The shadow underneath. What's behind it that you can see now and couldn't before."

Neville described it. He was better at it than Harry had expected, because Neville noticed things, and when he had described the shadow and the bit of hearthrug that would show under the pebble and the way the firelight would catch the underside, the pebble was an inch higher.

It stayed there about as long as it took Neville to see that it had, and then it fell, and Neville made a sound, and Augusta Longbottom stopped in the middle of a sentence about Kneazles.

"Do it again," she said.

Neville could not do it again that afternoon. He tried four times and the pebble lay there. Harry told him that the light had not come again for many nights after the first time, which was true, and Neville did not seem to mind. He looked at the pebble as if it had been given to him.

At the door Augusta put her hand on Harry's shoulder and looked down at him from under the vulture.

"My grandson will write to you. You'll answer him."

"Yes."

"Good." She looked past him at Mrs Figg, and back. "That child is not four, Mrs Figg, whatever his papers say. I don't know what he is and I don't propose to ask Albus Dumbledore, who wouldn't tell me. I'll say this. If he's going to do what I think he's going to do, he'll want people who don't frighten, and I don't."

She took Neville's hand and went down the step. Neville looked back once, at the window, where Tufty was sitting on the sill, having come out at some point during the afternoon without anyone seeing her do it.

---

Lupin began teaching him the Patronus Charm in February, because Harry had asked in January what Azkaban was like and Lupin had told him, as far as he knew, which was from one visit to its gates, and Harry had then asked what the defence was. There was one, Lupin said. It was difficult. He had taught it to thirteen-year-olds. The charm wanted a happy memory held whole. Harry could hold a thing whole; what he lacked was the memory. He tried the photograph first, which did not work, because it was of a thing he had not been there for. He tried Mrs Figg's hearthrug and the first book from the crate, and got a thread of silver that hung in the air of the kitchen for a second and went out. Lupin, who had not got that from a thirteen-year-old in under a month, sat down.

And on the last Tuesday in March, Dumbledore brought the letter from Ottery St Catchpole that Harry had been expecting since the jumper, in Molly Weasley's hand, with a drawing on the back by one of the children of a house that leaned, and the words in capitals: AUGUST. ALL OF YOU. DON'T BRING ANYTHING.
