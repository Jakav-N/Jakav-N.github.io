# Chapter Ten

*April to August 1985*

Flitwick had the card linked to the page by Easter, and he could not make it behave.

He showed Harry on the first morning of the holiday, on the library floor, with Madam Pince watching from her desk and not objecting, which was new. The card was the size of a playing card and plain white. The page was a page of Borage, torn out, which had cost Flitwick something to do. He laid the page on the floor and the card beside it and touched each with his wand, and the card went grey and then showed the page, the whole of it, small, as a photograph would have.

"That's the link. Now watch." He touched the card again, and the page on the card began to move, as the pictures in the Prophet moved: the lines of print slid upward, slowly, off the top, and the lower lines came up to replace them. "That's the rate charm off the Omnioculars. I can make it go as slow as you like or as fast. But it moves the whole page. I want it to show one word, here, in the middle, and then the next word in the same place. I've been at that since Christmas and I can't make it choose a word. It doesn't know what a word is."

Harry watched the print slide up the card at a slow walk. He could read it as it went, and it was no faster than the page.

"Could it know what a line is?"

Flitwick stopped. "A line."

"If it showed one line at a time, in the same place. A line is about ten words. My eyes would still have to go along it, but they wouldn't have to go down, and they wouldn't have to find the start of the next one, and that's half of what they do. It isn't what you want. It might be half of it."

Flitwick sat back on his heels and looked at the card, and at the torn page, and said, "A line is a thing I can show it," and took the card away, and did not come to dinner.

He came to breakfast the next morning with the card and gave it to Harry without a word. Harry held it over the page and touched it with his own wand as Flitwick showed him, and the card showed a line, one line, in the middle of the white, in print the size of a book's. He touched it again and it showed the next. He found the rate charm's dial, which was a scratch on the back that moved under a thumb, and turned it, and the lines came by themselves, one after another, faster as he turned, until they were coming at a pace at which his eyes could just travel each one before the next replaced it. He read the page in a third of the time it took him from paper.

"It isn't a word," Flitwick said.

"It's three times. The library's a year and a bit instead of three." Harry turned the card over and looked at the scratch. "And it isn't finished. You said it doesn't know what a word is. Does it know what a gap is? Because if it can see a line it can see the gaps in the line, and a word is what's between two gaps."

Flitwick took the card back and did not come to dinner that day either. The word did not come by the end of the holiday, or by the end of the summer. The line did. He made four cards, one for each of the texts Harry was reading, and Madam Pince allowed them in the library on the understanding that they touched nothing but the page they were linked to, and from Easter Harry read a book in twenty-five minutes.

---

Bathilda Bagshot got her History back in April with his notes on her notes, and wrote to say that he was impertinent and correct in about equal measure, and that she expected him in May. He went.

The visit was shorter than the first because she tired, and it gave him more, because she had decided over the winter what to tell him, which was the village in the war: who had left and who had stayed, who had been on which side without saying so, and which of the people he read about in the Prophet had been boys she had watched fight in the square. She said nothing about Dumbledore's family, who had lived in the village, and Harry, who had read that in her own book, noticed her not saying it and let it be.

He went to the grave on the way back, with Lupin, and stood there, and found that the second time was not easier or harder, only the second time.

On the bus he asked Lupin about Sirius Black. He had asked before, in pieces. Now he asked for all of it, and Lupin gave him all of it, from a train in 1971 to a night in 1981, over the length of Somerset, with his hands round his knees and his eyes on the window. At the end Harry asked whether there had been a trial.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it was November 1981 and there were two hundred people in the cells and Crouch was sending them to Azkaban in batches. Because twelve Muggles were dead in a street and Peter's finger was in the gutter and Sirius was standing in the middle of it laughing. Because everybody knew." Lupin's hands tightened on his knees. "Because I knew. I'd known him ten years and I knew it the moment they told me. Don't ask me how. I didn't need a trial and neither did anyone else."

Harry did not ask how. He asked Dumbledore instead, the next Tuesday, for the record of what had been done in Sirius Black's case, and Dumbledore said there was no record, because nothing had been done, and that he had not objected at the time, and had thought about that since. Harry put it on the list, low. There had been no trial, which was a fact about the Ministry. Everything else he had was a face from a broken doorway and Lupin's word, and he did not know how to weigh either.

---

The spell he tried to make did not take, and he had expected that, and tried anyway.

The library held very little on how spells came to be, and what it held was old: that a wizard who wished to make a spell bound an effect to a word and a movement by a method that none of the books agreed on, that it took years, that most who tried failed, and that the ones who succeeded could not say afterwards what they had done differently. Lupin had told him the Half-Blood Prince's hexes were the only new spells he had ever seen made and that the Prince would not say how.

The effect he chose was the smallest he had: making Flitwick's card show its next line. He could do it with a touch of the wand. He wanted it to happen on a word, so that his hands could stay on the book. He picked the word, which was the sound he had made to himself at the end of each line since the cot, in no language; and a movement, which was the smallest flick his wrist could make. He had Lupin keep count.

For six weeks, every evening, he held the card and the effect and the word and the flick together in his mind, as whole as he could hold them, and said the word and made the flick, and the card showed its next line every time; and every time it did so because he had pictured it doing so, and not because of the word, which he proved by saying the word with his mind on something else, whereupon the card did nothing. The word did not take. The flick did not take. The effect stayed in his head and would not go into the sound.

He wrote it up for Dumbledore in July, four pages: what he had tried, how many times, what had happened, and what he thought it meant, which was not much. Dumbledore read it at Mrs Figg's table and put it inside his robe with the others.

"Keep writing these," he said. "Every one. Nobody ever has."

---

The three weeks at Privet Drive were in July again, and were different, because Dudley could read.

He read slowly and aloud with his finger under the words, as his mother had read to him, and he read to her: a Ladybird book on the first evening, with Harry beside him on the settee saying nothing and his aunt in her chair with her mending in her lap and her hands still. When Dudley finished she said, "Well done, Diddy," in the voice that was for Dudley, and then looked past him at Harry, and for a moment her face was not set, and then she picked up her mending.

His uncle said at dinner that it was about time, that he had read at five himself, and that Dudley was a Dursley. Harry said nothing. He had brought nothing into the house except what was in his head, and he used none of it where anyone could see, and at night in the cupboard he did not write, because there was nothing now that could not wait three weeks for ink. He slept. He thought about the card, and the gap between two words. On the second Sunday his aunt put a library book on the arm of the settee beside him, from the children's section, without a word, and went into the kitchen, and he read it in eleven minutes and thanked her at tea, and she said, "Yes, well."

The house in Luton had twenty people in it by July, and a letter from the Ministry, which Hester sent on to him at Privet Drive by owl, which his aunt saw and said nothing about.

The letter was addressed to Miss H. Pye at the newsagent's, and it asked, in the Department of Magical Law Enforcement's politest form, what the nature of the establishment above the shop was, who was employed there, under what licences any potions were brewed, and whether the Department might send someone to look. Hester's note said she proposed to answer truthfully and would he like to see the answer first. He would. Her answer said that the establishment was a private house of research and manufacture owned by Miss Pye; that it employed twenty witches and wizards, whose names were attached; that the only restricted potion brewed under its direction was Wolfsbane, under licence to Professor S. Snape of Hogwarts; that the Department was welcome to send someone at any hour; and that the money was Mr H. J. Potter's, who was five next week and would be glad to answer any further questions by owl. Harry read it twice over, once for what it said and once for what it did not, and sent it back with one word on the bottom, which was yes.

The Department sent a young man in a bowler hat on a Thursday. He went through all four floors and the Alley room, and left, Hester wrote, looking as if he had been told something he would have to repeat to someone who would not believe him, and did not write again that year.

---

He was five on the thirty-first, at Hogwarts, where Lupin had brought him the day before. Hagrid's cake was the same cake. The owls were not the same owls: there were a hundred and forty on the day, and the school's owlery had to send for more perches. He answered them in two evenings. Among them was Neville's sixth, which said that the pebble had gone up twice more and that his gran had stopped saying it would come, and a first from a boy called Ron Weasley, which said: Mum says to tell you we have a ghoul. See you in August.

Flitwick's cards went into Flourish and Blotts' window in the last week of August. Not the word; the line. Owen Pryce and a retired Charms master in Hogsmeade whom Dumbledore had found had made two hundred of them to Flitwick's method over the summer, each linked to nothing until its owner touched it to a page, with the scratch on the back for the rate. The shop put them in the window at nine Sickles, with a card beside them saying whose charm it was, which was Flitwick's, and whose idea, which was Harry's, because his name was public now and Flitwick had refused to have the card without it. They sold out in four days to the parents of first-years, and the shop ordered five hundred, and the man in Hogsmeade took on two assistants.

---

The Burrow was a house that should have fallen down and had not, and Harry stood at the bottom of its garden on the second Saturday in August and looked at it while Lupin shut the gate.

It was five storeys and leaned, and each storey had been added by someone who had not consulted the one below, and there were boots on the step and a sign that said BEWARE OF THE GHOUL in a child's hand. The garden was full of children. Harry had never been anywhere full of children. There were five in the garden and two faces at a window, and one of the five was coming at him across the grass.

"Are you him? Fred says you're him. I said you'd be taller."

"I'm five."

"I'm five. Fred's seven. Ron's over there, he's five too, he's hiding because Mum made him wash." The boy was red-haired and had his hands on his hips. "I'm George."

Molly Weasley came out of the house drying her hands and looked at Harry with her head on one side. Then she came down the step and across the grass and put her arms round him without asking, which nobody had done, and said over his head to Lupin, "He's too thin, Remus, does that woman feed him," and Lupin said that Mrs Figg fed him a great deal, and Molly said, "Hm," and took him inside.

The house inside was as the outside had promised. There was a clock with nine hands and no numbers; there was a knitting pattern knitting itself in a chair; there was a smell of bread. Arthur Weasley came in from a shed at the bottom of the garden with oil on his hands and shook Harry's with one of them before Molly could stop him, and said that he was very glad, very glad indeed, that he worked at the Ministry in the Misuse of Muggle Artefacts Office, and did Harry by any chance know how a thing called a fuse worked, because he had three and could not make them do anything.

Harry knew how a fuse worked. He told him. Arthur sat down.

"I've been buying," Arthur said after a while, "from a woman in Surrey. By owl. Pens and torches and so forth. Molly says I've bought enough to open a shop. My office had a complaint about her in March, as it happens, from a man who sells quills, and I went to Luton myself to see whether anything there was enchanted, and nothing was, and I bought a flask." He looked at Lupin. "Remus, Albus said something at Christmas about Arabella Figg that I didn't follow. That's not the same Arabella Figg."

"It is," said Harry. "The pens are mine. Mrs Figg's name is on the sheet because mine can't be. Hester Pye in Luton does the parcels. You've had forty-one pens and six torches and a flask since January last year, and you asked twice whether there were other colours, and there are now."

Arthur looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, and went on laughing, and had to sit down again, and Molly said, "Arthur," and Arthur said, "Molly, I've been writing to a four-year-old about pens for eighteen months," and Molly put a plate in front of Harry with more on it than he had eaten in a day.

Ron came out from wherever he had been washed, red to the ears, sat down opposite Harry, and looked at him across the table. Harry looked back and waited.

"Do you play Exploding Snap?"

"No. I've read about it."

"Reading about it doesn't help. I'll show you."

He showed him. Harry lost four times, because the game was about speed, and his hands were as slow as anyone's, and about reading the other player's face, which Ron did without knowing he did it and Harry had never learned to do. Ron was delighted, and then sorry for being delighted, and then delighted again, and said that Harry could be on his side against the twins, who cheated.

Fred and George had him in the orchard after lunch. They wanted to know about the two charms on one object, which Lupin had mentioned to their father and their father had mentioned at dinner, and Harry told them about the quill and the hole in Mrs Figg's tablecloth and what Lupin had said. Fred said that they had put a Tickling Charm and a Hiccoughing Charm on the same frog and the frog had done something that was neither. George said the frog was all right now. Harry asked whether they had written down what the frog had done, and they looked at each other, and George went and found a bit of paper.

Ginny watched from the stairs. She was four and did not come down and did not look away, and Harry, going up to see the ghoul, said as he passed, "I'm Harry. You don't have to come down," and she said, "I know who you are," and went on watching.

The snake was in the long grass by the pond in the late afternoon, and Harry heard it before he saw it.

He heard it as a sound in the grass, as he heard the cats on the stairs, except that this sound had words in it, and the words were that the ground was warm and the pond was cold and there was a boy. He stopped. He looked down, and the snake was a grass snake, a yard long, looking up, and he said to it that he was the boy and that he was not going to step on it, and heard himself say it, and heard that what he had said was not English.

Ron, behind him, said, "What was that?"

Harry turned round. Ron was looking at him, not at the snake.

"You hissed at it. You hissed and it hissed back. Fred! Harry hissed at a snake."

The snake was going away into the grass, saying nothing he could hear. He told Ron that he did not know what that had been, which was true, and that he would ask Professor Dumbledore, which he did, that evening, in the Burrow's kitchen, where Dumbledore had come by Floo to take him home and was eating a scone.

Dumbledore put the scone down.

He said nothing for long enough that Molly stopped what she was doing at the stove. Then he asked Harry to tell him exactly what the snake had said and exactly what Harry had said back, and Harry told him, and Dumbledore listened without his face moving, and when it was done he said, "Thank you," and nothing else, and took Harry home through the fire, and said nothing in Mrs Figg's front room either, except goodnight.

He came back on the Tuesday and told Harry what he knew, which was short. There was a thing called Parseltongue, the speech of snakes, and very few wizards could understand or speak it. It ran in one family above all, the family of Salazar Slytherin, who had founded one of the school's houses a thousand years ago. The last person Dumbledore knew to have had it was Voldemort. Dumbledore did not know why Harry had it. He had an idea, and it was not one he was going to give Harry, because he was not sure enough of it and because it would do Harry no good to carry.

"Is it dangerous? To me."

"The speaking? No. Snakes aren't dangerous company, on the whole. What people will think when they hear of it is another matter, and I'd keep it to the Weasleys and this room."

"Ron heard."

"Ron is five and has six brothers and sisters and a ghoul. I don't think Ron will be the problem."

Harry read everything the library had on Parseltongue in the week that followed, which was eleven books and a pamphlet, and none of it explained why a boy whose parents could not speak to snakes could. He read what there was on Slytherin, which was more and worse. He read, because the Prophet had used the word once in a piece about Voldemort's followers, what was said about what the Dark Arts did to the people who used them, and found nothing about snakes and a great deal about souls, written by people who had not known what they were talking about and had said so at length. He put the question on the part of the list that needed magic nobody had, which was the part he went down every night and could do nothing about, and which had one more thing on it than it had had in July.
