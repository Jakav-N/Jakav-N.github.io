# Chapter Ten

*April to August 1985*

Flitwick had the card linked to the page by Easter, and he could not make it behave.

He showed Harry on the first morning of the holiday, on the library floor, with Madam Pince watching from her desk and not objecting, which was new. The card was the size of a playing card and plain white. The page was a page of Borage, torn out, which had cost Flitwick something to do. He laid the page on the floor and the card beside it and touched each with his wand, and the card went grey and then showed the page, the whole of it, small, as a photograph would have.

"That's the link. Protean, more or less, with the picture going one way and nothing coming back. Now watch." He touched the card again, and the page on the card began to move, as the pictures in the Prophet moved: the lines of print slid upward, slowly, and off the top, and the lower lines came up to replace them. "That's the rate charm off the Omnioculars. I can make it go as slow as you like or as fast. But it moves the whole page. I want it to show one word. One word, here, in the middle, and then the next word in the same place, and the next. I've been at that since Christmas and I can't make it choose a word. It doesn't know what a word is. It knows what a page is because I showed it one."

Harry looked at the card. The print slid up it at the pace Flitwick had set, which was a slow walk, and he could read it as it went, and it was no faster than reading the page.

"Could it know what a line is?"

Flitwick stopped. "A line."

"If it showed one line at a time, in the same place. A line is about ten words. My eyes would still have to go along the line, but they wouldn't have to go down, and they wouldn't have to find the start of the next one, and that's half of what they do. It wouldn't be what you want. It might be half of it."

Flitwick sat back on his heels and looked at the card for a while, and then at the torn page, and then he said, "A line is a thing I can show it," and took the card away, and did not come to dinner.

He came to breakfast the next morning with the card in his hand and gave it to Harry without saying anything, and Harry held it over the Borage page and touched it with his own wand as Flitwick showed him, and the card showed a line. One line, in the middle of the white, in print the size of a book's. He touched it again and it showed the next. He found the rate charm's dial, which was a scratch on the back of the card that moved under a thumb, and turned it, and the lines began to come by themselves, one after another, at the pace of a slow walk, and then faster as he turned it, until they were coming at a pace at which his eyes could just travel each one before the next replaced it.

He read the page of Borage in a third of the time it took him from paper. Then he read it again from the paper to be sure, and it was a third.

"It's not a word," Flitwick said. "It's not what you wanted."

"It's three times. The library's a year and a bit now instead of three." Harry turned the card over and looked at the scratch. "And it's not finished. You said it doesn't know what a word is. Does it know what a space is? Between the words. Because if it can see a line it can see the gaps in the line, and a word is what's between two gaps."

Flitwick took the card back and did not come to dinner again that day either, and the word did not come by the end of the holiday, or by the end of the summer. The line did. Flitwick made four cards, one for each of the texts Harry was reading, and Madam Pince allowed them in the library on the understanding that they touched nothing but the page they were linked to, and from Easter Harry read a book in twenty-five minutes.

---

Bathilda Bagshot got her History back in April with his notes on her notes, nine pages of them, and wrote to say that he was impertinent and correct in roughly equal measure, and that she expected him in May, and he went.

The visit was shorter than the first because she tired, and longer than the first in what it gave him, because she had thought since October about what to tell him and had decided to tell him about the village in the war: who had left and who had stayed, who had been on which side without saying so, and which of the people he read about in the Prophet had been boys she had seen fight in the square. She did not say anything about Dumbledore's family, and Harry, who had read in her own History that Dumbledore's family had lived in Godric's Hollow, noticed her not saying it and let it be.

He went to the grave on the way back, with Lupin, and stood there, and did not talk to it, and found that the second time was not easier and not harder, only the second time.

On the bus he asked Lupin about Sirius Black. He had asked before, in pieces; now he asked for all of it, and Lupin gave him all of it, from a train in 1971 to a night in 1981, over the length of Somerset, with his hands round his knees and his eyes on the window, and at the end of it Harry asked whether there had been a trial.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it was November 1981 and there were two hundred people in the cells and Crouch was sending them to Azkaban in batches. Because twelve Muggles were dead in a street and Peter's finger was in the gutter and Sirius was standing in the middle of it laughing. Because everybody knew." Lupin's hands tightened on his knees. "Because I knew. I'd known him for ten years and I knew it the moment they told me. Don't ask me how, Harry. I didn't need a trial and neither did anyone else."

Harry did not ask how. He asked Dumbledore instead, on the next Tuesday, for the record of what had been done in Sirius Black's case, and Dumbledore said that there was no record, because nothing had been done, and that he had not objected at the time, and that he had thought about that since. Harry put it on the list, and put it low. There had been no trial, which was a fact about the Ministry. Everything else he had was a face from a broken doorway and Lupin's word, and he did not know how to weigh either.

---

The spell he tried to make did not take, and he had expected that, and tried anyway.

He had read what the library held on how spells came to be, which was very little and mostly old: that a wizard who wished to make a spell bound an effect to a word and a movement by a method that none of the books agreed on, that it took years, that most who tried failed, and that the ones who succeeded could not say afterwards what they had done differently. Lupin had told him the Half-Blood Prince's hexes were the only new spells he had ever seen made and that the Prince would not say how. McGonagall had told him not to try anything living and had not said anything about trying this.

The effect he chose was the smallest he had: making Flitwick's card show its next line. He could do it with a touch of the wand, as Flitwick had taught him. He wanted it to happen on a word, so that his hands could stay on the book. He picked the word, which was the one he had been saying to himself at the end of each line since the cot, in no language, a sound with no spelling; and a movement, which was the smallest flick his wrist could make. He had Lupin keep the counts.

For six weeks, every evening, he held the card and the effect and the word and the flick together in his mind, as whole as he could hold them, and said the word and made the flick, and the card showed its next line every time; and every time it was because he had pictured it showing the next line, and not because of the word, which he proved by saying the word with his mind on something else, upon which the card did nothing. The word did not take. The flick did not take. The effect stayed his, in his head, and would not go into the sounds.

He wrote it up for Dumbledore in July, four pages: what he had tried, how many times, what had happened, what he thought it meant, which was not much. Dumbledore read the four pages at Mrs Figg's table and turned back to the second page and read it again.

"You've written down a failure."

"It's what happened. If I only wrote down what worked, you'd think I was better at this than I am."

"Nobody writes down a failure, Harry. In four hundred years of this school I don't think anyone has ever handed me one." Dumbledore folded the pages and put them in his robe with the others. "Keep it. Keep every one. In twenty years they'll be worth more than the successes to anyone who wants to know what's actually true about this, and I'd like them to have your name on."

---

The three weeks at Privet Drive were in July again, and were different, because Dudley could read.

He read slowly and aloud and with his finger under the words, as his mother had read to him, and he read to her: a Ladybird book on the first evening, with Harry beside him on the settee saying nothing, and his aunt in her chair with her mending in her lap and her hands still. When Dudley finished she said, "Well done, Diddy," in a voice that was for Dudley, and then looked past him at Harry, and for a moment her face was not set, and then she picked up her mending.

His uncle said at dinner that it was about time, and that he had read at five himself, and that Dudley was a Dursley. Harry said nothing. He had brought nothing into the house, as he had promised, except what was in his head, and he did not use any of it where anyone could see, and at night in the cupboard he did not write, because there was nothing now he needed to write that could not wait three weeks for ink. He slept. He thought about the card, and the gap between two words, and whether Flitwick's charm could be told that a gap was a thing. On the second Sunday his aunt put a book on the arm of the settee beside him without a word, a library book, from the children's section, and went into the kitchen, and he read it in eleven minutes and thanked her at tea, and she said, "Yes, well."

The house had twenty people in it by July, and a letter from the Ministry.

The letter was addressed to Miss H. Pye at the newsagent's in Luton and asked, in the Department of Magical Law Enforcement's politest form, what the nature of the establishment above the shop was, who was employed there, under what licences any potions were brewed, and whether the Department might send someone to look. Hester sent it to Harry by owl with a note that said she proposed to answer it truthfully and would he like to see the answer first. He would. The answer said that the establishment was a private house of research and manufacture owned by Miss Pye, that it employed twenty witches and wizards whose names were attached, that the only restricted potion brewed on the premises or elsewhere under its direction was Wolfsbane under licence to Professor S. Snape of Hogwarts, that the Department was welcome to send someone at any hour, and that the money was Mr H. J. Potter's, who was five and would be glad to answer any further questions by owl. The Department sent a young man in a bowler hat on a Thursday, who went through all four floors and the Alley room and left looking as if he had been told something he would have to repeat to someone who would not believe him, and did not write again that year.

Flitwick's cards went on sale in the last week of August. Not the word; the line. The charms reader in Luton and a retired Charms master in Hogsmeade whom Dumbledore had found had made two hundred of them to Flitwick's method over the summer, each linked to nothing until its owner touched it to a page, with the scratch on the back for the rate, and Flourish and Blotts put them in the window at nine Sickles with a card beside them that said whose charm it was, which was Flitwick's, and whose idea, which was Harry's, because his name was public now and Flitwick had refused to have the card without it. They sold out in four days to first-years' parents, and the shop ordered five hundred, and the Hogsmeade charms master took on two assistants.

He was five on the thirty-first, at Hogwarts, where Lupin had brought him the day before. Hagrid's cake was the same cake. The owls were not the same owls. There had been a sack at Christmas and a sack in January; there were a hundred and forty on his birthday alone, and the school's owlery had to send for more perches. He answered them in two evenings. Among them was one from Neville, which was his sixth, and said that the pebble had gone up twice more and that his gran had stopped saying it would come, and one from a boy called Ron Weasley, which was his first, and said: Mum says to tell you we have a ghoul. See you in August.

---

The Burrow was a house that should have fallen down and had not, and Harry stood at the bottom of its garden on the second Saturday in August and looked at it for the length of time it took Lupin to shut the gate.

It was five storeys and leaned, and each storey had been added by someone who had not consulted the one below, and there were boots on the step and a sign that said BEWARE OF THE GHOUL in a child's hand. The garden was full of children. Harry had never been anywhere full of children. He counted them as he looked, since he could not help it, and there were five in the garden and two faces at a window, and one of the five was coming at him across the grass.

"Are you him? Fred says you're him. I said you'd be taller."

"I'm five."

"I'm five. Fred's seven. Ron's over there, he's five too, he's hiding because Mum made him wash." The boy was red-haired and had his hands on his hips. "I'm George."

Molly Weasley came out of the house drying her hands and looked at Harry with her head on one side, and then came down the step and across the grass and put her arms round him without asking, which nobody had done, and said over his head to Lupin, "He's too thin, Remus, does that woman feed him," and Lupin said that Mrs Figg fed him a great deal, and Molly said, "Hm," in a way Harry recognised, and took him inside.

The house inside was as the outside had promised. There was a clock with nine hands and no numbers; there was a knitting pattern knitting itself in a chair; there was a smell of bread. Arthur Weasley came in from a shed at the bottom of the garden with oil on his hands and shook Harry's with one of them before Molly could stop him, and said that he was very glad, very glad indeed, and that he worked at the Ministry in the Misuse of Muggle Artefacts Office, and did Harry by any chance know how a thing called a fuse worked, because he had three and could not make them do anything.

Harry knew how a fuse worked. He told him. Arthur Weasley sat down.

"I've been buying," Arthur said, after a while, "from a woman in Surrey. By owl. Pens and torches and so forth. Molly says I've bought enough to open a shop. My office had a complaint about her in March, as it happens, from a man who sells quills, and I went to Luton myself to see whether anything there was enchanted, and nothing was, and I bought a flask." He looked at Lupin. "Remus, Albus said something at Christmas that I didn't follow, about Arabella Figg. That's not the same Arabella Figg."

"It is," said Harry. "The pens are mine. Mrs Figg's name is on the sheet because mine can't be. Hester Pye in Luton does the parcels. You've had forty-one pens and six torches and a flask since January last year, and you asked twice if there were other colours, and there are now."

Arthur Weasley looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, and went on laughing, and had to sit down again, and Molly said, "Arthur," and Arthur said, "Molly, I've been writing to a four-year-old about pens for eighteen months," and Molly put a plate in front of Harry with more on it than he had eaten in a day.

Ron came out from wherever he had been washed, red to the ears, and sat down opposite Harry and looked at him across the table, and Harry looked back and waited, which he was good at.

"Do you play Exploding Snap?"

"No. I've read about it."

"Reading about it doesn't help. I'll show you."

He showed him. Harry lost four times, because the game was about speed and his hands were as slow as anyone's, and about reading the other player's face, which Ron did without knowing he did it and Harry had never learned to do. Ron was delighted, and then sorry for being delighted, and then delighted again, and then said that Harry could be on his side against the twins, who cheated, and that was that.

Fred and George had him in the orchard after lunch. They wanted to know about the two charms on one object, which Lupin had mentioned to their father and their father had mentioned at dinner and they had not stopped thinking about since. Harry told them about the quill, and the hole in Mrs Figg's tablecloth, and what Lupin had said, and what he had found out since, which was that the counts mattered and the order mattered and that nobody had written any of it down. Fred said that they had put a Tickling Charm and a Hiccoughing Charm on the same frog and the frog had done something that was neither. George said that the frog was all right now. Harry asked whether they had written down what the frog had done, and they looked at each other, and George went and found a bit of paper.

Ginny watched from the stairs. She was four, and did not come down, and did not look away, and Harry, going up to see the ghoul, said as he passed, "I'm Harry. You don't have to come down," and she said, "I know who you are," and went on watching.

The snake was in the long grass by the pond in the late afternoon, and Harry heard it before he saw it.

He heard it the way he heard the owls on the sill and the cats on the stairs, as a sound in the grass, except that this sound had words in it, and the words were that the ground was warm and the pond was cold and there was a boy. He stopped. He looked down, and the snake was a grass snake, a yard long, looking up, and he said to it that he was the boy and that he was not going to step on it, and heard himself say it, and heard that what he had said was not English.

Ron, behind him, said, "What was that?"

Harry turned round. Ron was looking at him, not at the snake.

"You hissed at it. You hissed and it hissed back. Fred, Harry hissed at a snake."

Harry looked at the snake again. It was going away into the grass, saying nothing he could hear. He said to Ron that he did not know what that had been, which was true, and that he would ask Professor Dumbledore, which he did, that evening, in the kitchen at the Burrow, where Dumbledore had come by Floo to take him home and was eating a scone.

Dumbledore put the scone down.

He did not say anything for long enough that Molly Weasley stopped what she was doing at the stove. Then he asked Harry to tell him exactly what the snake had said and exactly what Harry had said back, and Harry told him, and Dumbledore listened with his face not moving, and when it was done he said, "Thank you," and nothing else, and took Harry home through the fire, and did not speak in Mrs Figg's front room either, except to say goodnight.

He came back on the Tuesday and sat in his chair and told Harry what he knew, which was short. There was a thing called Parseltongue, which was the speech of snakes, and a very few wizards could understand and speak it. It ran in one family above all, which was the family of Salazar Slytherin, who had founded one of the school's houses a thousand years ago. The last person Dumbledore knew to have had it was Voldemort. Dumbledore did not know why Harry had it. He had an idea, and it was not an idea he was going to give Harry, because he was not sure enough of it and because it would do Harry no good to carry it.

"Is it dangerous? To me."

"The speaking? No. Snakes are not dangerous company, on the whole. What people will think when they hear of it is another matter, and I'd keep it to the Weasleys and this room."

"Ron heard."

"Ron is five and has six brothers and sisters and a ghoul. I don't think Ron will be the problem."

Harry read everything the library had on Parseltongue in the week that followed, which was eleven books and a pamphlet, and none of it explained why a boy whose parents could not speak to snakes could. He read what there was on Slytherin, which was more and worse. He read, because the Prophet had used the word once in a piece about Voldemort's followers, about what the Dark Arts were said to do to the people who used them, and found nothing about snakes and a great deal about souls, written by people who had not known what they were talking about and had said so at length. He put the question on the list, in the part of the list that needed magic nobody had, which was the part he went down every night and could do nothing about, and which had one more thing on it now than it had had in July.
