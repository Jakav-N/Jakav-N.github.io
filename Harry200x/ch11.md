# Chapter Eleven

*September to December 1985*

Hester had painted the shelves herself, and there was still paint on her thumb when she unlocked the door.

It was a quarter to nine on the first Saturday in September and the Alley outside was grey and nearly empty. Harry sat on a stool behind the counter with his hat down and his feet a long way from the floor, and watched her go round the room touching things: the pens in their jars, six colours; the torches in a row with their batteries beside them; the flasks; the tape; the lined notebooks, which had turned out to want explaining; the three hundred Muggle novels along the back wall with a card tucked in each; the jars of dried herbs on the shelf behind the till, which were the same herbs the apothecaries sold at the good end of the Alley and had come from a Muggle seed merchant in Bedford at a fifth of the price, with Snape's verdict on each written on the jar in Hester's hand; the box of Flitwick's cards by the till with a sign in Flitwick's hand. She straightened the sign. She had straightened it twice already.

"You don't have to stay," she said, without turning round. "You've seen it."

"I've seen it empty. I haven't seen anyone buy anything."

"You've got the figures."

"The figures are what people wrote on a sheet and sent an owl about. That's not the same as watching them pick a thing up and put it down again." He looked at the window. "You said I should see it once from this side. I'm seeing it."

Hester said, "Hm," which she had got from Mrs Figg, and went to unlock the door.

Dennis arrived with the key still turning. He was nineteen and Muggle-born and had answered one of the house's briefs so well that Harry had asked to meet him, and he came in with his collar up and a paper bag of something hot, and said good morning to Hester and then saw Harry and said, "Oh. Morning," in a different voice, and went to the back counter under the lamp, where he was to sit and explain torches.

The first customer did not come until twenty past nine, and she did not want a torch. She was a woman of about fifty in a green cloak with a copy of the Prophet under her arm, and she stood inside the door and looked round the room as if she had been told about it and had not believed it, and then she came to the counter and looked at Hester and said, "Is it true what they're saying? About whose this is?"

"It's mine," said Hester. "My name's on the lease."

"That's not what I asked."

Hester looked at Harry. Harry looked back at her. They had talked about this on the Tuesday, and what they had agreed was that nobody would lie, and that Hester would decide how much truth a customer got.

"The money's his," Hester said, and nodded at the stool.

The woman turned. She looked at Harry under the hat for a long moment, and Harry looked back, as he looked at everybody, and said, "Good morning."

"You're very small."

"I'm five."

"Hm," said the woman, who had not met Mrs Figg and did not need to, and she turned back to the shelves and bought three pens in three colours and a flask, and paid in Sickles, counting them out slowly as if she were testing whether the shop would take them, and went out with the bell ringing behind her.

She was back in a quarter of an hour with her sister.

By ten there were four people in the shop at once and Dennis was explaining a torch to two of them. By half past, a wizard who had come for the novels had found the one he wanted and could not understand why it cost a penny a week rather than a price, and Hester explained lending to him twice and then gave up and let him read the card. A boy of eleven in new robes came in with his mother and stood in front of Flitwick's cards for a long time and then asked Harry, because Harry was nearest his height, what they did, and Harry told him, and the boy's mother bought one, and the boy went out of the shop reading the back of it. A man asked for a wireless. Hester said they did not sell anything with a plug, and the man said that wirelesses did not have plugs, they had batteries, and Hester looked at Harry, and Harry said that a wireless had a thing in it that the Ministry would call enchantment whether or not it was, and that he was not going to sell anything the Ministry could call anything, and the man said that was a pity and bought a pen.

Lupin took him out through the back at eleven, before the Alley had quite finished getting the idea. In the yard behind the shop, with the door shut on the bell, Harry stood for a moment.

"Well?" said Lupin.

"Dennis is good. The woman in green will come back every week and tell people. The wizard with the novel will bring it back late. Hester shouldn't have let him have it without a name." He thought. "And I didn't know about the wirelesses. I've read about them and I didn't know people would ask. I'll write to Mr Hadley. There'll be a kind that's just a box and a battery, and I want to know what's inside it before I say no again."

"That's what you've got from an hour."

"That's what I've got from an hour that I couldn't have got from the sheet. Yes." He pulled the hat down. "Can we go back the long way? I want to see the end of the Alley where the rents are two hundred."

---

Snape had taken two months to say yes, and when he said it he said Saturday, eleven, and bring whatever it is you have.

What Harry had was eleven pages. They set out, in the clerk's hand, every place where Borage's account of what happened to things when they were heated and mixed disagreed with the two Muggle chemistry books Colin had brought in the spring, with Borage on the right and the Muggles on the left and a third column down the middle for what Harry thought was going on. He had been adding to it for a year. He put it on Snape's desk under the one lamp and stood back, and Snape sat down and read all eleven pages without speaking, while the dungeon dripped somewhere and Lupin, on the stool by the door, read his own book and turned no pages.

Snape finished. He went back to the first page.

"The salt."

"The Muggle book says it lowers the point at which the water boils. Borage says it raises it. I've sided with Borage, because he was writing about a cauldron."

"You have sided with Borage because he has a wand." Snape did not look up. "The Muggle is right about the salt and wrong about why, and Borage is wrong about both, and has been for a hundred years, and nobody has boiled a pan of salt water with a thermometer in it to find out, because nobody in this country has owned a thermometer. Do not side with a man because of his wand. Side with the man who has boiled the water."

Harry took that. "I don't have a thermometer either."

"No. You have a Muggle student with a rucksack. Have him bring you one." Snape turned the page. "Stirring."

"The Muggle books say stirring is moving the liquid about so that the heat's even. Borage says the direction matters and doesn't say why. I've put that down as Borage not knowing."

"Borage knows. He says so in the third chapter in words you read and did not understand, and I am not going to repeat them, because you will go and read them again tonight and understand them this time. Stirring is moving the liquid about. A wizard stirring is doing that and something else, and the something else is what the direction is for, and your Muggles do not do the something else, which is why they do not know about it." He turned the page, and the next, and the next. "Wrong. Wrong. This is a question, not a disagreement; put it on a different list. This is right, and nobody has said it, and you will write it down somewhere other than a sheet you leave on my desk. This I am keeping." He pulled the seventh page out of the stack and set it to one side. "And this."

He had stopped on the ninth page. He read it twice, which Harry had not seen him do with anything.

"Where did you get this?"

"The second Muggle book. It's about what heat does to things that are made of two kinds of thing mixed. I thought it might be why the second ingredient in the Wolfsbane has to go in at the temperature it does and not hotter. Joan Tilley says it goes wrong if it's hotter and Belby doesn't say why."

Snape did not answer for a while.

"It will take me a month to tell you whether this is right," he said. "If it is right it is the only interesting thing anyone has brought me in five years, and I will tell you so, and you will not repeat that to anyone. If it is wrong I will tell you that." He squared the eleven pages, and the two he had set aside, and put all of them in his desk and shut the drawer. "Your Muggles know what heat is, Potter. We do not. We have never needed to; a man who can make a thing with a word does not stop to ask what it is. Your Muggles cannot make it, so they asked, and they have been asking for three hundred years, and some of what they have found is true of a cauldron as well as a kettle. Not all of it. You will spend a good deal of your life finding out which."

"Yes."

"Good." He stood up. "The thermometer. Before you come back."

*Interlude: the ninth page*

Severus Snape did not go to dinner that Saturday, and did not light the second lamp.

The ninth page lay on the desk under the first one. He had read it three times now, which was twice more than he read anything, and the third time he had stopped pretending to himself that he was looking for the error. The error was not there. What was there was a question nobody in his trade had asked in three hundred years, put in a child's clerk's hand with a Muggle book cited beside it, and the question was why the second ingredient of Belby's potion spoiled above a certain heat, and the child's answer was that the ingredient was not one thing but two, and that the heat undid the one before the other could do its work. He had known for eleven years that it spoiled. He had never once asked why. A man who could make heat with a word did not ask what heat was; the boy had been told that an hour ago and had written it down, and it had been Snape saying it.

He took a sheet of his own paper and began to write out what he would need: a Muggle thermometer, which he would buy himself in Croydon on Monday in a coat, because he would not have it known in the Alley that he had wanted one; a pound of the second ingredient; a month. He wrote the boy's name at the top of the sheet and looked at it, and then crossed it out and wrote the date instead, and went on writing.

Neville came to Hogwarts for the day on the twenty-third of December, and Madam Pince gave him a book.

She did it without a word, in the way she had begun doing it for Harry the summer before last: Neville had sat down at the table by her desk, next to Harry, with his hands in his lap and his eyes on the shelves going up into the dark, and Madam Pince had looked at him for a long moment and then gone away between the stacks and come back with a thick green book about plants and set it in front of him, and gone back to her desk. Neville looked at the book. He looked at Harry.

"She does that," Harry said. "It means she's decided you'll look after it."

Neville opened it. He read the way Dudley read, with his finger under the line, his lips moving a little, and Harry sat beside him with a card on a page of Borage and read eleven books in the time Neville read forty pages, and did not mind, because Neville was there and the library had never had anyone in it who was five.

At the end of the forty pages Neville said, without looking up, "This is wrong."

"Which bit?"

"Flutterby bushes. It says they wave when there's a draught. They don't. They wave at people. Gran's got one in the end greenhouse and it waves at her and not at me, and it's not the draught, because I stood where she stands." He turned the page back and put his finger on the sentence. "Whoever wrote this never had one."

"How do you know it's people and not the draught?"

Neville told him. It took twenty minutes. It took in the Flutterby bush and the Fanged Geranium next to it and the thing in the corner that Great-Uncle Algie had brought back from Assyria that nobody could name, and the way the greenhouse smelled in the mornings, and what his grandmother did with the dead heads, and which of the six plants minded being talked to and which did not. He talked the way he had never talked in nine months of letters, with his hands out of his lap and his eyes on something Harry could not see, and Harry sat and listened and did nothing else, which was rarer for him than Neville knew.

"You should write that down," Harry said at the end.

Neville looked at him as if he had suggested flying. "I can't write a book."

"You've written me fourteen letters. That was a book about a greenhouse, just now, and you didn't need a book to tell it from. Start with the Flutterby bush. One page. Send it to me and I'll tell you if it's wrong, and it won't be."

Neville did not say yes. He did not say no either. He looked at the sentence in the green book for a while longer, and then he turned the page, and read on with his finger under the line, and Harry went back to Borage.

They went down to Hagrid's in the afternoon, because Neville had asked what was outside and Harry had said pumpkins, and Hagrid, who had been told a friend of Harry's was coming and had made a cake about it, put Neville in a chair that swallowed him and gave him tea in a mug the size of his head and asked him what he knew about plants, and Neville, who had never been asked that by a grown-up who wanted to know, told him. Hagrid listened the way Harry had, and at the end of it took them out to the pumpkin patch in the dusk and showed Neville something at the back that he said he had been growing from a seed a centaur had given him and had not told the Headmaster about, and Neville knelt down in the frost and looked at it, and said, "It wants more light," and Hagrid said he knew it did, and had been afraid to move it.

On the stairs going up to dinner, with the castle dark round them and nobody within three landings, Neville said, "The pebble goes up nearly every time now."

"Good."

"Gran bought me a wand. From a second-hand shop in the Alley. For when it comes." He went up two more steps. "I haven't told her it's come. I think she'd be happier if it came the ordinary way. With the wand."

Harry went up beside him. He thought about what to say to that, and found that he did not know, which did not happen to him often, and said so.

"That's all right," Neville said. "I didn't want you to say anything. I just wanted someone to know."

---

Dumbledore came to Harry's room on Christmas night, late, when the castle had gone quiet, and sat on the end of the bed, and said that he had something to say which was not a present and could have waited until the New Year and that he had decided not to let it.

"Augusta has asked whether you'd come to St Mungo's in the spring and see Frank and Alice. Not for them. They won't know you're there. For Neville, who goes every month and has nobody to go with who isn't seventy. I said I'd ask. You may say no, and nobody will think less of you."

"I'll go."

"Yes. I thought you would." Dumbledore looked at the window, where the lake was black. "I'll tell you now what you'll find, so you don't find it there. There's nothing to be done for them. Nothing anyone knows how to do. I've looked, and Severus has looked, and the Healers have looked for four years, and what was done to them has no undoing in any book I know of. You'll want to look yourself, and you will, and you'll come to the same wall. I'd rather you knew where it was before you went."

"I know where it is," Harry said. "It's the same wall as the snake."

Dumbledore did not answer that at once.

"Yes," he said. "It is. Go to sleep."
