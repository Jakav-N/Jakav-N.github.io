# Chapter Eleven

*September to December 1985*

The shop opened on the first Saturday in September, and Harry stood behind the counter in a hat for the first hour, because Hester had said he should see it once from that side.

It was the room next to the brewing room, which Hester had taken quietly in March and filled through the summer, and it had a window onto the Alley now, and shelves, and a bell on the door. The shelves held what the sheet had held for twenty months: pens in six colours, torches and their batteries, tape, flasks, notebooks, bicycle lamps, which had turned out to sell to people who had no bicycles, and three hundred Muggle novels along the back wall with a card on each saying a penny a week. There were the cards from Hogsmeade in a box by the till, nine Sickles, with a sign in Flitwick's hand. There was a shelf of Pepperup from next door. There was a stool for Harry, which he did not use, and a second counter at the back with a lamp over it where a Muggle-born boy of nineteen called Dennis sat ready to explain a torch to anyone who asked.

The first customer was a woman in a green cloak who had read about the shop in the Prophet, which had given it two inches on page nine under the heading A SQUIB'S SHOP, and who wanted a pen and wanted to know whether it was true what people said about who was behind it.

"It's true," said Hester. "He's over there."

The woman looked at Harry. Harry looked back, under the hat, and said, "Good morning," and she bought three pens and a flask and went out without saying anything else, and came back in a quarter of an hour with her sister.

By eleven the shop had taken eight Galleons and the Alley had got the idea, and Lupin took Harry out through the back before the idea got any larger. On the way to the bus Harry asked him what he thought it would take in a month. Lupin said he had no idea. Harry said that he had done the figures three ways and got three answers, and that he would know in a month, and did: four hundred and sixty Galleons, after wages, of which two hundred and ten was the lending library, which Hester had been right about and he had not.

---

The reporter came to Wisteria Walk on a Tuesday in October, which was a day Mrs Figg was at home and Lupin was in Luton and Harry was reading on the hearthrug with a card.

She was a young woman with a notebook and a quill that wrote by itself, and Mrs Figg kept her on the step for the time it took to say that she did not take callers and that the boy's whereabouts were not for the Prophet, during which Mr Tibbles and Snowy came and sat on the step either side of the reporter's feet and looked up at her. The reporter said that she had not come from the Prophet, exactly; that she wrote for them when they would have her, and that she had found Mrs Figg's address in the school's records, which anyone could, and that she would rather hear something true from the boy than print something she had been told by a boy in Ravenclaw's mother's cousin, and that she would go away if asked twice.

Mrs Figg asked her twice. Then Harry, from the hearthrug, said, "Ten minutes."

Mrs Figg turned round. "You don't have to."

"I know. She's going to write something either way. I'd rather she had something to write that's true."

The reporter sat in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's with her quill over her notebook and Mr Tibbles on the arm beside her, and asked what Harry read, and he told her, and she asked who taught him, and he told her that too, and she asked what he thought about being the Boy Who Lived, and he said that he did not think about it much, that he had been fifteen months old and did not know what had happened, and that the people who had done something that night were his mother and his father and they were not here to be asked. She wrote that down. She asked about the shop, and he said it was Hester Pye's and the money was his and he had said so to the Ministry. She asked how a boy of five had money, and he said that his parents had left him some and that he had made some, and she asked how, and he told her about the revision books, without the publisher's name. She asked how he could possibly have written a revision book at four, and he said that he read a great deal and had a good memory and that she was welcome to not believe it, and she said, after a moment, that she was not sure what she believed and would say so.

At ten minutes Mrs Figg said, "That's your ten," and the reporter put her quill away and thanked him and went, stepping over Snowy.

The piece came out on the Saturday, half a page, and was kind, and was wrong in three places: it said he lived with Mrs Figg, which was most of the truth; it said he had read every book in the Hogwarts library, which was not yet true and he had told her the number; and it said that he had said he did not remember his parents, when he had said that he did not know what had happened. He wrote to the Prophet that evening, a page, with the three corrections and nothing else, and the Prophet printed the page the following week in a box, and the reporter wrote to him privately to say that two of the three had been the subeditor and the third had been her, and that she was sorry, and would he mind if she wrote again when there was something worth writing. He said he would not mind.

---

Snape had him in the dungeon on a Saturday in November, about the chemistry, because Harry had asked in September and Snape had taken two months to say yes.

Harry had brought the list. It had grown since the summer; it was eleven pages now, in the clerk's hand, and it set out every place where Borage and the two Muggle chemistry textbooks disagreed about what happened when things were heated and mixed, with the Muggle account on the left and the wizarding on the right and a column in the middle for what Harry thought the difference was. Snape took it without a word, sat down at his own desk under the one lamp, and read all eleven pages while Harry stood.

Then he went through them.

"This is wrong. The Muggle is right about the salt and wrong about why, and Borage is wrong about both, and you have sided with Borage because he is the one with a wand. Never side with anyone because of their wand." He turned the page. "This is wrong. Stirring is not mixing. A Muggle who stirs is moving the liquid about. A wizard who stirs is doing that and something else, and the something else is why the direction matters, and Borage says so in the third chapter in words you read and did not understand." He turned the page. "This is right. Nobody has said it before. Write it down somewhere other than here." He turned the page. "This is wrong, this is wrong, this is a question and not a disagreement, this is right and I will be taking it, and this," on the ninth page, "is the only interesting thing you have brought me, and it will take me a month to tell you whether it is right, and I will tell you."

It took an hour. Harry asked eleven questions, one a page, and Snape answered ten and said of the eleventh that he did not know and that nobody did, and at the end he did not give the list back.

"Your Muggles," Snape said, "know what heat is. We do not, and we have never needed to, because we can make it with a word, and a man who can make a thing with a word does not ask what it is. Your Muggles cannot make it, so they asked, and they have been asking for three hundred years, and some of what they found out is true of a cauldron as well as a kettle. Not all. You will spend a good deal of your life finding out which, if you go on as you are going." He looked at Harry under the lamp. "Go on as you are going."

---

The house in Luton had thirty people in it by Christmas, and the rooms above the newsagent's had become the whole of the building next door as well, which Hester's father had bought with a loan from the account and let back to the house at a rent that paid the loan.

The thirty did what the briefs said. The two on the twenties had numbers now on forty casters, from Hester's cousin to a retired Auror whom Lupin had found, and the numbers said what the first twelve had said and more: that everybody failed, that the failures came in runs, that the runs came when the caster was tired or had been talking, and that nobody had known any of this because nobody had counted, and Harry wrote it up in December as a paper with the counts in it and no theory, twenty pages, and sent it under his own name to the only journal there was, and the journal wrote back to ask whether it was a joke. The werewolf count had stopped at six hundred and forty because the man keeping it had run out of people to ask, and the count was in the Prophet in November under Lupin's name, with the Ministry's two hundred and eleven beside it and no comment, and the Ministry had written to Lupin and Lupin had written back. The ordinary potions sold from the Alley door and now from the shop paid for the wages of the people who brewed them and a tenth over, which went to the Wolfsbane, which was for ninety now in two rooms with Joan Tilley and two assistants and Snape on Tuesdays. The warding reader had finished the books and started on the wards themselves, with a brief that said to touch nothing and write down everything, and had sent Harry a drawing of the ward on Gringotts' door that Harry looked at for a long time.

The Muggle side had begun in October, because Mr Hadley had been asked a question in the summer and had come back with an answer. The question had been what the publishing company in Oxford, which the trust now owned a quarter of, could do besides print the books. The answer was a course: the books sent out by post a chapter at a time to anyone who paid, with exercises, and the exercises sent back and marked by somebody paid to mark them, which was how a good many Muggles got their qualifications who could not get to a school. The company had advertised in two newspapers in October. By Christmas it had six hundred pupils at four pounds a month and four graduates in a room in Oxford marking to a scheme Harry had written in an afternoon, and Mr Hadley said that it would have two thousand by the summer and that he had been wrong to advise against the outright sum, since the outright sum had bought a quarter of a company that now had a business, and Harry said that he had been right with what he knew at the time.

---

The primer went into a second edition in October with his name on it.

The first edition had sold out in a fortnight once the Prophet had printed his name and the shop had said who had written it, and Flourish and Blotts had asked whether the second might say so on the spine. Harry had thought about that for the length of a breakfast and then said yes, because the thing he had been protecting by leaving the name off was a secret that no longer existed, and because a book with a name on it could be argued with, and he wanted it argued with. The second edition had a fifth chapter the first had not, on what nobody knew about why the words mattered, which Dumbledore had argued with him about for three Tuesdays in January and conceded in February and asked him to put in. It sold two thousand by Christmas, which Flourish and Blotts said was more than any book on magical theory had sold in a year since the shop had kept records.

The letters of correction began in November. There were nine by Christmas. Seven were wrong and he wrote back to say why. One was from a wizard in Yorkshire who had found an error in the third chapter that Harry had made and Dumbledore had not caught, and Harry wrote back to thank him and fixed it for the third edition. One was from a girl of nine in Kent who had found the same error a week after the wizard in Yorkshire and had also found a sentence in the fifth chapter that she said could be read two ways and ought not to be, and she was right, and he wrote back to say so, and she wrote back to say that she had three more.

---

Neville came to Hogwarts for the day on the twenty-third of December, with his grandmother, who stayed for lunch and left him until dinner.

They spent the afternoon in the library, where Madam Pince looked at Neville for a long moment and then put a book in front of him without being asked, about plants, and Neville read it slowly with his finger under the lines while Harry read beside him with a card, and after an hour Neville said, without looking up, that the book was wrong about Flutterby bushes, because his gran had one and it did not do that. Harry asked how he knew. Neville told him, for twenty minutes, about the Flutterby bush and six other plants in his grandmother's greenhouse, with the care and the detail of someone who had spent a great deal of time with plants because plants did not mind that he could not do anything, and Harry listened and did nothing else, and at the end of it said that Neville should write that down, because the book was the only book there was and it was wrong, and Neville looked at him as though he had suggested flying.

"I can't write a book."

"You wrote me fourteen letters. Start with the Flutterby bush."

Neville said he would think about it. He also said, on the stairs on the way down to dinner, where nobody could hear, that the pebble went up most times now, and that his gran had bought him a wand from a second-hand shop so that he would have one when it came, and that he had not told her it already had.

Ron's Christmas letter said that Fred and George had written down the frog, and twenty other things, in a book they would not let anyone see, and that Ginny had asked whether Harry was coming in the summer and had pretended she had not asked. The twins' letter was a drawing of a frog with a list under it, in two hands, of what they had put on it and what it had done, with a column at the side that said WHAT WE EXPECTED, which was Harry's column, and which they had filled in.

Dumbledore came to Harry's room on Christmas night, late, when the castle was quiet, and sat on the end of the bed, and said that he had something to say that was not a present and could wait until the New Year and that he had decided not to let it.

"The Longbottoms. Augusta has asked whether you would come to St Mungo's in the spring and see Frank and Alice. Not for them; they won't know you. For Neville, who has to go every month and has nobody to go with who isn't seventy. I said I'd ask you. You may say no."

"I'll go."

"Yes. I thought you would." Dumbledore looked at the window, where the lake was dark. "I'll tell you now what you'll find, so that you don't find it there. There's nothing to be done for them. Nothing anyone knows. I've looked, and Severus has looked, and the Healers have looked, and the thing that was done to them has no undoing in any book. You'll want to look yourself, and you will, and you'll find the same wall I found. I'd rather you knew where it was before you went."

"I know where it is. It's the same wall as the snake."

Dumbledore did not answer that for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "It is. Go to sleep, Harry."
