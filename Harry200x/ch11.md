# Chapter Eleven

*September to December 1985*

The shop opened at nine on the first Saturday in September. Harry was there at a quarter to, on a stool behind the counter with his hat pulled down, because Hester had said he ought to see it once from that side, and he had agreed with her.

It was the room next to the brewing room. Hester had taken the lease in March without telling Snape and had spent the summer filling it. There was a window onto the Alley now, and a bell over the door, and shelves along three walls. Everything that had gone out by owl for twenty months was on the shelves: pens in six colours, torches with their batteries beside them, rolls of tape, flasks, notebooks. Along the back wall stood the Muggle novels, three hundred of them, each with a card tucked in that said a penny a week. Behind the till was a row of jars of dried herbs, the same herbs the apothecaries sold at the good end of the Alley, bought from a seed merchant in Bedford for a fifth of the price. Hester had written Snape's opinion of each on its jar. Beside the till was a box of Flitwick's cards with a sign in Flitwick's handwriting.

Hester went round the room straightening things that were already straight. She had been up since five.

"You don't have to stay," she said. "You've seen it."

"I've seen it empty. I haven't seen anyone buy anything."

"You've had the figures every month."

"The figures say what people ordered. They don't say what they picked up and put back."

She let that go and went to unlock the door.

Dennis came in as she turned the key. He was nineteen and Muggle-born, and he had answered one of the house's briefs so well that Harry had asked to meet him, and then asked him to work in the shop. He said good morning to Hester, saw Harry on the stool, said "Oh. Morning," in a different voice, and went to his place at the back counter, where there was a lamp and a torch taken to pieces for showing people.

Nobody came for twenty minutes. Then a woman of about fifty in a green cloak came in with the Prophet under her arm, stood inside the door, and looked round as if she had been told about the shop and not believed it. The Prophet had given it two inches on page nine that morning. She came to the counter.

"Is it true what it says? About whose this is?"

"It's mine," Hester said. "My name's on the lease."

"That isn't what I asked."

Hester glanced at Harry. They had settled this on the Tuesday: nobody would lie, and Hester would decide how much of the truth a customer got.

"The money's his," she said, and nodded at the stool.

The woman turned and looked at Harry. He looked back and said good morning.

"You're very small."

"I'm five."

She looked at him a little longer, then turned to the shelves. She bought three pens in three colours and a flask, counted out the Sickles slowly, as if she were testing whether the shop would take them, and went out. In a quarter of an hour she was back with her sister.

By ten there were four people in the shop and Dennis had the torch in pieces on his counter. A boy of eleven in new robes stood in front of Flitwick's cards for some time and then asked Harry what they did, because Harry was the person nearest his own height. Harry told him. The boy's mother bought one, and the boy went out reading the back of it. A wizard who had come for the novels could not see why the one he wanted cost a penny a week instead of a price, and Hester explained lending to him twice before he read the card himself. A man asked for a wireless.

"We don't sell anything with a plug," Hester said.

"A wireless doesn't have a plug. It has a battery."

She looked at Harry.

"There's a part inside a wireless that the Ministry would call an enchantment," Harry said. "It isn't one. But they'd call it one, and I'm not selling anything the Ministry can call anything."

"That's a pity," the man said, and bought a pen.

Lupin took Harry out through the back at eleven, before the whole Alley had the idea. In the yard, with the door shut on the bell, he asked what Harry thought.

"Dennis is good. The woman in green will come every week and tell people. The man with the novel will bring it back late, and Hester shouldn't have let him take it without a name." He pulled his hat straight. "And I didn't know people would ask for wirelesses. I've read how they work, and I didn't think of it. There must be a kind that's only a box and a battery. I'll ask Mr Hadley what's inside one before I say no again."

"That's what you got from an hour."

"It's what I couldn't have got from the figures." He looked up at Lupin. "Can we go back past the good end? I want to see what two hundred Galleons a month looks like."

---

Snape had taken two months to say yes, and when he did he said Saturday, eleven o'clock, and bring whatever it is you have.

What Harry had was eleven pages. Down the right of each page was what Borage said happened when things were heated and mixed; down the left, what the two Muggle chemistry textbooks Colin had brought in the spring said; in the middle, where the two disagreed, what Harry thought was going on. He had been adding to it for a year. He put it on Snape's desk under the one lamp and stepped back, and Snape sat and read all eleven pages without a word. Somewhere behind the walls water dripped. Lupin sat on the stool by the door with a book open and did not turn a page.

Snape finished, and went back to the first sheet.

"The salt."

"The Muggle book says salt lowers the temperature at which water boils. Borage says it raises it. I went with Borage, because he was writing about a cauldron."

"You went with Borage because he has a wand." Snape did not look up. "The Muggle is right about the salt and wrong about why. Borage is wrong about both and has been for a hundred years, and nobody has boiled a pan of salt water with a thermometer in it to find out, because nobody in this country owns a thermometer. Don't side with a man because of his wand. Side with the one who has boiled the water."

"I don't own a thermometer either."

"You own a Muggle student with a rucksack. Have him bring you one." Snape turned the sheet. "Stirring."

"The Muggle books say stirring is moving the liquid about so the heat is even. Borage says the direction matters and doesn't say why. I put that down as Borage not knowing."

"Borage knows. He says it in his third chapter, in words you read and did not understand. I am not going to repeat them, because you'll read the chapter again tonight and understand it this time. Stirring is moving the liquid. A wizard who stirs is doing that and something else, and the direction is for the something else. Your Muggles don't do the something else, so they don't know it exists." He went on through the pages. "Wrong. Wrong. That is a question, not a disagreement. Put it on a different list. That is right, and nobody has said it, and you will write it down somewhere other than a sheet you leave on my desk. That one I am keeping." He drew the seventh page out and set it aside. "And this."

He had stopped at the ninth page. He read it twice. Harry had not seen him read anything twice.

"Where did you get this?"

"The second Muggle book. It's about what heat does to things that are made of two kinds of thing mixed together. I thought it might be why the second ingredient in the Wolfsbane has to go in at the temperature it does and not hotter. Joan Tilley says it goes wrong if it's hotter. Belby doesn't say why."

Snape was silent for a while.

"It will take me a month to tell you whether this is right," he said. "If it is, it is the only interesting thing anyone has brought me in five years, and I'll say so, and you won't repeat it. If it's wrong I'll say that." He squared the eleven pages and the two he had taken out, put them all in his desk, and shut the drawer. "Your Muggles know what heat is, Potter. We don't. We've never needed to. A man who can make a thing with a word doesn't stop to ask what it is. Your Muggles can't make it, so they asked, and they've been asking for three hundred years, and some of what they found out is as true of a cauldron as a kettle. Not all of it. You'll spend a good deal of your life finding out which."

"Yes."

"The thermometer. Before you come back."

---

*Interlude: the ninth page*

Snape did not go to dinner that Saturday and did not light the second lamp.

The ninth page lay on the desk on top of the others. He had read it three times, which was twice more than he read anything, and on the third reading he had stopped telling himself that he was looking for the mistake. There was no mistake. There was a question that nobody in his trade had asked in three hundred years, written in a child's copybook hand with a Muggle book cited beside it. The question was why the second ingredient in Belby's potion spoiled above a certain heat. The child's answer was that the ingredient was not one thing but two, and that the heat undid one of them before the other had done its work.

He had known for eleven years that it spoiled. He had never once asked why. An hour ago he had told the boy that a man who could make heat with a word did not ask what heat was, and the boy had written it down, and it had been Snape saying it.

He took a clean sheet and wrote out what he would need. A Muggle thermometer, which he would buy himself, in Croydon, on Monday, in a coat, because he would not have it known in the Alley that he had wanted one. A pound of the second ingredient. A month. He wrote the boy's name at the top of the sheet, looked at it, crossed it out, wrote the date instead, and went on.

---

Neville came to Hogwarts for the day on the twenty-third of December, and Madam Pince gave him a book.

She did it the way she had begun doing it for Harry: without a word. Neville sat down at the table by her desk, next to Harry, with his hands in his lap, looking up at the shelves going into the dark. Madam Pince looked at him for a moment, went away between the stacks, came back with a thick green book about plants, put it in front of him, and went back to her desk.

Neville looked at the book, and then at Harry.

"She does that," Harry said. "It means she's decided you'll look after it."

Neville opened it. He read the way Dudley read, with his finger under the line and his lips moving a little. Harry read beside him with a card, and in the time Neville took over forty pages Harry read eleven books, and did not mind at all, because Neville was there, and nobody his own age had ever sat in this library with him.

At the end of the forty pages Neville said, without looking up, "This is wrong."

"Which part?"

"Flutterby bushes. It says they wave when there's a draught. They don't. They wave at people. Gran's got one in the end greenhouse. It waves at her and not at me, and it isn't the draught, because I stood where she stands." He turned back a page and put his finger on the sentence. "Whoever wrote this never had one."

"How do you know it's people and not the draught?"

Neville told him. It took twenty minutes. It took in the Flutterby bush, and the Fanged Geranium next to it, and the plant in the corner that Great-Uncle Algie had brought back from Assyria that nobody could name; what the greenhouse smelled like in the morning; what his grandmother did with the dead heads; which of the six plants minded being talked to and which did not. He talked as he had never talked in nine months of letters, with his hands out of his lap and his eyes on something Harry could not see. Harry listened and did nothing else.

"You should write that down," he said at the end.

Neville looked at him as if he had suggested flying. "I can't write a book."

"You've written me fourteen letters. What you just said was a book about a greenhouse. Start with the Flutterby bush. One page. Send it to me and I'll tell you if it's wrong."

Neville did not say yes or no. He looked at the sentence in the green book a little longer, turned the page, and went on reading with his finger under the line, and Harry went back to his card.

In the afternoon they went down to Hagrid's, because Neville had asked what was outside and Harry had said pumpkins. Hagrid had been told a friend of Harry's was coming and had made a cake about it. He put Neville in a chair that swallowed him, gave him tea in a mug the size of his head, and asked what he knew about plants, and Neville, who had never been asked that by an adult who wanted to know, told him. Hagrid listened as Harry had. Then he took them out to the pumpkin patch in the dusk and showed Neville something at the back of it that he said he had grown from a seed a centaur had given him and had not told the Headmaster about. Neville knelt in the frost and looked at it.

"It wants more light," he said.

"I know it does," said Hagrid. "I've been afraid to move it."

On the stairs going up to dinner, with the castle dark around them and nobody within three landings, Neville said, "The pebble goes up nearly every time now."

"Good."

"Gran bought me a wand. From a second-hand shop in the Alley. For when it comes." He went up two more steps. "I haven't told her it's come. I think she'd be happier if it came the ordinary way. With the wand."

Harry went up beside him and tried to think what to say, and did not know, which did not happen to him often. He said so.

"That's all right," Neville said. "I didn't want you to say anything. I wanted someone to know."

---

Dumbledore came to Harry's room on Christmas night, late, when the castle had gone quiet. He sat on the end of the bed and said that he had something to say which was not a present, and which could have waited until the New Year, and that he had decided not to let it.

"Augusta has asked whether you'd come to St Mungo's in the spring and see Frank and Alice. Not for them. They won't know you're there. For Neville, who goes every month and has nobody to go with who isn't seventy. I said I'd ask you. You may say no, and nobody will think less of you."

"I'll go."

"I thought you would." Dumbledore looked at the window, where the lake was black. "I'll tell you now what you'll find, so that you don't find it there. There's nothing to be done for them. Nothing anyone knows how to do. I've looked, Severus has looked, the Healers have looked for four years, and what was done to them has no undoing in any book I know. You'll want to look yourself, and you will, and you'll come to the same wall. I'd rather you knew where it was before you went."

"I know where it is," Harry said. "It's the same wall as the snake."

Dumbledore did not answer at once.

"Yes," he said. "It is. Go to sleep."
