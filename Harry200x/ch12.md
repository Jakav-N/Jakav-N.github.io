# Chapter Twelve

*January to June 1986*

Flitwick came out of Mrs Figg's gas fire at seven in the morning on the fourth of March, with soot on his shoulders and a card in his hand, and did not appear to notice that Mrs Figg was in her dressing-gown.

"Touch it to the page," he said. "Any page. Then turn the scratch."

Harry was on the hearthrug with the Prophet. He touched the card to the leading article. The card went grey, and then showed a word: the first word of the article, alone in the middle of the white, in print the size of a book's. He turned the scratch on the back with his thumb, and the word was the next word, in the same place, so that his eye had not moved; and the next; and as he turned further they came faster, each one there just long enough to be seen and then gone and the next one there, and his eye sat still in the middle of the card while the article went through it.

He read the leading article. He looked up.

"A little under a minute. From the paper it's three."

"Three times, or a bit over." Flitwick had not sat down. He was small and had not slept and there was soot in his hair. "I don't think it'll go past that. The eye wants its moment on each word whatever we do, and the charm can't give it less than it wants. But three." He looked at the card as if it might do something else. "It's the gaps. You said it at Easter and I heard it and didn't hear it. I've spent a year trying to teach the charm what a word is, and it doesn't need to know what a word is. It needs to know what white is. It's known what white is since Christmas. I woke up at four with it."

Harry turned the card over in his hands. He did the sum, which was short: a book in a little over twenty minutes; the library, which had been four years from the floor of it in August 1984 and had come down to eighteen months at Easter, was eight months now, and most of that was the terms.

"Eight months," he said.

"Yes."

He looked at Flitwick, who had sat on library floors for twenty months answering a child's questions about his eyes, and who had come through a fireplace at seven to put the answer in his hand.

"Thank you. I've thought for a year about how to say it better than that, and that's what I've got."

"It'll do," said Flitwick, and sat down at last in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's, and Mrs Figg, who had said nothing at all, went to make him tea.

St Mungo's was behind a shop window in Oxford Street that had a dummy in it, and the dummy let them in.

Augusta Longbottom went first, with the vulture, and Neville went behind her with his hands in his coat pockets, and Harry went last and looked at everything, because he had read about this building for two years and had never been inside it. It smelled of what hospitals smelled of in the Muggle books and of something else under that, herbal and sharp. The woman at the desk knew Augusta and said, "Fourth floor, Mrs Longbottom," before she was asked, and looked at Harry, and looked again.

The ward was called Spell Damage, and it was a long room with eight beds and tall windows that showed a sky that was not the sky over Oxford Street. Two of the beds were at the far end by a window, and Neville walked to them without looking at anything else, the way a boy walks to his own front door, and sat down in the chair beside the nearer one, and said, "Hello, Mum."

Alice Longbottom was thin, and her hair was white, and she was sitting up. She looked at Neville. She did not know him; Harry could see that from the foot of the bed, in the way her eyes went over his face and found nothing there to stop on. But she knew he was there, and after a while she put a hand into the pocket of her dressing-gown and brought out a sweet wrapper, pink, folded small, and held it out to him, and Neville took it and said, "Thanks, Mum," and put it in his pocket.

"He keeps them," Augusta said. She was standing beside Harry at the foot of the bed with both hands on the head of her stick, and she said it to the window, not to him. "Every one. He has a tin."

Harry said nothing. He had understood, somewhere between the lift and the door of the ward, that he was being allowed to see something and that the whole of what was wanted from him was to see it, and he saw it. He looked at Alice Longbottom and at the boy in the chair and at the hand that had given the wrapper and gone back to the blanket, and he looked at the other bed, where Frank Longbottom slept with his face turned to the window, and at the chart at the foot of that bed, which was a single sheet with a date on it from 1981 and nothing since.

He had read everything there was. Three paragraphs in a book about the war, a pamphlet from this hospital with no author's name, and everything Colin had been able to find on what Muggles knew about minds that had been broken and did not mend, which was a great deal and none of it about this. He had known where the wall was before he came. He had told Dumbledore so on Christmas night. He found, at the foot of the bed, that knowing where a wall was and standing with his hand on it were not the same thing, and that he had known that too, from a churchyard, and that it did not help.

A Healer came along the ward, a tired woman in lime green with her hair coming down, and stopped by Augusta and said the things she said every month, and Augusta answered them, and Neville went on sitting with his mother and did not seem to hear. When the Healer had finished Harry asked her a question.

"Does the ward keep a record? Of how they are. Month to month. What they eat and say and do, written down."

The Healer looked at him, and at Augusta.

"Answer him," said Augusta.

"No," the Healer said. "There's nothing to write. They're the same."

"Are they? To the day?"

She did not answer that at once. "Near enough."

"Would you keep one, if someone paid for the time? Numbers. The same things every month, written in the same place."

"I don't see what good it would do."

"Neither do I. Yet." He looked at the chart with its one date. "That's usually how it starts."

The Healer looked at Augusta again, and Augusta said nothing, and the Healer said that she supposed she would not mind, and went on down the ward.

They stayed an hour. Neville talked to his mother about the greenhouse, and his mother listened in the way she had, with her eyes on his face and nothing in them, and gave him a second wrapper, blue. Frank did not wake. When they left, Augusta stopped at the door of the ward and looked back down the length of it at the two beds by the window, as Harry had seen Lupin look back at a grave, and then she put her hand on Harry's shoulder and steered him into the corridor.

In Dumbledore's office, afterwards, while Neville was in the bathroom and Dumbledore was somewhere else, she said: "You did that well. I wasn't sure you would."

"I didn't do anything."

"No. That was the doing it well." She looked at him from under the vulture. "There's a great deal you can do, Mr Potter, about a great many things. I've seen the shop and I've read the primer and I've had two letters from Arthur Weasley about pens. That," and she tilted her head towards the fireplace they had come through, "is not one of them. I want you to know that I know you know it."

"I know it. I found out at a grave in October. It didn't make the bed easier."

"No," said Augusta. "It doesn't."

The Lovegoods' house stood in a field over the hill from the Burrow and was shaped like a rook from a chess set, and Fred said so from the top of the hill and George said he had said it first.

Arthur Weasley walked them over on the second Saturday in June, with Ron, who had been promised there was a stream, and the twins, who had been promised nothing and had come anyway with a book under Fred's arm that they would not let anyone see. Molly had stayed at the Burrow with Ginny, who was five now and had said from the stairs that she was not the baby and would come next time, and Harry had said that he hoped she would, and she had gone red and gone upstairs.

The door of the rook was opened by a man with hair like a dandelion clock and a very small girl in yellow who looked at Harry for a long time and then said, "Are you sad?"

"Luna," said her father.

Harry had been asked a great many things by a great many people in eighteen months. He had not been asked that. He thought about it, which took no time, and gave her the answer, which took a little.

"Sometimes. Not often. I've got a great deal to do and I like most of it. Sometimes at night."

Luna nodded as if that were the answer she had expected, and took his hand, which nobody but Lupin did, and led him into the house, which was round inside, and up a staircase that went round too, past a kitchen and a sitting room and a printing press, to a room at the top where a woman with Luna's hair stood at a long bench with a wand in one hand and a notebook in the other and something on the bench in front of her that had been a teacup an hour ago.

"You're the boy," said Pandora Lovegood, without turning round. "You're smaller than your letters. Come and look at this and don't touch it."

Harry went and looked. The teacup was pouring. It was tilted on its saddle on the bench and pouring, steadily, from its lip, and nothing came out of it, and it went on pouring.

"I wanted a cup that would pour itself. It does. It pours itself when it's empty and I can't make it stop, and I've been standing here for an hour trying to think what I held in my head when I did it." She looked at Harry for the first time. She was thin and quick and had ink on her face. "Well?"

"What did you hold?"

"The cup pouring. Full. Tilting and pouring."

"And not the cup stopping. Not the cup upright again with nothing in it."

Pandora Lovegood looked at the cup. Then she laughed, which she did with her whole body, so that the quill in the notebook jumped, and said that he had written exactly that on the second page of the thing in the Quibbler, and she had read it, and agreed with it, and written to him to say so, and then stood at a bench for an hour and not done it.

"That's why it's four pages and not one," Harry said. "Everyone agrees with it. Then they stand at the bench."

"Yes. Yes, that's it exactly." She wrote something in the notebook, fast, and did not look up for a while, and Harry stood and watched the cup pour nothing and waited, and when she looked up she said, "Right. Sit there. Don't touch anything. Tell me when I'm about to do something stupid," and went back to work.

He sat there all afternoon. She worked and talked while she worked, which he had not seen anyone do; she threw questions over her shoulder and argued with the answers; twice she stopped and did what he had said and once it worked and once it did not, and she wrote down both. The twins came up the stairs after an hour and stood in the door with the book, and Pandora said, "What's that," and Fred said it was nothing, and George said it was a frog, and she held out her hand, and they gave it to her, and she read every page of it standing at the bench with the cup still pouring beside her and asked them eleven questions, and told them that two of the things they had written under WHAT WE EXPECTED had been right and the rest had been what they wanted, and that wanting was how everybody began and the trick was to notice you were doing it. Fred wrote that down. Ron stayed on the stairs with Luna, who was telling him about something that lived in the stream and had no name, and did not come up, and was heard, once, to say, "That's not a real thing," and once, a while later, "Is it?"

Xenophilius made tea that tasted of something Harry could not identify and did not finish, and talked about a creature he was going to Sweden to find, and asked Harry whether he would write for the Quibbler again, and Harry said yes.

On the walk back over the hill, with the twins ahead arguing about the book and Ron behind telling nobody in particular that there was nothing in the stream, Arthur Weasley said, to the hedge rather than to Harry: "She's the cleverest witch in Devon. Everyone who knows her knows she's going to kill herself on that bench one day, and nobody's ever been able to say it to her in a way she'd hear."

"She read the four pages. She said so."

"She agreed with them. That's not hearing them." Arthur looked at the hedge a while longer. "Go on writing to her. She might hear it from you. She won't from anyone who was at school with her."

Harry went on writing to her. The letters came twice a week by the end of June, and were about nothing but how a spell was made and what it was that killed the people who tried.

He wrote to Amelia Bones in April, after the ward, and he read the letter to Lupin before it went.

It was one page. It said that Sirius Black had been sent to Azkaban in November 1981 without a trial; that this was a matter of record because there was no record; that the writer did not know whether Sirius Black was guilty and had no evidence either way; and that a person who did not know was exactly the person who ought to be asking for a trial, since the people who were sure had not. Amelia Bones had been head of Magical Law Enforcement since the New Year, and she was the only person in the Ministry whom Lupin's reports described as honest without putting anything after the word.

Lupin heard it to the end. He was sitting at Mrs Figg's kitchen table with his hands round a cup he had not drunk from, and when Harry finished he did not say anything for long enough that Mrs Figg, at the sink, turned the tap off.

"You know what he did."

"I know what everyone knows. I don't know what he did. Nobody does, because nobody asked him." Harry kept his voice level, which was not difficult, because it was always level; the difficulty was in Lupin's face. "I'm not saying he didn't. I'm saying nobody asked."

"I asked. I asked myself every day for a year. I was in that street the morning after, Harry. I saw the hole in the road. I held you." Lupin's voice had gone quiet in a way Harry had not heard it go. "Don't ask me to want this."

"I'm not asking you to want it. I'm telling you before I send it, so that you don't read it in the Prophet. If you tell me not to send it, I'll think about that. I won't promise."

Lupin got up. He went to the window over the sink and stood with his back to the room, and Mrs Figg moved out of his way and did not say anything, and Mr Tibbles came in from the hall and sat down in the doorway and watched him. The clock on the mantelpiece in the front room came through the wall to Harry the way everything did, one tick and then a long while and then the next, and he counted nine of them before Lupin spoke.

"Send it."

He did not turn round when he said it. Harry sent it.

Amelia Bones wrote back in a fortnight, in a plain hand, two paragraphs. She said that he was right that there had been no trial. She said that the law did not provide for a trial to be ordered on the ground that there had not been one, but only on the ground of new evidence, and that there was none. She said that she had put his letter in the file, and that there was now a file, and that if evidence ever came she would know where to put it.

Harry read the two paragraphs once. He had, now, a face from a broken doorway; a man's word that there had been no doubt; nine ticks of a clock; and a file at the Ministry with one letter in it. He did not know what they came to. He put them where he kept the things that waited for something to happen to them, and went back to work.
