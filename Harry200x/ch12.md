# Chapter Twelve

*January to June 1986*

Flitwick came out of Mrs Figg's gas fire at seven in the morning on the fourth of March with soot on his shoulders and a card in his hand. Mrs Figg was in her dressing-gown. He did not seem to notice.

"Touch it to the page," he said. "Any page. Then turn the scratch."

Harry was on the hearthrug with the Prophet. He touched the card to the leading article. The card went grey, and then it showed a word: the first word of the article, alone in the middle of the white, in print the size of a book's. He turned the scratch on the back with his thumb. The word was replaced by the next word, in the same place, so that his eye had not moved. He turned further. The words came faster, each one there long enough to be seen and then gone, and his eye sat still in the middle of the card while the article went through it.

He read to the end of the article and looked up.

"A little under a minute. From the paper it takes three."

"Three times, or a bit more." Flitwick had not sat down. He was small and had not slept, and there was soot in his hair. "I don't think it will go past that. The eye needs its moment on each word whatever we do, and the charm can't give it less than it needs. But three." He looked at the card as if it might do something else. "It's the gaps. You said it at Easter and I didn't hear it. I've spent a year trying to teach the charm what a word is. It doesn't need to know what a word is. It needs to know what white is, and it has known that since Christmas. I woke at four with it."

Harry turned the card over. A book would take a little over twenty minutes. The library, which had been four years from the floor of it in August 1984 and had come down to eighteen months at Easter, was eight months now, and most of that was the terms.

"Eight months," he said.

"Yes."

He looked at Flitwick, who had sat on library floors for twenty months answering a child's questions about his eyes, and who had come through a fireplace at seven to put the answer in his hand.

"Thank you," he said. "I've thought for a year about how to say that better, and that's what I've got."

"It'll do," said Flitwick, and sat down at last in the chair that was not Mrs Figg's, and Mrs Figg went to make him tea.

St Mungo's was behind a shop window in Oxford Street with a dummy in it, and the dummy let them in.

Augusta Longbottom went first, with the vulture. Neville went behind her with his hands in his coat pockets. Harry went last and looked at everything, because he had read about this building for two years and had never been inside it. It smelled the way hospitals smelled in the Muggle books, and under that of something herbal and sharp. The woman at the desk knew Augusta and said, "Fourth floor, Mrs Longbottom," before she was asked. Then she looked at Harry, and looked again.

The ward was called Spell Damage. It was a long room with eight beds and tall windows that showed a sky that was not the sky over Oxford Street. Two of the beds stood at the far end by a window. Neville walked to them without looking at anything else, the way a boy walks to his own front door, sat down in the chair beside the nearer one, and said, "Hello, Mum."

Alice Longbottom was thin, and her hair was white, and she was sitting up. She looked at Neville. She did not know him; Harry could see that from the foot of the bed, in the way her eyes went over his face and found nothing to stop on. But she knew he was there. After a while she put a hand into the pocket of her dressing-gown, brought out a sweet wrapper, pink, folded small, and held it out to him. Neville took it, said, "Thanks, Mum," and put it in his pocket.

"He keeps them," Augusta said. She was standing beside Harry at the foot of the bed with both hands on her stick, and she said it to the window. "Every one. He has a tin."

Harry said nothing. He had understood, between the lift and the door of the ward, that he was being allowed to see something, and that what was wanted of him was to see it. He looked at Alice Longbottom and at the boy in the chair. He looked at the other bed, where Frank Longbottom slept with his face to the window, and at the chart at its foot, which was one sheet with a date from 1981 on it and nothing since.

He had read everything there was: three paragraphs in a book about the war, a pamphlet from this hospital with no author's name, and everything Colin had found on what Muggles knew about minds that had been damaged and did not mend, which was a great deal and none of it about this. He had known where the wall was before he came. He had told Dumbledore so on Christmas night. He found now that knowing where a wall was and standing with his hand on it were not the same, and that he had learned this already, in a churchyard, and that it did not help.

A Healer came down the ward, a tired woman in lime green with her hair coming down, and stopped by Augusta and said what she said every month, and Augusta answered. Neville went on sitting with his mother and did not seem to hear. When the Healer had finished, Harry asked her a question.

"Does the ward keep a record? Of how they are, month to month. What they eat and say and do, written down."

The Healer looked at him, and then at Augusta.

"Answer him," Augusta said.

"No," said the Healer. "There's nothing to write. They're the same."

"To the day?"

She paused. "Near enough."

"Would you keep one, if someone paid for the time? The same things every month, written in the same place."

"I don't see what good it would do."

"Nor do I, yet." He looked at the chart with its one date. "That's usually where it starts."

The Healer looked at Augusta again. Augusta said nothing. The Healer said that she supposed she would not mind, and went on down the ward.

They stayed an hour. Neville talked to his mother about the greenhouse, and she listened the way she had, with her eyes on his face and nothing behind them, and gave him a second wrapper, blue. Frank did not wake. At the door Augusta stopped and looked back down the ward at the two beds by the window, as Harry had seen Lupin look back at a grave, and then she put her hand on Harry's shoulder and steered him out.

In Dumbledore's office afterwards, with Neville out of the room and Dumbledore not yet in it, she said, "You did that well. I wasn't sure you would."

"I didn't do anything."

"No. That was doing it well." She looked at him from under the vulture. "There's a great deal you can do, Mr Potter, about a great many things. I've seen the shop and I've read the primer and I've had two letters from Arthur Weasley about pens. That is not one of them. I wanted you to know that I know you know it."

"I know it. I found it out at a grave in October. It didn't make the bed any easier."

"No," said Augusta. "It doesn't."

The Lovegoods lived in a field over the hill from the Burrow, in a house shaped like a rook from a chess set. Fred said so from the top of the hill, and George said he had said it first.

Arthur Weasley walked them over on the second Saturday in June: Ron, who had been promised a stream; the twins, who had been promised nothing and had come anyway with a book under Fred's arm that nobody else was allowed to see; and Harry. Molly had stayed at the Burrow with Ginny, who was five and had said from the stairs that she was not the baby and would come next time. Harry had said he hoped she would. She had gone red and gone upstairs.

The door was opened by a man with hair like a dandelion clock and a very small girl in yellow, who looked at Harry for a long time and then said, "Are you sad?"

"Luna," said her father.

Harry had been asked a great many things by a great many people in eighteen months, and not that. He thought about it, which took no time, and answered, which took a little.

"Sometimes. Not often. I've got a great deal to do, and I like most of it. Sometimes at night."

Luna nodded, as if that were the answer she had expected. She took his hand, which nobody but Lupin did, and led him into the house, which was round inside, and up a staircase that went round too, past a kitchen and a sitting room and a printing press, to a room at the top. A woman with Luna's hair stood at a long bench with a wand in one hand and a notebook in the other. On the bench in front of her was something that had been a teacup an hour ago.

"You're the boy," said Pandora Lovegood, without turning round. "You're smaller than your letters. Come and look at this and don't touch it."

Harry went and looked. The teacup was pouring. It stood tilted on a little stand and poured from its lip, steadily, and nothing came out, and it went on pouring.

"I wanted a cup that would pour itself. It does. It pours when it's empty, and I can't make it stop, and I've stood here an hour trying to think what I held in my head when I did it." She looked at him for the first time. She was thin and quick and had ink on her cheek. "Well?"

"What did you hold?"

"The cup pouring. Full. Tilting and pouring."

"And not the cup stopping. Not the cup upright again, with nothing in it."

Pandora Lovegood looked at the cup. Then she laughed, with her whole body, so that the quill jumped in her notebook, and said that he had written exactly that on the second page of the thing in the Quibbler, that she had read it and agreed with it and written to tell him so, and that she had then stood at a bench for an hour and not done it.

"That's why it's four pages and not one," Harry said. "Everyone agrees with it. Then they stand at the bench."

"Yes. That's it exactly." She wrote in the notebook, fast, and did not look up for a while. Harry stood and watched the cup pour nothing. When she looked up she said, "Sit there. Touch nothing. Tell me when I'm about to do something stupid," and went back to work.

He sat there all afternoon. She worked and talked at the same time, which he had not seen anyone do. She threw questions over her shoulder and argued with the answers. Twice she stopped and did what he said; once it worked and once it did not, and she wrote down both. After an hour the twins came up the stairs and stood in the doorway with the book, and Pandora said, "What's that?" Fred said it was nothing. George said it was a frog. She held out her hand, and they gave it to her, and she read it standing at the bench with the cup still pouring beside her, and asked them a few questions about the frog, and told them it was better than anything she had done at nine, and gave it back. Fred carried it down the stairs in both hands.

Ron stayed on the stairs with Luna, who was telling him about something that lived in the stream and had no name. He did not come up. Once he was heard to say, "That's not a real thing," and some time later, "Is it?"

Xenophilius made tea that tasted of something Harry could not identify and did not finish. He talked about a creature he was going to Sweden to look for, and asked whether Harry would write for the Quibbler again, and Harry said yes.

On the walk back over the hill, with the twins ahead arguing about the book and Ron behind telling nobody in particular that there was nothing in the stream, Arthur Weasley spoke to the hedge rather than to Harry.

"She's the cleverest witch in Devon. Everyone who knows her knows she's going to kill herself on that bench one day, and nobody has ever been able to say it to her in a way she'd hear."

"She read the four pages. She said so."

"She agreed with them. That isn't hearing them." Arthur looked at the hedge a while longer. "Go on writing to her. She might hear it from you. She won't hear it from anyone who was at school with her."

Harry went on writing to her. By the end of June the letters came twice a week, and they were about nothing but how a spell was made and what it was that killed the people who tried.

He wrote to Amelia Bones in April, after the ward, and read the letter to Lupin before it went.

It was one page. It said that Sirius Black had been sent to Azkaban in November 1981 without a trial; that this was a matter of record, because there was no record; that the writer did not know whether Sirius Black was guilty and had no evidence either way; and that a person who did not know was the person who ought to ask for a trial, since the people who were sure had not. A second paragraph said that the Ministry's register of werewolves held two hundred and eleven names, that the writer's own count, printed in the Prophet, held six hundred and forty, and that the writer employed people and had never asked one of them whether they were on the register and did not intend to start, and would rather say so to her than have her find it out. Amelia Bones had been head of Magical Law Enforcement since the New Year. She was the only person in the Ministry whom Lupin's reports called honest without adding anything.

Lupin heard it through. He was at Mrs Figg's kitchen table with his hands round a cup he had not drunk from, and when Harry finished he said nothing for long enough that Mrs Figg, at the sink, turned off the tap.

"You know what he did."

"I know what everyone knows. I don't know what he did. Nobody does, because nobody asked him." Harry kept his voice level, which was not hard; the hard part was Lupin's face. "I'm not saying he didn't. I'm saying nobody asked."

"I asked. I asked myself every day for a year. I was in that street the morning after, Harry. I saw the hole in the road. I held you." Lupin's voice had gone quiet in a way Harry had not heard before. "Don't ask me to want this."

"I'm not asking you to want it. I'm telling you before I send it, so you don't read it in the Prophet. If you tell me not to send it, I'll think about that. I won't promise."

Lupin got up and went to the window over the sink and stood with his back to the room. Mrs Figg moved out of his way and said nothing. Mr Tibbles came in from the hall, sat down in the doorway, and watched him. The clock on the mantelpiece in the front room came through the wall to Harry as it always did, one tick and a long wait and the next, and he counted nine before Lupin spoke.

"Send it."

He did not turn round. Harry sent it.

Amelia Bones wrote back in a fortnight, two paragraphs in a plain hand. She said that he was right: there had been no trial. She said that the law allowed a trial to be ordered only on new evidence, not on the ground that there had never been one, and that there was no new evidence. She said that she had put his letter in the file, that there was now a file, and that if evidence ever came she would know where to put it. She did not mention the second paragraph at all. Lupin, reading over Harry's shoulder, said that was the most anyone at the Ministry had ever done for him.

Harry read the two paragraphs once. He had, now, a face from a broken doorway; a man's word that there had been no doubt; nine ticks of a clock; and a file at the Ministry with one letter in it. He did not know what they added up to. He put them with the other things that were waiting for something to happen to them, and went back to work.
