# Chapter Twelve

*January to June 1986*

The word came on the fourth of March, and Flitwick brought it to Wisteria Walk himself, by Floo, without warning, at seven in the morning.

Mrs Figg was in her dressing-gown. Flitwick did not appear to notice. He came out of the gas fire with soot on his shoulders and a card in his hand and crossed the front room to where Harry sat on the hearthrug with the Prophet, and put the card down on the paper, and said, "Touch it to the page. Any page. Then turn the scratch."

Harry touched it to the Prophet. The card showed a word. It was the first word of the leading article, in print the size of a book's, alone in the middle of the white, and when he turned the scratch the next word replaced it, and the next, in the same place, where his eye already was, so that his eye did not move at all; and as he turned the scratch further the words came faster, and faster, until they were coming at a pace where each one was there just long enough to be seen and then was the next, and his eye sat still in the middle of the card and the leading article went through it like thread through a needle.

He read the article. It took him a little under a minute, and from the paper it would have taken him three.

"It's the gaps," Flitwick said. He had not sat down. "You said it at Easter and I didn't hear it. A word's the thing between two gaps. I've been trying to teach the charm what a word is for a year, and it doesn't need to know what a word is, it only needs to know what white is, and it's known that since Christmas. I woke up at four with it." He looked at the card. "Three times. A little more, if your eye will have it. I don't think it'll go past that; the eye wants its moment on each word and the charm can't give it less than the eye wants. But three."

"Three and a bit. The library's eight months."

"Yes."

Harry looked at the card and then at Flitwick, who was small and sooty and had not slept, and who had sat on library floors for twenty months answering a child's questions about his eyes.

"Thank you," Harry said. "I don't know how to say it better than that. I've thought about how for a year and that's what I've got."

"It'll do," said Flitwick, and sat down at last, and Mrs Figg went to make him tea.

The cards in the shop were the line until June, because the Hogsmeade man had to learn the new charm and said that it was harder than it looked, which Flitwick agreed with. In June the word went on sale, at twelve Sickles, and the first-years' parents who had bought the line in September came back and bought the word, and the Hogsmeade man took on a third assistant and asked, through Hester, whether the house would like to own half of his workshop, and Harry said yes, and the account did. Harry had the library by the end of the summer. He had done the arithmetic in the library in August 1984 with the real numbers and got four years, and it had been two, and the two had been Flitwick's.

---

He went to St Mungo's in April with Augusta Longbottom and Neville, by Floo from Dumbledore's office to a fireplace behind a department store that was not a department store.

The ward was on the fourth floor and was called Spell Damage and had eight beds, and two of them were Frank and Alice Longbottom. Harry had read everything the library had on what had been done to them, which was three paragraphs in a book about the war and a pamphlet from the hospital with no author, and he had read everything Colin could find on what Muggles knew about minds that had been damaged and did not come back, which was a great deal and none of it about this. He knew where the wall was. He stood at the foot of Alice Longbottom's bed and looked at it.

She was thin and her hair was white and she was sitting up, and she was looking at Neville, who had sat down in the chair beside her as a boy sits down in a chair he has sat in every month of his life, and she did not know him. She knew that he was there. She gave him a sweet wrapper, which Neville took and put in his pocket, and Augusta said, "He keeps them," in a voice that was not for Harry, and Harry understood that he was being allowed to see something and that the right thing was to see it and not to say anything, and he did.

Frank Longbottom slept. Harry looked at him for a while, and then at the chart at the foot of the bed, which said nothing, because there was nothing to say that had not been said in 1981, and then at the Healer who had come to stand by Augusta, a tired woman in lime green, and asked her whether the ward kept a record of how the eight patients were from month to month, what they ate and said and did, in numbers.

The Healer said that it did not. Harry asked whether it would, if someone paid for the time, and whether she would mind. She looked at him, and at Augusta, who said, "Answer him," and the Healer said that she would not mind and did not see the use, and Harry said that neither did he, yet.

On the way back, in Dumbledore's office, Augusta Longbottom said that he had done well, and that she had not been sure he would, and that there were a great many things he could do about a great many things and this was not one of them, and he said that he knew, and she looked at him and said that knowing it and standing at the foot of the bed were not the same, and he said that he had found that out at a grave.

---

The methodology was four pages, and the Quibbler printed it in May.

He had written it in January, after the second spell he tried to make had not taken either, because Lupin had said that people who tried to make spells got hurt and Harry had asked how, and the answer had been that nobody wrote down how, because the people it happened to were mostly dead or not talking. So he wrote down how it should be done. One change at a time. A target that could not be hurt, for anything that might hurt. Counts, and a person who was not the inventor to keep them. A caster who was not the inventor for the first trial on anything living, because the inventor's picture of the spell was never the spell. What the phrase "the magic does what it is given" meant when the picture was half held, which was that the magic did half the thing, and the half it did was not the inventor's to choose. He sent it to Dumbledore, who sent it to Flitwick and McGonagall, who sent it back with eleven notes between them, and Harry put the notes in, and then did not know where to put the pages.

Transfiguration Today had written back about his twenty pages of counts in January to say that they had decided it was not a joke and would print it in the autumn, and that was a journal for people who already knew things. The methodology was for people who did not and were about to try. Lupin suggested the Quibbler, and said why, which was that its editor printed anything, that half of what he printed was nonsense, and that the other half was things nobody else would touch, and that people who were about to try making spells on their kitchen tables read the Quibbler and did not read Transfiguration Today.

Xenophilius Lovegood printed the four pages in full, between a piece about a creature called a Crumple-Horned Snorkack and a piece about the Minister's teeth, under the heading HOW NOT TO BLOW YOURSELF UP, BY H. J. POTTER (5), and sent Harry a letter saying that his wife had read it and had things to say.

Pandora Lovegood's letter was three pages, in a hand that sloped backward, and the first page said that the fourth point was wrong, and why, and the second and third pages were questions. The fourth point was wrong. He wrote back to say so, and to answer two of the questions and say that he did not know the answer to the third, and she wrote back in a week, and he wrote back in a day, and by the end of May the letters were coming twice a week and were about nothing but how a spell was made and what killed the people who tried.

---

The Lovegoods lived over the hill from the Burrow, and in June the Weasleys walked Harry over it.

Not all of the Weasleys; Molly stayed with the baby of the family, who was Ginny, who was five now and did not consider herself the baby and said so from the stairs. Arthur came, and Ron, and the twins, who wanted to see the Lovegoods' house because they had heard it was shaped like a chess piece, and it was. It stood in a field with a sign at the gate about mistletoe, and the door was opened by a man with hair like a dandelion clock and a very small girl who looked at Harry for a long time and then said, "You've got something in your head."

"Luna," said her father.

"He has. It's not his." She considered Harry a moment longer. "Are you sad?"

Harry had been asked a good many things in two years by a good many people. He had not been asked that. He thought about it, which took no time, and answered it, which took a little.

"Sometimes. Not often. I've got a great deal to do, and I like most of it, and the people I'd be sad about I don't remember well enough. Sometimes at night."

Luna nodded, as if that were the answer she had expected, and took his hand and led him into the house, which was round inside, and up a staircase that went round too, to a room at the top where a woman with Luna's hair was standing at a bench with a wand in one hand and a notebook in the other and a thing on the bench that had been a teacup.

"You're the boy," Pandora Lovegood said. "You're smaller than your letters. Come and look at this. Don't touch it. It was a cup an hour ago and I wanted it to be a cup that poured itself, and it's that, except that it pours itself when there's nothing in it, and I can't make it stop."

Harry looked at the cup. It was pouring. Nothing came out of it, and it went on pouring.

"What did you hold? When you did it."

"The cup pouring. Full."

"And not the cup stopping."

Pandora Lovegood looked at him, and then at the cup, and then she laughed, which she did with her whole body, and said that he had said that on page two and she had agreed with it in a letter and had then stood at a bench and not done it, and that this was why the methodology was four pages and not one, and she wrote something in the notebook and did not look up for some time.

They were there all afternoon. Pandora worked, and talked while she worked, which Harry had not seen anyone do, and asked him questions over her shoulder and argued with the answers, and twice stopped and did what he suggested and once it worked. Fred and George watched from the door for an hour and then went away and came back with the frog book and showed it to her, and she read every page and asked them eleven questions and told them that two of their expectations had been right and the rest were wishful, and that wishful was how everybody started. Ron sat on the stairs with Luna, who was telling him about something that lived in the stream, and did not come up. Xenophilius made tea that tasted of something Harry could not name and did not finish.

On the walk back over the hill, Arthur Weasley said, to the hedge rather than to Harry, that Pandora Lovegood was the cleverest witch in Devon and was going to kill herself on that bench one day, and that everybody who knew her knew it, and that nobody had been able to say so to her in a way she would hear.

"She heard the methodology," Harry said. "She said so."

"She agreed with it. That's not the same." Arthur looked at the hedge. "Go on writing to her. She might hear it from you. She's not going to hear it from anyone who was at school with her."

Harry went on writing to her. He did not forget what Luna had said at the door, either. He put it with the snake.

---

Other things went on that spring.

The house in Luton had forty-five people in it by June and had stopped being a thing he could hold in his head as names; he held it as briefs, and as the forty-five pages that came every week and the forty-five that went back, and as Hester's wages book, which had become two books. The loan to Hester's father had been the first; the second was in March, to a Muggle-born boy who had left Hogwarts the year before and wanted to mend brooms for a living and could not get a lease because nobody would take a Muggle-born's word for the rent. The house took his word, on a page he had written setting out what he would do and what it would cost, which Harry read in a minute and found two mistakes in, and corrected, and lent him sixty Galleons at the rate the Muggle banks charged, which Mr Hadley had told him and which the boy had never heard of. There were four such loans by June. Lupin said that the goblins did the same, at three times the rate.

The cauldrons came from Sheffield. He had read, in one of Colin's books, how iron was cast, and in another how the cost of a cast thing fell when a hundred were made from one pattern, and he had drawn a cauldron, which was not difficult, and Mr Hadley had found a foundry that would make two hundred blanks to the drawing for a price that came to a third of what a cauldron cost in the Alley, and the house had a wizard finish them with the charms a cauldron wanted, which were few, and the shop sold them at half the Alley's price, and the cauldron shop two doors down complained to the Ministry about where the iron had come from, and the Ministry wrote to Hester, and Hester wrote back that the law said nothing about where iron came from, and the Ministry agreed that it did not.

Lucius Malfoy wrote for a third time in May, from Wiltshire, with great courtesy, to say that he had read the second edition of Harry's primer with interest, that he sat on the school's board of governors and had a particular concern for the quality of what students read, and that he hoped Harry would call on him in the summer, when his son Draco would be at home. Harry answered as he had answered before, and added, because it was true, that he was glad Mr Malfoy had found the primer interesting and would welcome his corrections. Mr Malfoy did not send any.

The Patronus was a mist by Easter, a silver mist that filled the kitchen at Wisteria Walk and held for the time it took Mrs Figg to say that it was getting on the cups, and did not take a shape. Lupin said that the shape came when it came, and that he had known grown wizards who never got one, and that a mist that held was more than most thirteen-year-olds managed in a year. Harry did not say what memory he was using. He had found it in January, and it was not the photograph or the hearthrug or the first book. It was a kitchen in Devon, and a woman drying her hands, and being put into a pair of arms without being asked.

And in April, after the ward, he wrote to a woman called Amelia Bones, whose name Lupin's reports had turned up as the head of the Department of Magical Law Enforcement since the New Year and the only person in the Ministry whom Lupin's reports described as honest without qualification. He wrote one page. It said that Sirius Black had been sent to Azkaban in November 1981 without a trial, that this was a matter of record because there was no record, that he, the writer, did not know whether Sirius Black was guilty and had no evidence either way, and that a person who did not know was the person who should be asking for a trial, since the people who knew had not. He read it to Lupin before it went.

Lupin did not say anything for a long time.

"You know what he did."

"I know what everybody knows. I don't know what he did. Nobody does, because nobody asked him. I'm not saying he didn't do it. I'm saying nobody asked."

"I asked. I asked myself for a year. I know." Lupin's voice had gone very quiet. "Harry, I held you the night after. I was in that house the next morning. Don't ask me to want this."

"I'm not asking you to want it. I'm telling you before I send it, so that you don't read it in the Prophet."

Lupin got up and went to the window and stood there with his back to the room for the time it took the clock on Mrs Figg's mantel to tick nine times, and then he said, "Send it," without turning round, and Harry sent it.

Amelia Bones wrote back in a fortnight, two paragraphs, in a plain hand. She said that he was right that there had been no trial. She said that the law did not provide for a trial to be ordered on the ground that there had not been one, only on the ground of new evidence, and that there was none. She said that she had put his letter in the file, and that there was now a file, and that if evidence came she would know where to put it. Harry read the two paragraphs and put them with the face from the broken doorway and Lupin's word and the ninth tick of Mrs Figg's clock, on the part of the list that waited for something to happen, and went back to work.
