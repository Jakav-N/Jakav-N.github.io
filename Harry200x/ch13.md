# Chapter Thirteen

*July to December 1986*

He finished the library on a Tuesday afternoon in August, with a book about broom varnish.

It was not a good book. It had been written in 1870 by a man who made varnish, and it was mostly about his own, and it was the last one on the last shelf that he had not read. He read it with a card in twenty minutes, closed it, and sat for a moment at the table by Madam Pince's desk with his hand flat on the cover. Then he wrote the card for it, in the clerk's hand, with the title and the shelf and six lines on what it was about, and took the book and the card to the desk.

Madam Pince stamped the book. She took the card and put it in the eleventh shoebox, which was nearly full, and closed the lid.

"That's the last," Harry said.

"I know what it is." She did not look up. She squared the shoebox on the desk with the others, and then she did look up, and she said, "I thought I'd be dead before anyone had read all of it. I didn't think it would be a child, and I didn't think I'd mind, and I find I do."

He did not know what to say to that, and said thank you, which was not right either. She nodded once and went back to her ledger, and he went down the stairs and out across the lawn to Hagrid's, because it was Tuesday and the pumpkins wanted doing, and halfway across the grass he found that he had stopped walking and was standing in the sun with nothing in his hands, and that this had not happened to him since the cupboard.

At dinner Dumbledore stood up with a glass and said nothing about it in front of the others, and sat down, and afterwards in the office he said that he had done the sum in September 1983 and got four years, and had been glad to be wrong, and that the two years had been Filius's as much as Harry's. Harry said that he knew that, and that he had told Flitwick so, and Dumbledore said that he had heard, and that Filius had told the whole staff room.

---

The list took him a week, and it was the first thing he did with the library once he had all of it.

He wrote it at Mrs Figg's table, a page at a time: two hundred and forty charms and enchantments that the books described and nobody taught, with the book and the page beside each one and a line on what it might be good for now. Some were in Latin that had drifted since the book was printed, and for those he wrote the incantation as the book gave it and, beside it, as he thought it had been meant. Some were plainly nonsense, and he wrote that too.

Flitwick came to read it on a Tuesday. He read the whole of it at the table without speaking, which took him two hours, and when he reached the end he went out into Mrs Figg's back garden and stood among the cabbages. Harry waited. When Flitwick came in again he said, "I've taught Charms for thirty years. I knew eleven of those."

"Forty of them look simple. I'd like to know how many work."

"So would I."

Owen Pryce cast the first of them in Luton on the Thursday, on a block of wood, with Lupin counting. It was a charm from 1611 for keeping food from spoiling. The book said to speak a word and draw a circle in the air, and Pryce spoke the word and drew the circle, and nothing happened that anyone could see, and a week later the apple on the bench beside the block had not gone brown. The second, which the book said would keep a cellar dry, did nothing, a week later or ever. Pryce wrote down both. By Christmas he had tried forty, and twenty-six had done what their books said they would, and one of the twenty-six was a charm from 1703 that made bad water fit to drink.

That one Harry wrote out plain on a single sheet, the word and the movement and nothing else, and before it went anywhere he and Dumbledore read the Statute of Secrecy together at Mrs Figg's table, line by line, to be sure that a wizard who poured clean water for a Muggle was breaking nothing, and he was not. The sheet went out free with the shop's list and the course's lessons to anyone who asked. The first letter back came from Caithness, from a wizard who said his well had been bad for thirty years and that he had not known there was anything to be done about it.

---

The map came out of Filch's office because of a question about cards.

Harry had asked Lupin, over a brief for the house about the index, whether any charm existed that would show where everything in a building was. He meant books. Lupin, who had been reading the brief, stopped reading it and did not answer for a while, and then said that there had been such a thing, once, that it showed people and not books, that four boys had made it at school and that Filch had taken it off one of them in their seventh year and would still have it, because Filch kept everything.

"You were one of the four."

"Yes."

"Who were the others?"

Lupin told him. James. Sirius. Peter Pettigrew.

Dumbledore went to Filch's office himself the next morning and came back with a square of old parchment, folded, with nothing written on it. At the staff table that evening, when the others had gone, he laid it in front of Lupin and sat down across from him, and Harry sat at Lupin's side, and Lupin looked at the parchment for some time before he touched it.

"You need to say something to it," he said. "We thought it was funny at the time."

He put his wand to the parchment and said what had to be said, and ink came up out of the blank page in lines, from the middle outward, and the lines were the castle. Every floor of it. Every stair. And on every floor, moving, small dots with names written beside them in a cramped hand: Argus Filch in his office, Poppy Pomfrey in the hospital wing, Hagrid on the path to the gates, the three of them at the staff table in the Great Hall, and across the top in large letters the names of the four boys who had made it, which were not their names.

"Moony," Lupin said. "That was me. Wormtail was Peter. Padfoot was Sirius. Prongs was your father." He had his hand flat on the parchment, over the Gryffindor tower. "It shows the passages, too. All of them. Seven. We found them in third year and nobody else has ever found them all, as far as I know. The Headmaster hasn't."

"I had found five," said Dumbledore.

Harry looked at the map for a long while. He looked at the dots and the names and the stairs that moved on the parchment when they moved in the castle, which he could see from where he sat because a stair had just moved on the second floor. He looked at the four names at the top. Then he asked why the map's makers had used the names they had, and Lupin told him that, too, briefly, and would not say more, and Dumbledore did not ask him to.

Dumbledore kept the map in his desk. He said that it should not leave the castle and that Harry could ask for it whenever he liked, and Harry said that he would not want it often, and did not, that year.

---

The governors met on the second Friday in October, and Augusta Longbottom came to Wisteria Walk afterwards to tell him about it, because she had been there.

Harry knew the motion. Lupin had brought it from the Ministry's reading room, where the board's papers were lodged a week before each meeting, and it was one sentence: that the board should inquire into the presence at the school of a minor not enrolled as a pupil, and into what arrangements had been made for his supervision, and at whose expense. It was moved by Lucius Malfoy. Dumbledore had read it at Mrs Figg's table and said that it was perfectly in order and that he would answer it, and had gone away looking as if he was thinking of something else.

Augusta took the chair that was not Mrs Figg's, and the tea, and told it.

"He spoke well. He always does. Nothing against the boy, nothing against Albus, a father's concern for the school's good name, a wish to be sure that whatever was being done was being done properly. Two of them nodded all the way through. Then Albus got up."

"What did he say?"

"He said that the minor in question had the run of the library under the librarian's eye, ate at the staff table, slept in a room next to the hospital wing, and had cost the school nothing but the matron's time and a quantity of ink. He said that the minor had read every book in the general collection and had made a catalogue of it that the librarian used daily, which the school had also not paid for. He said that any governor who wished to see the arrangements was welcome at any time, and that he would be glad to show them the catalogue." She drank some tea. "Then he sat down. He didn't say your name once. Neither did Malfoy. It was like watching two men discuss a horse."

"How did it go?"

"Six to five against the motion. I voted. I'm not on the board, but I was in the room, and nobody could remember whether I was entitled to be, and by the time they had looked it up it was done." She set the cup down. "Malfoy came to me afterwards and said how good it was to see me, and asked after Neville. I said Neville was well. He said that his boy would be glad of company that summer. I said I was sure he would."

Harry thought about Lucius Malfoy, whose fourth letter was in the drawer of the desk upstairs with the other three.

"What does he want?"

"To be able to say he knew you first," said Augusta. "Before anyone else did. He's not a fool, Mr Potter. He can see what you're going to be as well as I can. He'd like to have been kind to you when it didn't cost anything, so that he can remind you of it when it does." She stood up. "Don't let him. And answer his letters. You've done both so far."

---

Fred and George came to Hogwarts for a week in August, which was Dumbledore's idea, and nothing exploded, which was Hagrid's doing more than anyone's.

They were nine, and they had the frog book, and they had three things they wanted to try that they had not been allowed to try at the Burrow. Dumbledore gave them the second-year Potions room in the mornings, with Lupin on the stool by the door, and Hagrid's garden in the afternoons, with Hagrid. On the second day Fred fell in the lake. On the fourth they made a thing with two charms on it that did what they had meant it to do, which was turn a teacup blue and then back again, over and over, until somebody said the word to stop it; it was not useful and it was not funny, and both of them stood and looked at it for a quarter of an hour as if it were. On the last evening George asked Harry, on the stairs, whether he had ever made a spell, and Harry said no, that he had tried twice and that it had not taken either time, and George said, "Oh," and then, "We'll have a go, then."

Tom Dennison was nine as well. He was Mrs Dennison's grandson, and he came to the library on the High Street on Saturday mornings to play chess against anyone who would, and in September, when Lupin brought Harry in for the week's books, he was sitting at the board in the corner with nobody opposite, and he looked at Harry and said, "Do you play?"

Harry had read four books on chess. He had never played. He sat down and lost in nineteen moves, and then in twenty-six, and Tom Dennison, who did not know anything about him except that he was small and read a great deal, said that he was not bad for someone who had never played, and that he should come next week, and he did. He lost every Saturday until the following spring. He did not mind as much as he had expected to. Tom was the first person his own age he had met who had no idea who he was, and who talked to him about nothing but the board, and he found that he looked forward to Saturdays.

In November a batch of Pepperup went out with the preserving charm on the flasks, and the charm preserved the wrong thing, and the potion in two hundred flasks was a week old on the day it was bottled. Nobody was hurt. Hester found it before the customers did, because the tally charm on the shop door counted a flask that came back. Harry wrote the recall himself, one page, saying what had happened and what the house would do about it, which was replace every flask and pay for the owls, and Hester read it and said it would cost a month's profit and sent it. The shop in Hogsmeade had opened in October with Dennis to run it, and its first month's profit was the one that paid.

Snape's note came in November. It was one line, in green ink, with no name on it. *The ninth page is right. Saturday, eleven. Bring the thermometer.*

---

He went to Privet Drive for Christmas Day, which he had not done before.

It had been Dumbledore's suggestion, made carefully, and Harry had thought about it for a breakfast and said yes, and had written to his aunt, and his aunt had written back, four lines, saying that he could come for dinner at one and that Marge would be there and that he was not to bring anything that would upset her. He did not bring anything. Lupin walked him round at half past twelve and left him at the gate.

Dinner was in the dining room, which was only used for Marge. Marge was large and had a dog under the table and did not speak to Harry except to tell him to sit up, which he did. His uncle carved. His aunt served. Dudley, who was six and could read anything now, had a book beside his plate that his mother had told him twice to put away and had not, and when Marge asked what it was, Dudley said it was about the sea, and Harry's, and Marge looked at Harry down the table for the first time and said that she had heard he was clever.

"Yes," Harry said.

"Vernon says you're at some school."

"I'm at Mrs Figg's, in Wisteria Walk. I go to a school in the holidays."

"Boarding?"

"Something like that."

Marge said that boarding had been the making of her, and the making of Vernon, and that it would be the making of Dudley if Vernon had any sense, and Vernon said that Dudley was going to Smeltings and that was settled, and the conversation went where it went, which was away from Harry, and stayed there. He ate. He watched his aunt watch Dudley's book. When the pudding came she lit it, and the flame went up blue and slow, and Dudley said, "Harry, look," and Harry looked, and his aunt, with the match still in her hand, looked at Harry over the flame until the match had burned down, and then put it out.

Lupin came for him at four. At the gate his aunt said, "Thank you for coming," in the voice she used for the neighbours, and then, in a different one, "He reads all the time now. He's never without one," and went in.
