# Chapter Fourteen

*January to June 1987*

The first exercises came back in the third week of January, and Harry marked them himself at Mrs Figg's table, because he wanted to see what children got wrong.

The course had gone out at the start of the month: a first lesson, eight pages, to four hundred children whose parents read the Prophet and had written in. It was the primer taken apart and put together again for a child of seven, with a page of questions at the end and an envelope to send them back in. Hester had printed it in Luton. Lupin had read it and said it was clear. Dumbledore had read it and said that it was clearer than anything he had been given at seven.

The exercises came in a sack. Harry tipped them onto the table and began.

He had expected the children to get the hard question wrong, which was the one about why the words of a spell mattered. They got that one right, nearly all of them, in their own ways. What they got wrong was the easy one. He had asked what a wand was for. He had thought the answer obvious. Three hundred and eleven children out of four hundred said that the wand made the magic, and forty of them drew a picture of it doing so, and one, a girl in Cornwall, wrote that her mother said she could not have a wand until she was eleven and was that right, and if it was, what was she supposed to do until then.

He sat and read the three hundred and eleven. Then he put the sack down on the floor, fetched a clean sheet, and wrote lesson two again from the beginning, because the one Hester had printed assumed something that nobody under eleven in the country believed. It took him an hour, most of it looking at what he had written and seeing where a child of seven would stop.

Lupin read it that evening. "You've put the wand in last."

"They think it's first. If I put it first they'll stop listening after that, because they've got what they came for. It goes last, after the thing it's for."

"And the girl in Cornwall?"

"I've written to her." He had. He had told her that her mother was right about the wand and wrong about the waiting, and what she could do in the meantime, which was what he had done, and that she should write and tell him when something happened. He had signed it with his name, because the course had his name on it and there was no point pretending otherwise. The girl wrote back in February to say that a pebble had gone up an inch, and he put the letter in the drawer with Neville's.

By Easter there were nine hundred children on the course. By June there were fourteen hundred, and Hester had taken on two more people in Luton to do nothing but open envelopes, and Harry marked a hundred exercises a week himself, chosen at random by Hester so that he could not choose the good ones, and rewrote whatever the hundred told him to.

---

Snape had the thermometer on the desk when Harry came in, and a pound of the second ingredient in a jar beside it, and a cauldron on a low fire that was not yet warm.

"Sit."

Harry sat. Lupin sat on his stool by the door. Snape did not sit. He stood over the cauldron with the thermometer in his hand and his wand in the other, and for the first hour he said almost nothing. He brought the water up by degrees and said the number aloud at each one, and Harry wrote it down, because Snape had told him to, and at a certain number Snape put the ingredient in, a little at a time, and watched, and said the number again, and the water did what the ninth page had said it would do, which was go cloudy and then clear.

"Again," said Snape, and emptied the cauldron, and they did it again, and it did the same.

The third time he took the water higher first, past the number, and put the ingredient in, and it went cloudy and stayed cloudy.

"That," he said, "is what happens in every apothecary in Britain, because Belby's recipe says to bring the water to a simmer and nobody has ever asked what a simmer is. It is this number." He put his finger on the glass. "Not above. Your Muggle book is right, and Belby is lucky."

"What does it do to the price?"

"If the second ingredient is not spoiled, you need a fifth of it. The second ingredient is the dear one." Snape set the thermometer down. "A tenth of Belby's price. Perhaps less, if Tilley can hold a fire steady, which she can."

Harry looked at the cauldron.

"Will you write it up?"

"No."

"It should have your name on it."

"It should have yours. It was your question." Snape wiped his hands. "I will not put my name beside a Muggle thermometer in a wizarding journal to be laughed at by men who have never boiled water. You may say in your paper that the brewing was done at Hogwarts under supervision. That is true, and nobody will ask whose."

Harry wrote the paper that week, four pages, with the numbers in it and the two things the ingredient was, and sent it to Snape before anyone else. It came back in a fortnight with the margins full of green ink and, at the top of the first page, in the same ink, two names instead of one. Snape had put his second. Harry did not ask him why, and Snape never said.

By May the brewing room in the Alley was making the new potion for a hundred and twenty people at a price that most of them could pay without help, and Lupin, who had been on the old potion for three years, came back from the May moon and said that it was the same, and that the same was all he had wanted.

*Interlude: a file*

Amelia Bones kept two kinds of file. The first kind was the Department's, in the Department's cabinets, with the Department's labels. The second kind was hers, in a drawer of her own desk that locked, and there were eleven of them, and most of them were about people who had not yet done anything.

She opened a twelfth on a Thursday in March, and the label she wrote on it was LUTON.

What went into it first was a cutting from the Prophet of the previous November, a column of figures under R. J. Lupin's name beside the Department's own figures, which were a fifth of the size and had been for six years. What went in second was the Department's letter to Miss H. Pye of the previous summer and Miss Pye's reply, which she had read when it came in and had not forgotten, because it named a child as the money and gave his age. What went in third was a copy of a report, nine pages, in a hand she recognised from the cutting, on how the register was compiled and why it could not be right, which had arrived on her desk in a plain envelope with no note, and which she had read twice.

She did not know who had sent it. She had an idea. She wrote the date on the inside of the folder, and under it, *Nobody at the house has been asked whether they are on the register. The boy told me so himself, in writing, last April. I did not answer that paragraph. I am noting here that I read it.*

She put the folder in the drawer with the other eleven, and locked it, and went to a meeting about cauldron thickness that lasted two hours.

Pandora Lovegood's letters came on Tuesdays and Fridays now, and in April one of them came on a Wednesday as well, with a drawing.

The drawing was of her workroom, from above, with the bench marked and the door marked and a shape in the corner that she had labelled *where it went*. The letter said that she had been trying a thing on a wooden block, as he had told her to, instead of on herself, as she would have done a year ago, and that the block had gone through the wall into the next room and taken the wall with it, and that Luna had been in the next room, and that Luna was fine, and had asked whether it was going to happen again, and had been told yes, probably, but not to her.

Harry read the letter. Then he wrote back, one page, and did not say anything about the wall or the block or what she had held in her head. He asked whether Luna had been frightened. He asked whether there was a room in the house with nothing in it that she could use instead, and whether Xenophilius would sit outside the door. He asked her to send the drawing again with the next room marked. She wrote back on the Friday that Luna had not been frightened, that there was such a room and she was moving into it, and that Xenophilius had said he would sit outside any door she liked as long as he could bring the press.

---

Augusta Longbottom wrote in June, on the subject of Neville and the wand.

*He told me,* the letter said. *He came into the drawing room on Sunday and stood in front of me and said that the magic had come eighteen months ago and that he had not told me because he thought I would rather it had come with the wand. He said this to my face, which he has never done about anything. I said that I had known for a year. He asked how. I said that Mrs Figg's cat had told me, which was a lie, and the first I have told him, and I told it because the truth was that I had watched him with a pebble in the greenhouse in the spring of last year and had not said anything, and I was not ready to tell him that. We did not discuss it further. He has the wand now. It is a bad one, and I have written to Ollivander. I am telling you this because you will want to know, and because I think he will tell you himself in a way that makes it sound smaller than it was.*

Neville's letter came three days later. It said that he had told his gran about the pebble and that she had said she knew, and that he was getting a proper wand in August, and that the Flutterby bush had waved at him on Tuesday for the first time, and was that in the book, because he had looked and it was not.

---

Mr Hadley came in June with the papers for the publisher, which the trust now owned all but a tenth of, and for a second thing he had been asked to arrange in March and had taken three months over because he did not like it.

"A fund," he said. "With other people's money in it. Run on your method, which is to buy what pays and do nothing. I've found six people who'll put money in on the strength of two years of your figures, and none of them knows how old you are, and I've told none of them, and I'd like it in writing that if they ask, I'm to tell them."

"If they ask, tell them."

"They won't ask. That's what I don't like." He put the papers on the table. "I'll run it. You'll write me a page when you want anything changed, and I'll argue, and you'll usually win. I want one thing understood. If this goes wrong, it's my name on it, not yours, and I'll have been the man who let a six-year-old run money that belonged to a dentist in Reading."

"If it goes wrong, I'll have been the six-year-old. I'd rather it was my name."

"You can't have it. That's the law, and for once I'm glad of it." He pushed the papers across. "Sign where the trust signs. Professor Dumbledore has already."

Harry signed. He had read what the law said about funds in the spring, and he had read what the newspapers said about the ones that went wrong, and he had written his one page in March and had not needed to write another since.
