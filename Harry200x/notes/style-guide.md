## Part two: the method

What the prose is for. A reader should see what Harry sees and hear what people say, and should never hear the narrator. The narrator knows more words than Harry but no more facts, and has no opinions.

Paragraphs. Each one does one thing: something happens, or something is seen, or someone speaks. A paragraph does not also sum itself up. Sections stop on an action or a plain fact.

Sentences. Vary them on purpose. A run of plain declaratives with strong verbs. Then one that opens with its circumstance ("When the bell rang, ..."). Then a long one, if it is carrying a sequence of actions in their real order. Two independent clauses joined by "and" is allowed about once a page; three never. A list is only a list when the items are things (the books in the basket), never when they are events. No dash as a rhythm device.

Order within a sentence. Put the thing that matters where the weight falls, at the end, and do not hang a small afterthought there ("..., and showed her"). If an afterthought is worth having, it is worth a sentence.

Names. Call things what Harry would call them: Voldemort, the motorbike, H. Never "the first letter of his name", "the man with the red eyes", "something dangerous", once he has the word.

Speed. It was established in chapter one. After that it appears only as what he notices (a brick turning, a spark landing) and in the real constraints (eyes, hands, sleep). Never "which for him was a long time". His body is an ordinary body on ordinary time.

Interior. Show his thinking as its content, not as the fact of it: the price of aconite by the pound, what Dumbledore will say, whether the fence would have gone up anyway. Decisions take no time and get none. His purpose, that things go well for people, is the measure he applies when choosing what to do next, and the reader should see him applying it, in his own terms, a few times a chapter, never as a speech.

Summary. When a passage summarises rather than shows, it is still narrative: time order, concrete nouns, cause and consequence, one or two particulars that stand for the rest, in the same voice as the scenes around it. Never a checklist, never items with colons, never tallies ("five done, two promised"), never a ledger in prose. If the material is a list, give it to a character to say or to read, and let someone answer.

Acting. Every chapter from eight onward shows him doing at least one thing to the world beyond reading: money, letters, people, research. The ledger keeps the open threads under PENDING.

Dialogue. People talk. Contractions, short answers, half-finished thoughts, reacting to the last thing said. Dumbledore is courteous and warm and explains without lecturing; McGonagall is dry and exact; Lupin is gentle and self-mocking; Figg talks to Harry as she talks to the cats; Snape is short. Formality lives in vocabulary and manner, not in syntax. No one delivers a balanced pair of clauses or a closing epigram. Give scenes dialogue rather than summarising what was said, when the saying matters.

Humour. Comes from what happens (the cake, the cats, Mr Hadley's knees) and never from how the narrator phrases it.

Memory. He rereads nothing and redoes no sum unless the inputs changed. He has it after once.

Bodies. A four-year-old's hand cannot do an adult's wand movement, and practice does not change the length of his arm. Growth does, over years.

Facts. Canon dates, canon objects, expert-true statements from experts. The magic's rules apply to him as to anyone, and he learns them slowly or not at all.

Before sending. Read the chapter once as Jacob would, marking every sentence that fits a fault above, and fix those; then read the dialogue aloud in the head and fix anything no one would say.
