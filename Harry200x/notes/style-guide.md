# Style guide

Jacob's directives are reproduced verbatim below. They are the authority. The only reworked section is the last one, which is derived from another session's suggestions and is marked as such. Do not paraphrase this file when applying it; read it.

## Story parameters (verbatim)

- Write this very human-ly. Avoid short phrases connected by chained ands, stating the obvious, coining new terms without permission (you can ask! Please do it you find yourself going here), overloading existing terms in ways that don't fit their unusual meaning, or "punchy" statements.
- It'll eventually be 100 chapters. Each will be approx 3k words long
- you'll write the outline 10 chapters at a time. The outline will be dense, require a lot of thought, and take about as much time as it does to write the ten chapters.
- Only write chapters up to 2 chapters ahead of where I am unless I say otherwise.

## Reader feedback on chapter one (verbatim) and the pattern behind it

- "You have him logic too early, even before he had significant amounts of sped up time." Quoted line: "The bottle coming to him was a thing that could be done, and he had done it, and it was the thing in this house that was most feared and most hated"
- "The TV is handled a little strangely, but I suppose leave it because I'm not sure how I'd want you to change it."

Pattern: the narrator handing an infant finished inferences in abstract nouns ("a thing that could be done", "most feared and most hated", "he understood that", "he set about"). Before he has language, and for a good while after, what he has are pairings and recognitions: this sound went with that sight, this word came with that voice. Conclusions arrive late and in concrete terms, and the narration must not tidy them into propositions for him. The amount of sped-up time matters: in the first months he has had only a subjective year or two of being a baby with almost nothing to look at, and that is not the same as being an adult.

## Reader feedback on chapter four (verbatim)

- "\"Each time it goes down takes about as long as Mrs Figg takes to say Mr Tibbles.\" - false" (Every outside event reaches him slowed by the same factor, so he can never measure the factor by comparing two outside events. He can only report how a thing of known real duration looks to him.)

- "on the thing that roared" - Harry probably understands that this was a motorcycle or something else
- (the Sirius paragraph) "Harry does not have a reason to suspect that Sirius is innocent at this point, and he remembers everything just by default. Unfortunately, the way this paragraph is written is also bad."
- "I will tell you the truth or I will tell you that I am not going to tell you, and I will try not to do the second thing often."

Pattern: Dumbledore (and the narrator) speaking in balanced parallel clauses, triplets and epigrams ("X, and it is Y, and I will Z"; "not A but B"; "the number we did not say"). People do not talk like that. Dumbledore talks plainly to Harry. Also: Harry uses the ordinary name for a thing he knows the name of; and he does not do inference he has no grounds for, nor does the narrator make a device out of his remembering.

## Reader feedback on chapter five (verbatim)

- "\"Hogwarts: A History was the one he went back to, because it had a chapter on the library, and the chapter said that the library held some eleven thousand volumes\" - perhaps he might, but do remember that he knows every word in the book."

Pattern: he never rereads for retention and never "goes back to" a text. He has it. Fixed in chapters three, five and six wherever a reread or a repeat sum appeared.

## Reader feedback on chapter five, second pass (verbatim)

- "\"there had been people like his mother for as long as there had been people, and that they had hidden themselves from everyone else by law for three hundred years, and why\""
- "Chapter 5 has a variety of other issues, which I hope you can find."

Pattern: coy labels for things Harry has the word for ("people like his mother", "the man with the red eyes" after he has read the name), and withheld reasons ("and why"). Also found and fixed in that pass: a timeline slip (Dumbledore's visit on a day Harry could not have been at Figg's), an observation Harry could not have made (cups "already washed"), sitcom buttons on Dudley, Figg and Mr Tibbles, an echo gag at the library desk, a false claim that the cart moved at its proper pace for him, "he stopped counting" (he cannot), a contradiction with chapter three about what he had moved, and more Dumbledore parallelism.

## Reader feedback on chapter five, third pass (verbatim)

- "\"It took him most of an hour, because the pictures moved and he watched them\" - I'm pretty sure wizarding pictures do not repeat for an hour long."
- "\"and that there were several ways of not naming him and the paper used all of them\""
- "\"and took his hand in the kitchen, and said that this would be unpleasant for a moment and that he was sorry, and Harry said, \"All right,\" and the kitchen went.\""
- "You should fix more than just what I say is an issue, and in the future avoid bad patterns as well."
- "\"bent over, pulling breath in\" - Harry's breathing does not run faster than other people... why would he panic so fast? And of course, \"briefly\" for Dumbledore is less brief for Harry: Harry knows this! It's not necessary to say that Harry know it, too."

Rules added: his body is an ordinary body on ordinary time; nothing physical happens to it faster than it would to anyone. The narration does not keep pointing out that a moment is long for him; that was established in chapter one. Before sending a chapter, make a full pass for: chained "and"s, quips and buttons, coy labels, withheld reasons, epigrams, deliberation given a duration, rereading, the "he put it with the things he did not understand" tic, "X went" closers, and anything his body or eyes could not have done.

## Reader feedback on chapter five, fourth pass (verbatim)

- "\"which is not a rate anyone else gets to set\""
- "\"It is a comfortable one, and it is yours, and until you are of age I hold the key and will spend it on your behalf when I think it wise and tell you when I do\" - this is not how Dumbledore talks..."
- "\"and dust on the cushion, and it was dark inside, and the man who came out of the back was old in a different way from Dumbledore, with pale eyes that did not blink enough.\" - This is the most common repeated phrasing. 50% of the annoying stuff is concentrated around this phrasing. It's not just the phrasing, but also the things that go along with it. There's more possibilities for formation of sentences."
- "\"Harry held a wand, and it was taken away. He held another...\" - normal writing does not work like this. Good authors write differently, although I don't know quite how to specify it."
- "\"\"Holly,\" said Mr Ollivander. \"Eleven inches. Phoenix feather, which is not a common core, and this is not a common feather.\"\""
- "You appear to have missed all of these."
- "\"Flourish and Blotts was the only shop that day in which anyone had to tell him it was time to go.\" - this kind of wording/tone/phrasing isn't terrible, but it feels like I keep seeing it. Currently, I'm finding it annoying."

The sentence-shape rule, which matters more than any single line: my default sentence has been a chain of clauses joined by "and", often ending on a dry turn ("that did not blink enough"), or else a clipped pair of parallel statements ("He held a wand, and it was taken away. He held another."). Both are tics. Vary the shape: subordinate clauses, a semicolon now and then, sentences that start with something other than the subject, real verbs instead of "was" and "went", lengths that differ from one sentence to the next. No wry summary sentences opening a paragraph ("X was the only shop in which..."). No parallel epigrams in dialogue ("not a common core, and this is not a common feather"). Dumbledore and others speak in ordinary sentences with contractions.

## Reader feedback on chapter six (verbatim)

- (McGonagall's "That is not a first attempt. I do not mean that you have practised..." speech) "everybody is talking weird in general; this is not how people normally talk."

Dialogue rule: people talk in speech, not in prose. Contractions. Short sentences mixed with run-ons. Reactions before explanations. A person explaining something does it the way they would to a colleague across a table, with "I mean" and "look" and half-finished thoughts, not in numbered, balanced clauses. Formal characters (McGonagall, Dumbledore) are formal in vocabulary and manner, not in syntax; they still say "that's" and "I'd rather".

## Derived working rules (reworked from another session's suggestions; not verbatim, not authoritative over the sections above)

- Take the arithmetic literally before writing. Know what Harry has actually done with the time, year by year and field by field, so the plot is dense enough that nothing is hidden for centuries or deferred to a convenient age.
- Every act survives one "why?". If a boy with a perfect memory writes something, it is for a named reader. If there is no reason, cut the act.
- Thinking is near-instant. Never give a decision a duration. Durations belong only to reading, practice, waiting, and genuinely hard open problems.
- Name things plainly: the hair, the magic, the letter. Not "the other thing", not "the thing in him".
- No paragraph-ending twists. No trailing "because…" or "which was…" clauses. No paradox-shaped sentences dressed as logic. No scheduling prose ("the third day he spent on…").
- No caption-similes on people ("with the face of a man who…", "in the voice of someone who…", "and meant it"). Describe what they did.
- Characters say the half the listener needs. They do not explain the obvious. Dry characters (Figg, McGonagall, Snape) do not get sitcom buttons at the end of their speeches. Intelligent characters never say stupid things for a gag.
- Experts say expert-true things. The magic's rules apply to Harry exactly as to everyone else, with the reason stated when something seems to bend.
- Deliver each finished chapter immediately. Keep this file and the continuity ledger current so nothing is lost to context compaction.
- Stay clear of anything a safety classifier could misread: nothing cyber, nothing graphic, nothing that reads as instructions for harm.
- When Jacob pastes a line that reads off, find the pattern behind it and fix it in every chapter, not just the line.

## Additional habits to avoid (my own list, subordinate to everything above)

- Dashes as a rhythm device. Use commas and full stops.
- "Not X but Y" contrasts as a tic. Rhythmic triplets. One-line paragraphs for drama. "Something shifted." "A beat." "He realised" as a reveal. Rhetorical questions in narration. "Somehow." Overuse of "quietly", "simply", "just", "exactly".
- Narrating the premise. Show the slowness through concrete perception (a dropped spoon, a scan line, a word that has to be held whole before it means anything) and then stop pointing at it.
- Ending a scene on the neatest available line. End where the content ends.
