Superlinear lower bounds for problems in NP

What can actually be proved, with complete proofs, and where the line is between what is known and what has resisted decades of attempts.

Honest scope. Nobody knows how to prove that SAT (or any NP-complete problem) needs $\omega(n)$ time on a random-access machine, or that any function in NP needs superlinear-size Boolean circuits. The best explicit circuit lower bound for a function in NP is about $3.1n$. This page does not claim to solve that. It proves superlinear lower bounds for NP problems in models where that is possible, and explains why those proofs stop where they do.

Theorem 1: Palindromes need $\Omega(n^2)$ time on a one-tape Turing machine

Let $\mathrm{PAL} = \{ w \in \{0,1\}^* : w = w^R \}$. PAL is decidable in polynomial time, so $\mathrm{PAL} \in \mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{NP}$.

Theorem 1. Let $M$ be any deterministic single-tape Turing machine with $q \ge 2$ states that decides PAL. Then for all sufficiently large $n$ there is an input of length $n$ on which $M$ runs for at least $\dfrac{n^2}{20 \log_2 q}$ steps. In fact this holds for the average running time over a family of $2^{\lfloor n/3 \rfloor}$ inputs of length $n$.

The bound is tight up to the constant: the obvious zig-zag algorithm runs in $O(n^2)$ on one tape. (With two tapes PAL is solvable in $O(n)$, so this is a genuinely model-dependent statement.)

Setup: crossing sequences

The input occupies cells $1, \dots, n$ and the head starts on cell $1$. For $1 \le i \le n-1$, boundary $i$ is the edge between cell $i$ and cell $i+1$. The crossing sequence $C_i(x)$ is the list $(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k)$ where $p_j$ is the state $M$ enters on the step that is its $j$-th move across boundary $i$. Because the head starts to the left of every boundary, odd crossings go left-to-right and even crossings go right-to-left.

Lemma 1 (time). $\mathrm{time}_M(x) \ge \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} |C_i(x)|$.

Each step moves the head by at most one cell, so it crosses at most one boundary. ∎

Lemma 2 (cut and paste). Let $x = ab$ and $y = cd$ with $|a| = |c| = i \ge 1$, suppose $M$ halts on $x$ and on $y$, and suppose $C_i(x) = C_i(y)$. Then $M$ halts on $ad$, and its answer on $ad$ is its answer on $x$ (if $|C_i(x)|$ is even) or on $y$ (if odd). In particular, if $M$ accepts both $x$ and $y$, it accepts $ad$.

Let $C = (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ be the common crossing sequence. One observation drives everything: between two consecutive crossings the head stays on one side of the boundary, and every cell it writes is on that side (a transition writes the cell it is leaving). So what happens on the left side between crossings depends only on the left side's tape contents and the state at the moment of entry, and symmetrically for the right. We show by induction on $j$: right after the $j$-th crossing of boundary $i$ in the run on $ad$, the state is $p_j$, the left half of the tape equals the left half of the run on $x$ right after its $j$-th crossing, and the right half equals the right half of the run on $y$ right after its $j$-th crossing. For $j = 0$ (the start), the left half is $a$ in both $ad$ and $x$, the right half is $d$ followed by blanks in both $ad$ and $y$, and the state is the start state. For the step $j \to j+1$: if $j$ is even the head is on the left, and the run on $ad$ repeats exactly what the run on $x$ does on the left until its $(j+1)$-th crossing, entering $p_{j+1}$. The right half is untouched, and in the run on $y$ the right half is also untouched between crossings $j$ and $j+1$, so it still agrees with $y$. The odd case is symmetric, with $y$ in place of $x$. After the $k$-th (last) crossing, the head is on the left if $k$ is even and on the right if $k$ is odd. From then on the run on $ad$ copies the corresponding run on $x$ or $y$, which never crosses again and halts with that run's answer. ∎

The proof of Theorem 1

Let $m = \lfloor n/3 \rfloor$ and, for each $u \in \{0,1\}^m$, let $$x_u = u\, 0^{\,n-2m}\, u^R,$$ a palindrome of length $n$. Call a boundary $i$ with $m \le i \le n - m$ a middle boundary. There are $n - 2m + 1 \ge m + 1$ of them, and each one lies inside the block of zeros.

Claim. For each middle boundary $i$, the map $u \mapsto C_i(x_u)$ is injective.

Suppose $u \ne v$ but $C_i(x_u) = C_i(x_v)$. Split $x_u = a b$ with $a = u\,0^{\,i-m}$ and $x_v = c d$ with $d = 0^{\,n-m-i}\, v^R$, both at boundary $i$. $M$ accepts both $x_u$ and $x_v$, so by Lemma 2 it accepts $$ad = u\, 0^{\,n-2m}\, v^R.$$ But $(ad)^R = v\, 0^{\,n-2m}\, u^R$, which differs from $ad$ in the first $m$ symbols because $u \ne v$. So $ad \notin \mathrm{PAL}$, and $M$ would be wrong. This proves the claim.

Counting. Let $\ell = \lfloor m / (2\log_2 q) \rfloor$. The number of state sequences of length less than $\ell$ is $\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1} q^k < q^\ell \le 2^{m/2}$. By the claim, for each middle boundary $i$ fewer than $2^{m/2}$ of the $2^m$ strings $u$ have $|C_i(x_u)| < \ell$. Summing over all $u$ and all middle boundaries, then using Lemma 1: $$\sum_{u \in \{0,1\}^m} \mathrm{time}_M(x_u) \;\ge\; \sum_{u}\sum_{i\ \text{middle}} |C_i(x_u)| \;\ge\; (m+1)\,\bigl(2^m - 2^{m/2}\bigr)\,\ell.$$ Dividing by $2^m$, the average running time over the $x_u$ is at least $(m+1)(1 - 2^{-m/2})\,\ell$. Since $m \approx n/3$ and $\ell \ge m/(2\log_2 q) - 1$, this is $(1 - o(1))\, \dfrac{n^2}{18 \log_2 q} \ge \dfrac{n^2}{20\log_2 q}$ for large $n$. Some input does at least as well as the average. ∎

Theorem 2: Multitape Turing machines, via the time hierarchy

The one-tape bound depends on the single tape being an information bottleneck. Here is a superlinear bound for the standard multitape model, where palindromes are easy.

Theorem 2. There is a language $D \in \mathrm{DTIME}(n^2) \subseteq \mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{NP}$ such that no deterministic multitape Turing machine decides $D$ in time $O(n^{1.9})$. In particular, every machine for $D$ takes more than $c\,n^{1.9}$ steps, for every $c$, on infinitely many input lengths.

Fix an encoding $\langle M \rangle$ of multitape machines and a universal machine $U$ that simulates $t$ steps of $M$ in $c_M\, t \log t$ steps (Hennie–Stearns). Define $$D = \{\, w = \langle M\rangle 1 0^k \;:\; U \text{, simulating } M \text{ on } w \text{ for at most } |w|^2 \text{ of its own steps, does not see } M \text{ accept} \,\}.$$ Deciding $D$ means running $U$ with a clock of $|w|^2$ steps, so $D \in \mathrm{DTIME}(n^2)$. Suppose some $M$ decides $D$ in time at most $c\, n^{1.9}$. Choose $k$ large enough that $w = \langle M \rangle 1 0^k$ satisfies $c_M \cdot c|w|^{1.9} \log(c|w|^{1.9}) < |w|^2$. That is possible because $n^{1.9}\log n = o(n^2)$. Then $U$'s simulation of $M$ on $w$ finishes within the clock, so $w \in D \iff M$ does not accept $w$. That contradicts the assumption that $M$ decides $D$. ∎

The catch is that $D$ is an artificial, diagonal language, not a natural problem like SAT. Diagonalization also relativizes, and Baker–Gill–Solovay showed that relativizing arguments alone cannot settle P vs NP.

Why the general case is hard

  • Information bottlenecks disappear. Theorem 1 works because one tape forces $\Omega(n)$ bits to be ferried across each of $\Omega(n)$ boundaries. A second tape, or random access, removes the bottleneck.
  • Relativization (Baker–Gill–Solovay 1975). Pure diagonalization, as in Theorem 2, gives artificial languages and cannot resolve P vs NP.
  • Natural proofs (Razborov–Rudich 1997). Most combinatorial circuit lower-bound techniques would, if they worked against general circuits, break pseudorandom functions.
  • Algebrization (Aaronson–Wigderson 2008). This rules out the arithmetization tricks that get around relativization.

So the theorems above are real superlinear lower bounds for problems in NP. But an unconditional superlinear bound for SAT on a RAM, or for an explicit NP function against general circuits, would be a major breakthrough. Treat any claimed proof of one, including one produced by an AI, with heavy skepticism until experts have checked it.
