Chapter 1: Calibration

(Wednesday, June 17th, 1992. 6:40 in the evening.)

On Monday afternoon, an hour before the Hogwarts Express left, Fred and George Weasley had trimmed Hermione Granger’s fingernails with the Sword of Gryffindor.

They had insisted on a parchment first, absolving the Weasley family of any curse, doom, or hereditary misfortune that might attach to persons who clipped a unicorn, and Hermione had corrected the spelling before she signed it. Then Professor Flitwick had turned up out of nowhere, swept the clippings into a little lead-lined box, and refused to say what he wanted them for.

By Wednesday Harry had done the arithmetic. Human fingernails grew about three and a half millimetres per month, and alicorn sold in Diagon Alley—on the rare occasions anyone sold it at all—for a price per ounce that the apothecary had only been willing to whisper, so if you multiplied that out over ten fingers, and then threw in the toenails, which was only reasonable, then the Noble House of Granger could be financially self-sufficient by Christmas just from its own manicures, and that was before anyone had done a single experiment—

“I am not,” said Hermione, “selling my fingernails.”

“I’m only saying that you could—”

“Nobody,” said Headmistress McGonagall, “is selling anybody’s fingernails.” She did not sound like a witch who had expected, when she put on the Sorting Hat three days ago, that this would be one of the things she’d have to say as Headmistress. “Mr Potter, you told me that this experiment was potentially lethal, and that it had to take place inside my castle. I would very much like it to begin, so that it can be over.”

They were in Harry’s new office, at the top of a tower that couldn’t be seen from anywhere on the grounds, even though the grounds could be seen perfectly well from inside it, which would have kept Harry up at night if he’d had any nights to spare that week. Four glass walls, four chairs, a desk, and a ladder up to the roof.

“Right,” Harry said. “Um… Headmistress, before we start, I need to tell Hermione something. Alone. I’m sorry.”

“I shall be at the bottom of the stairs,” Professor McGonagall said finally. “If anything explodes, Mr Potter, I shall come back up.”

When her footsteps had gone all the way down, Harry took out the Elder Wand and cast three privacy Charms that he’d learned from a book on Monday and couldn’t manage with his own wand at all. The Elder Wand put up with this, in the manner of a wand that was still wondering how it had ended up being used by a first-year.

“Okay,” Harry said. “The thing you have to understand is that I can’t untell you this. Once you know it, you’ll always know it. There’s a reason ordinary Patronuses are animals: animals don’t know, so they’re protected from it. And if I tell you, and it turns out you can’t cast the other kind, then you’ll never be able to cast the animal kind either, ever, because you won’t be able to stop knowing.”

“I couldn’t cast the animal kind anyway,” said Hermione. “Not in January, and not since. I tried every night for a month.”

“I know. That’s the only reason I’m allowed to tell you. There’s nothing for it to take away.”

Hermione narrowed her eyes at him. “Is this what’s in the envelope? The one you gave me in January, with 42 on it?”

“…Sort of. It’s what’s in there, only less cryptic. Did you open it?”

“You said only to open it if I ever had to fight Dementors,” said Hermione, with dignity. “So no.”

So Harry told her what he’d seen under the tattered cloak, back in January, when the Headmaster had brought a Dementor to Defence class—what it really was, and why none of the books ever said so, and what Godric Gryffindor had called them. He told her what he’d been thinking when he looked straight at it. He did a bad job of it, because it had never really been something made out of words.

“So it isn’t exactly a happy thought,” Hermione said at last.

“It’s a happy thought that isn’t about something else.”

“That’s not a sentence, Harry.”

“I know. It’s the best I’ve got. And you can’t just borrow mine—it has to be yours, it has to be something you actually believe while you’re looking straight at it and not looking away.” Harry hesitated before going on. “I’m not even sure it’ll work without a real Dementor in the room. But we’re not bringing a Dementor into Hogwarts, because the last time somebody did, it got lost.”

Harry went to the door and called down the stairs, and Professor McGonagall came up them so fast that she had obviously been standing on the bottom step the whole time.

Hermione stood in the middle of the room and raised her wand.

“Expecto Patronum!”

A thin silver mist came out of the end of her wand, curled around once, and vanished.

Hermione glared at her wand, and then closed her eyes and stood there for a while with her lips moving silently, and then opened her eyes and looked up at the corner of the ceiling, the way she always did in exams when she was remembering something word for word, and said “Expecto Patronum” in her answering-questions-in-class voice.

And light came out of her wand, so bright that the glass walls threw it back at them and then out across the lake, where a whole flock of birds took off from the water at once. There was a shape inside the light—two arms, two legs, a head—the shape of a human being, the same shape Harry’s had been, because there was only one shape a True Patronus could have. It was brighter than Harry’s had been on his first try. Harry noticed that, and then noticed himself noticing it, and decided that he would have a talk with himself about that later.

Professor McGonagall had her hand up in front of her eyes, and was staring at Hermione through her fingers with her mouth open.

“Oh,” said Hermione, in a small voice, looking up at the glowing figure. “I always sort of thought it would be an otter.”

In January, when Harry had asked her what she thought her animal would be, she had only smiled and not said anything.

“Why an otter?”

“I don’t know! I just did.” She tilted her head to one side, and the figure got brighter. “Harry, it’s warm, it’s…”

It kept getting brighter, until Harry could feel the heat of it on his face; and then the edges of the shining figure started to blur, started to run into the light around it until you couldn’t see where the person ended and the light began…

…and Harry had seen exactly that happen once before, from the inside, in a corridor in Azkaban, and he knew what it meant.

“Stop! Hermione, let it go, now!”

She let it go, and the light snapped back into her wand, and all at once the office was dim and normal again; and Hermione sat down hard on the stone floor, as if her legs had decided that for her.

“That,” she said, a little breathlessly, “felt like being poured out of a jug.”

“It doesn’t run on magic.” Harry was kneeling next to her; he didn’t remember crossing the room. “That’s the part nobody knows, because nobody else can cast it. It runs on you. Keeping it lit is cheap. But making it burn, making it strong enough to actually destroy them—that comes straight out of your life. And the further away they are, the more it costs. So don’t ever push it like that unless you have to. Promise me.”

“I promise.” Hermione held up one hand and counted her fingers, which were all there, and which were also, now, glowing slightly. “How did you know when to stop me?”

“Experience.”

“Where?”

“I’ll tell you once you can keep Mr Bester out of your head.”

Hermione gave him a long look, and Harry could practically see her writing it down in some mental notebook with a heading like Things Harry Isn’t Telling Me.

“What thought did you use?” Harry said. “Just now. If you don’t mind saying.”

“I’m not sure yet.” She was still looking at her hand. “It wasn’t yours. Not exactly.” And then, before he could ask what not exactly was supposed to mean: “How many Dementors are there in Azkaban?”

“More than a hundred.”

“And when I go there, where will I be standing when I cast it?”

“We’ll work out the geometry,” said Harry.

Professor McGonagall had lowered her hand, and she was looking at Harry now, not Hermione.

“Mr Potter,” she said. “What about Miss Granger’s parents?”

“After,” Harry said. “That’s the plan. They find out after she comes back, so they just get the one pleasant surprise, instead of a whole week of—” He waved his hand around vaguely, to indicate Azkaban, the North Sea, and the general state of the universe. “—of that.”

“I was the one who told them,” said Professor McGonagall. “In April, in my office. Mr Granger asked me whether she had been frightened at the end, and I told him that she had not been.” She paused for a moment. “I have taught at Hogwarts for thirty-six years, Mr Potter, and that was the first lie I have ever told to a parent. I did not tell it so that you could keep it fresh for them while you arranged a surprise.”

Seventy-five percent she’s right, said Ravenclaw, and the other twenty-five percent is that she’s right and we just haven’t figured out why yet.

She was the one who had to tell them, said Hufflepuff quietly.

Slytherin, for once, had nothing to say.

“It’s not my decision,” Harry said. “It’s Hermione’s. And she agreed to it. On Monday.”

They both looked at Hermione, who was still sitting on the floor, and who didn’t say anything at all.

Hermione left a little after seven, saying something about studying for her O.W.L.s, and Harry was already thinking about whether you could focus a True Patronus with a lens, and he wouldn’t have noticed anything at all if she hadn’t stopped in the doorway and turned around.

“Harry? On Monday, when I asked you how literal the descent into Hell was going to be, and you said eighty-seven—was that how literal the Hell is? Or how likely I am to come back?”

“The first one,” said Harry.

“Oh,” said Hermione. “All right,” and she went down the stairs.

It was a few minutes later, staring at the empty doorway, that it occurred to Harry that this had been a strange question; and that he hadn’t asked her why she wanted to know; and that this wasn’t like him.

Chapter 2: Noticing Confusion

(Wednesday, June 17th, 1992. 7:52 in the evening.)

Professor McGonagall hadn’t argued, which was not at all what Hermione had expected. She had caught up with the Headmistress on the stairs and asked, all in one breath, whether Professor McGonagall would please take her to her parents’ house. Professor McGonagall had looked at her for a few seconds and then said only, “Side-Along Apparition is unpleasant, Miss Granger. You will want to hold on.”

Hermione was grateful for that, and also a little unnerved by it. She suspected that Mr Bester would have had something to say about why.

Her first Occlumency lesson had been that morning, in a white featureless room with a man in black robes, who had looked into her eyes and gone quiet for a while before saying, “Well. That was a troll.” But the part she kept coming back to was something he had said near the end. Your mind would try to protect you by not looking at things, Mr Bester had told her, and it was so good at this that you would never feel yourself flinch; you would only notice, later, that you had been thinking about something else. So when you notice that you keep thinking about something else, Miss Granger—that is where to look.

She had been thinking about something else for most of the afternoon, Hermione realised, and possibly since Monday.

They walked down to the gates together, since you couldn’t Apparate inside Hogwarts. Then the Headmistress held out her arm, and the world turned itself inside out, and Hermione found herself at the end of her own road being sick into the Pattersons’ privet hedge.

“Yes,” said Professor McGonagall, not unkindly, “it does that the first time. I shall wait here, Miss Granger, for as long as you need.”

Hermione put on the Cloak and walked up the road to her own house. It looked exactly the same as it always had, which was somehow offensive: the same green front door, the same brass lion knocker, and the same roses, which somebody was still deadheading. She went round the side of the house, through the gate that had never latched properly, and looked in through the kitchen window at her mother, who was cooking.

There was a wooden stand on the counter that Hermione had never seen before. On it was the fat brown cookbook with the broken spine, open to page two hundred and twelve, chicken in cider, and Mum was following it with one finger moving down the page and her lips moving slightly. Hermione knew that recipe by heart, the way she knew every recipe in that kitchen by heart. She had always been standing right there at Mum’s elbow saying it says two tablespoons, not three and you’re supposed to brown the chicken first—

—and Mum didn’t brown the chicken first.

She put it straight into the pan with the onions, and Hermione’s lips moved, brown it first, but nothing came out of them. Then Mum stopped, and read the page again with her finger on the line, and saw what she’d done. She stood there for a while holding the wooden spoon and looking down at the pan. Hermione had braced herself for crying, but Mum didn’t cry; she turned the heat down and went on, and did all the rest of it right.

In the sitting room the telephone rang, and Dad called “I’ll get it,” and didn’t, and after a while it stopped ringing.

On the doormat inside the front door lay that morning’s post, which nobody had picked up. On top of the pile was a brown envelope with a little window in it, through which Hermione could read the words FINAL NOTICE and, under them, MISS H. GRANGER. It was from the public library, and Hermione Granger had never in her entire life had an overdue library book.

She found that she was holding the handle of the back door so hard that the metal had started to bend.

There wasn’t any rule to stop her from just going in. Harry had said her parents would be told afterwards, and she had said Why, that’s very thoughtful of you, in a voice that anyone who actually knew her would have recognised. But she was the Head of a Noble House now, apparently, and Harry wasn’t her father, and nobody had made her promise anything. She could open this door and take off the Cloak and say Mum—

—and then tomorrow she would go to Azkaban, and if she didn’t come back…

Hermione made herself finish the thought, because Mr Bester had said to look where she flinched, and right now she was flinching so hard that it was almost a physical movement. If she went in tonight and died tomorrow, her parents would lose her twice, and that would be worse than if she had never come home at all. But if she didn’t go in, and died tomorrow, they would never get to see her again, not even once.

She could have stood there for hours being pulled both ways at once, except that some other part of her mind had just noticed something and gone very still.

Harry had never had to make this choice.

He hadn’t agonised over it at all, and Harry was someone who couldn’t walk past a moral question without picking it up and turning it over to look at the bottom. On Monday he’d told her, as if it were nothing, that her parents would find out after you come back from your mission, if you choose to accept it. The only if in that sentence was whether she agreed to go, not whether she came back. Then he’d said they would get one pleasant surprise instead of having to worry, as though a nice surprise and some worrying were the only two possibilities in the universe.

He had never once mentioned the possibility where she didn’t come home. That was strange, because Harry always mentioned that possibility; Harry mentioned that possibility when they were crossing a road.

So either he hadn’t thought of it, which as hypotheses about Harry Potter went had never once been true of anything, or else he had thought of it and found it empty.

All right, Hermione told herself. Look at it properly, and don’t skip any steps.

At her trial, in front of the entire Wizengamot, Harry had said that he would go to Azkaban and start snapping his fingers, and that it might cost him his life. Afterwards Professor Dumbledore had told her that Harry never lied, and that he really would have tried it, even at cost of his life. So destroying the Dementors was the kind of thing that could kill you, and this afternoon she had found out why. The True Patronus ran on life rather than magic, and the further away the Dementors were, the more it cost; she had felt that for herself, like a jug tipping over.

There were more than a hundred Dementors in Azkaban, and when she’d asked Harry where she would be standing, he’d said they would work out the geometry. That was not a thing Harry said. It was a thing Harry did, out loud and at length, usually while you were trying to eat your lunch, and the only time Harry didn’t tell you a number was when he didn’t like the number.

On Monday, when he’d asked whether she was willing to risk the life she’d just got back, and she’d said Risk my life?, she had seen him not nod. He’d warned her that he would have to keep some secrets from her—Dumbledore her, he’d called it—but keeping secrets wasn’t the same as lying, and Harry didn’t lie to her. He would find six ways not to say a thing before he would say something false, so if he hadn’t nodded, it was because risk was the wrong word. And afterwards, giving her the Cloak, he’d told her that he couldn’t risk himself adventuring, ever, and you… can.

Through all of it he had been calm, and Harry Potter was not calm about her. Harry Potter had once spent twenty minutes looking up whether a papercut could get infected.

Hermione ran the sum again, standing in her parents’ back garden, and it came out the same way every time: Hermione Granger, very probably, dies. Harry was calm about that, and his plans for her parents didn’t have any branch in them where she didn’t come home. Which meant that he wasn’t planning to stop it from happening, but to undo it.

There had been a man at St Mungo’s on the first morning, a thin man in grey from the Department of Mysteries, who’d come in before Professor Flitwick could stop him. He had waved his wand over her in a way that made her teeth hum, and then announced to the room that her soul was in excellent health and at least a mile away from her body. Professor Flitwick had removed him, by a route that Hermione was fairly sure had involved a window, and everyone had treated what he’d said as more gibberish, like the diagnostic Charm that said she was a unicorn who needed her mane combed. And she had been thinking about something else every single time she remembered it.

Fine, Hermione thought. I’m looking now.

If her soul was a mile away from her body, then something, somewhere, was holding on to it. Tomorrow, when the Patronus took the last of her life, Harry was going to go and get her back out of whatever it was, and of course he knew where it was.

She let go of the door handle, which stayed bent, and went back round the side of the house to the front path. There she pushed the letterbox open with one invisible finger, very slowly so that it wouldn’t clack, and reached in as far as her arm would go until she found the brown envelope by feel, and pulled it out and put it in her pocket.

Then she walked back down the road to where Professor McGonagall was waiting by the privet hedge, and took off the Cloak.

“You didn’t go in,” said Professor McGonagall.

“Not yet,” said Hermione, and her voice came out perfectly steady, which surprised her. “Headmistress? When you told my father that I wasn’t frightened at the end…”

The Headmistress’s mouth went thin, and she waited.

“Thank you,” said Hermione.

Professor McGonagall looked at her for a moment, and then, rather abruptly, held out her arm. As Hermione took it and the world began turning itself inside out again, she was already working out, very carefully, the first thing she was going to say to Mr Harry James Potter-Evans-Verres. It was going to be a question about where, exactly, he was keeping her soul.

Chapter 3: Precommitment

(Wednesday, June 17th, 1992. 11:48 at night.)

Harry sat on the roof of his office in the dark, clicking a dead Muggle laser pointer on and off and not really thinking about it. On the parchment beside him, weighed down with a stone, was the geometry.

He had exactly two data points to go on. In January he had destroyed one Dementor from five paces away, and it had taken something out of him, but a lot less than he’d expected. In February he had tried to destroy every Dementor in Azkaban from a corridor inside the walls, and it had very nearly taken everything. If the cost went up with the square of the distance, the way it would if the spell behaved anything like light—the only model he had—and if Hermione stood where his Vow would let her stand, on the roof, a hundred feet back behind the Aurors…

Then destroying every Dementor in Azkaban would cost somewhere between two and five human lives.

But Hermione wasn’t exactly human any more: there was troll in her now, and unicorn, and the man who’d put them there had said that only Fiendfyre and the Killing Curse need ever worry her again; and nobody, including Harry, had the faintest idea how much life that added up to. Probably enough to finish the job, Harry’s numbers said, and probably with nothing left over; and he had checked them three times.

When the trapdoor creaked behind him Harry turned, and Hermione came up through it the way she climbed everything now, as if the ladder were a small flight of stairs. She walked straight over to him and put a brown envelope down on top of the geometry.

It had a little window in it, and through the window it said FINAL NOTICE, and MISS H. GRANGER.

“My library book,” said Hermione, “is overdue.”

“I—what?”

“Harry,” said Hermione, “where is my soul?”

She already knows, said Ravenclaw at once. She’s asking to see what we’ll do.

Deny everything, said Slytherin, and Hufflepuff said No, and that was that.

“That’s a strange question,” said Harry, which was true.

“I’m not going to go through all of it, Mr Potter, because I’ve already been through it twice tonight,” said Hermione, in a perfectly level voice. “The short version is that I went home and stood in the garden, and you didn’t nod. It was never a risk, it was the plan—the spell kills me, and then you bring me back. So I’m asking you, Harry: where is it?”

Harry, for once in his life, had nothing to say. He reached into his pouch and said “Roger Bacon’s diary,” and the pouch gave it to him.

It was small and rough-hewn, with thick strange pages, and it was darker than it had been when Professor Quirrell tossed it across a dinner table to him in October—as though something had crept over it, the night in the graveyard, and never quite gone away. Harry held it out to her on his open hand, and Hermione stared at it.

“It’s a book,” she said.

“It’s Roger Bacon’s diary—he was a Muggle-born, actually, he turned down his Hogwarts letter, he did all his research without a wand, it’s from the thirteenth century—”

“I know who Roger Bacon was!” said Hermione, and her voice cracked. “My soul is in a book?”

“It’s a very good book,” said Harry.

“How does it work?” she said, without taking it from him.

“It’s anchored by a—” Harry began, and his mouth stopped.

It didn’t feel like deciding not to say something. It felt like reaching for the next step on a staircase and finding there wasn’t one; and along with the stop came the reason, as it had in the Headmistress’s office three days ago. Hermione didn’t know Occlumency yet, so anyone who looked into her eyes could take this out of her head, and once the principle got loose there would be people who’d murder a stranger every week for one more anchor, and then a hundred little Voldemorts who couldn’t be killed—

Harry’s mouth stayed shut, and Hermione went white, because he had told her on Monday what his Vow was for.

“Harry,” she said quietly. “Your Vow is about not destroying the world. And it just stopped you from telling me how my soul works.”

Harry nodded, since that was still allowed.

For a while neither of them said anything. Then Hermione sat down on the cushion across from his.

“Did someone die to make it?” she said.

“Yes.”

“Did they choose to?”

And for a moment Harry was back in the graveyard, watching the body Voldemort had just left push itself up on shaking arms, and hearing Quirinus Quirrell—the real one, a man Harry had never actually met—whisper Free… oh, free… A little while later, that man had been lying unconscious on the grass when the green light hit him, and he hadn’t even twitched.

“No,” said Harry.

“Was it you?”

“No! No. Hermione, no.” And then, because it was true, and because he’d spent long enough finding clever ways around things: “But I gave him the book.”

Hermione closed her eyes for a moment. “I had to ask,” she said. “I’d have asked anyone.”

When she opened them again, they were very bright, and not from the aura.

“You warned me on Monday,” she said. “You said that if I went down this path, you’d have to Dumbledore me, and not tell me some things—manipulate me, if only for a short while—and I said yes. I thought you meant secrets, Harry. I didn’t think you meant you’d keep my own death a secret from me.” She glanced at the parchment under the envelope—of course she’d read it upside down, she’d been reading things upside down on his desk since September. “So I’d die on the roof, and then you’d use that. How long would it take?”

“About forty minutes. I checked the ritual three times. The Stone does the body, the book does the rest, and the last part’s a Patronus—mine, that’s how you came back the first time—and then you’d wake up, and you’d be fine, you’d be completely—”

“And for those forty minutes,” said Hermione, “where would I be?”

Harry opened his mouth, and found that he knew the answer.

He’d been told it four nights ago, in a room lit with white light, by a man who had spent nine years and four months inside his own anchors with nothing to do but think. I spent most of my time looking at the stars. Harry had built a whole plan on top of that, and never once let himself think the next thought: and forty minutes, from the inside, would feel like how long?

“Awake,” Harry said. “I think. I think you’d be awake.”

“Do you know what I remember?” said Hermione, and when Harry didn’t say anything, she went on. “It was sunny.” She wasn’t looking at him now, but out at the lake. “That’s what I keep coming back to. It was a bright day, and the floor was marble, and there was blue fire everywhere, and I could hear it—not the troll roaring, the noise it made while it was… And nobody was coming. I was so frightened I couldn’t remember my own name. And then you came.” She turned back from the lake to look at him. “You were late, I think, but you came, and you were there, and I wasn’t alone at the end. That’s the only good thing I’ve got about it, Harry. And you were going to do it to me again, on a roof in the North Sea, and put me in a book for forty minutes afterwards—and this time you wouldn’t even be there.”

“You want to know why,” Harry said. His voice wasn’t steady, but there was a why, and she ought to hear it. “Right now there are people in Azkaban reliving the worst moment of their lives, over and over, with no end date, and every hour I wait is another hour of that for every one of them. There are two people in the world who can end it. One is you. The other can’t go anywhere near a Dementor, ever, because there’s a Transfiguration he has to keep up every second for the rest of his life, and Dementors drain magic, and if it slipped it would be very, very bad.” Without his quite meaning it to, his left hand had closed over the little green stone on his smallest finger. “I thought the Hell was going to happen anyway, if you chose to go. So I looked for the version where you couldn’t be lost forever. I thought I was giving you the safe one.”

Hermione heard him out to the end without interrupting, which was how Harry knew exactly how angry she was.

“On Monday,” she said, “you told me this was something I had to choose freely, if I did it at all. You said that was an actual mission requirement.” She looked straight at him. “How was I supposed to choose, Harry, when you hadn’t told me what I was choosing?”

In fairness, said Slytherin, a resource that regenerates is, by definition—

Listen to yourself, said Hufflepuff. Listen to who you sound like.

And Harry listened, and there it was. A resource that regenerates. That thought would never have occurred to Dad, or Mum, or Professor McGonagall, or Neville. It had the cold, patient shape of a mind that had spent fifty years among people it thought of as furniture, and it had walked into the middle of Harry’s plan wearing Harry’s face, and Harry had let it in, because the plan had worked. And he’d been doing to his best friend exactly what had made him want to scream at Albus Dumbledore: deciding for her, arranging her life and her death for the greater good, and calling it a kindness to her parents.

“I’m sorry,” Harry said. It came out small, so he said it again. “I’m sorry. I should have asked you. I knew, on some level, and I didn’t let myself look—which is the exact thing I decided on Monday to stop doing.”

“I’m still extremely angry with you, Mr Potter,” said Hermione, but a little more gently. “Now tell me the rest. Why the roof? Why can’t I be closer? Or—why can’t they just bring them to me, one at a time, in cages, the way they brought one to Hogwarts?”

“Because they’d learn. Dementors take their shape from what people expect. After the first one, everyone in that building would be expecting the Girl-Who-Revived to come for the rest, and the rest would scatter, and a hundred Dementors loose in Britain is worse than a hundred in Azkaban. It has to be all of them at once.” Harry swallowed hard before he went on. “And closer means near them. Normally they don’t Kiss anyone unless they’ve been told to. But since the night Bellatrix Black escaped, the Dementors in Azkaban have had orders to Kiss anyone they find outside a cell—and Madam Bones can’t take that back without telling them someone’s coming. A Kiss destroys everything you are, all at once. The book can bring you back from being dead. It can’t bring you back from that. There wouldn’t be anything left to bring.”

“Then—”

“And when you died in April, somebody heard a prophecy.”

“A prophecy? Another prophecy? Seriously, Harry?”

“Yeah, I know. It said, more or less, that without you I turn into something that destroys the world. So my Vow won’t let me take any chance of losing you for good, not even a tenth of a percent. The only way out written into it is if the Vow itself is leading to the end of the world and the friend I’ve confided in honestly agrees. So the only plan it would let me make was the one where you stand a hundred feet back, and the spell kills you instead, and I bring you back. Because that one was safe. For the world.”

“You chose for me to die,” said Hermione slowly, “because dying was the safe option.”

“…Yes.”

“The friend you’ve confided in honestly,” said Hermione. “That’s me, isn’t it? That’s the whole safety system, the only one it’s got: a friend who’ll tell you when you’re wrong. So what happens to your safety system, Harry, when the friend is someone you keep things from so she’ll stay safe, and she’s only allowed to do whatever your Vow has decided is safe? That isn’t a friend, that’s a hostage, and a hostage can’t tell you you’re wrong.”

Ah, said Ravenclaw, after a moment. Um.

“I told you on Monday that I wasn’t comfortable being the one thing holding you together,” Hermione went on, before he could answer. “Now it turns out it’s the whole world, and your Vow has decided the best way to keep me safe is to kill me on a schedule. I am not going to spend my life in a box so the world doesn’t end. If you’d end it without me, that’s a fact about you, and you’ll have to fix it yourself, because I can’t fix it for you by never doing anything.”

Harry opened his mouth, and closed it again. Somewhere in the back of his head, Ravenclaw was very quietly writing that down.

“So,” said Hermione. “Closer. How much closer?”

Harry tried to answer, and the Vow stopped his mouth again.

“Oh,” said Hermione, looking at his shut mouth. “It won’t let you tell me, because you think I’d do it.” She picked up the parchment from under the envelope and read it properly this time, the right way up. “Inverse square. So half as far costs a quarter as much, and a tenth as far costs a hundredth, and if I’m right next to them…” She looked up from the parchment. “Almost nothing. Isn’t it? And they can’t see me if I’m wearing a Cloak that hides people from Death. So I walk down into the pit with the Cloak on, right into the middle of them. And then I take it off.”

“I know,” said Harry hoarsely, because that sentence would come out. “I thought of that in April.”

“Of course you did,” said Hermione, who didn’t look at all surprised. “If I just stood in the middle and cast, some of them would still be fifty yards away. But if I take the Cloak off, they’ll come, won’t they? All of them, right to me. And the closer they come, the cheaper they get.”

“And if your Patronus fails when they’re that close, even for one second—”

“Then I’m gone forever, I know; you just told me. But I’m choosing between dying for certain, tomorrow, for the second time in nine weeks, alone, and then being a book for forty minutes—or a chance of not dying at all. I’ll take the chance, because it’s my life—it’s literally my soul—and I think I get to choose.”

On his desk downstairs lay the portkeys Amelia Bones had sent up that afternoon: two tarnished silver sugar spoons, which was apparently what the Department of Magical Law Enforcement used when it wanted to be taken seriously. One was set for the roof of Azkaban at ten tomorrow morning; the other was for afterwards, or emergencies. Harry climbed down the ladder, with Hermione behind him, and picked up the first one, and turned, and held out his hand to give it to her.

His hand stopped halfway between them, with his fingers closed tight around the spoon, and it would not go any further. It wasn’t that Harry didn’t want to move it; it was that moving it any further was no longer a thing that Harry Potter’s hand did. They both looked at it, and Harry said, “It’s my—”

“I know what it is.” Hermione stepped forward and very carefully started unbending his fingers from around the spoon, one at a time. She was being extremely gentle about it, and it took Harry a moment to understand why: she could have broken every one of them without noticing. “Your Vow is about what you do, Harry. It can stop your hand, but it can’t stop mine.”

The last finger came open, and she took the spoon.

And Harry’s hand came back to him all at once, so suddenly that he nearly fell over, and the realisation caught up with him about a second later than it should have.

“Oh,” he said. “Oh. You’re going whether I help or not. So now helping you is the safer thing to do—compared to the counterfactual. So the Vow doesn’t mind.”

“Yes,” said Hermione. “I worked that out on the ladder. And don’t bother asking Madam Bones to cancel the portkey, either, because I’d only go by broomstick, without any Aurors at all, and your Vow would hate that.” She tucked the spoon away inside her robes. “So. Is there anything you can tell me that will help?”

Harry thought about it—properly, this time, with all of himself.

“Yes,” he said slowly. “Don’t wait for them with nothing up. If you take the Cloak off and just stand there, they’ll drain everything out of you before they’re close enough—you won’t have a happy thought left by the time you need one. So light it first, small, as dim as you can make it and still have it hold—keeping it lit is cheap, remember?—just enough to keep them off you. Then let them come, all of them, and pile up against it as close as they can get, and then burn.”

Hermione stared at him for a moment. “…Oh,” she said. “That’s better than mine.”

“I’ve been in that corridor,” said Harry, “and you haven’t.”

There was one more thing, and Harry told her about the woman on the level above the lowest, behind the only steel door that was in the middle of its corridor, who kept saying No, I didn’t mean it, please don’t die, over and over. Harry had walked past her in February, while she screamed that she couldn’t remember her children’s names any more.

“If you get that far,” Harry said. “Afterwards.”

“And tell her what?”

“I don’t know,” said Harry, without looking up. “That somebody came back.”

After a moment Hermione held out her hand—not for the spoon, which she already had, but for the book. Harry gave her the diary of Roger Bacon, and she held it in both hands before she held it out to him again.

“You keep it,” she said.

“Hermione, it’s yours—”

“I know what it is. I’d only lose it, or someone would take it off me in a fight, and that would be worse.” She put it back into his hands and closed his fingers around it, the same fingers she’d just finished prying open. “I trust you to keep it, Harry. I don’t trust you to decide what it’s for.”

She was halfway out of the door when she stopped and looked back.

“Does Professor McGonagall know? About the book?”

“…No.”

“Tell her.”

“Tomorrow, I’ll—”

“Tonight, Harry,” said Hermione Granger, and went down the stairs.

Chapter 4: Constant Vigilance

(Thursday, June 18th, 1992. 9:41 in the morning.)

You could hear Mad-Eye Moody coming up the stairs from a long way off, clunk, step, clunk, step, and Hermione suspected that this was entirely on purpose, on the theory that an enemy who knew you were coming might decide not to be there when you arrived.

Professor McGonagall was already in Harry’s office, with the look of someone who had been told a great many things at two in the morning and had not yet finished being told them. Harry looked as though he hadn’t slept at all, which Hermione thought was only fair.

The door opened on the scarred man, his normal eye on Hermione and his magical one spinning around to look at everything else, and he pointed his wand straight at her and said, “Avada Ked—”

Hermione was standing behind him before he got to the second word.

She hadn’t decided to move; there had just been a wand pointing at her, and then there was the back of Alastor Moody’s grizzled head right in front of her nose. Across the room, Harry had flinched so violently that he’d knocked over his tea.

“Huh,” said Moody, without turning around. His blue eye had rolled all the way round into the back of his skull to look at her, which Hermione could have done without. “Voldie would’ve dodged.”

“Mr Moody,” said Hermione, “anyone would dodge if they could. Your test only works on people who are too slow.”

The blue eye considered this from several angles.

“Fair,” Moody said finally, and stumped into the room. “Good, girl. Means you want to live. Keep wanting it, down there. It’ll try to talk you out of it.” His blue eye swivelled round to Harry. “And you. Told me about the book last night. First sensible thing you’ve done since I met you, son—telling people things. Do it more.”

Harry opened his mouth as if to argue, and then, to Hermione’s private astonishment, closed it again.

Moody had brought her a present: a small brass button, which he sewed onto the inside of her robe’s hem himself, with a needle he took out of somewhere inside his coat, muttering a spell over every single stitch.

“If your heart stops for thirty seconds,” he said, biting off the thread, “this brings whatever’s left of you straight to me. And then Potter knows what to do with it.”

“That’s horrible,” said Hermione.

“That’s vigilance,” said Moody.

By nearly ten o’clock the sugar spoon was lying on Harry’s desk where she had put it, and Harry was standing at the window with his hands shoved into his pockets, staring at the lake, visibly not managing anyone.

Hermione went over and stood beside him. “Is it all right,” she said, “if I hug you?”

Harry looked at her for a few seconds before he said, “…Yes.”

She hugged him very, very carefully, as if he were made of spun sugar, which compared to her he more or less was. He was shaking a little, and Hermione decided she wasn’t going to mention it, then or ever.

“Harry,” she said into his shoulder, quietly, so the others wouldn’t hear. “Whatever happens, stay here. Please. Don’t come after me.”

There was a long pause before Harry said, “Okay. I’ll stay.”

“Ten o’clock,” said Professor McGonagall from behind them, in a voice that wasn’t quite as crisp as usual.

Hermione let go of him, and went to the desk, and put one finger on the spoon next to Mr Moody’s gnarled one; and there was a jerk behind her navel, and Hogwarts went away.

Azkaban stuck up out of the North Sea like something that had been put there to make a point.

They’d arrived on a raised square of steel at one corner of an enormous triangular roof, in a wind straight off the sea, with fog on the water in every direction. In the middle of the triangle there was no roof at all, just a gigantic three-sided hole going straight down; and even from here, under the cold, Hermione could feel a draught coming out of it, like a door left open that ought to have been shut.

There were about a dozen Aurors up on the roof, with their Patronuses around them in a ring—a hawk, a hare, a silver beetle, and, to Hermione’s surprise, an orangutan. The orangutan belonged to Auror Komodo, who had last seen her in April, when he’d stood in the Great Hall and told her she was under arrest for attempted murder. He inclined his head to her very gravely, as if he were greeting a colleague.

Mr Moody walked her as far as the top of the stairs that went down into the building, and no further.

“Don’t bother with that thing round your neck,” he said, jerking his chin at her Time-Turner’s chain. “Nothing turns in this place. Ministry’s not completely stupid.” He was quiet for a moment, and for once both his eyes were pointed at her. “Listen to me, girl. Down there, it’s going to show you things. It’ll show you the worst thing you’ve got, over and over and over. You remember that it only knows what you know. It hasn’t got anything of its own. Everything it shows you, it took out of your head.”

“I’ll remember,” Hermione said.

“Good. And girl—” the scarred man’s voice rose to a bark that made two of the Aurors jump— “constant vigilance!”

She took the Cloak of Invisibility out of her pouch and put it on, and disappeared. As it settled over her it sang, very softly, the strange not-quite-a-sound that she was starting to think of as its voice.

Then she went down the stairs into Azkaban.

Chapter 5: Inverse Square

(Thursday, June 18th, 1992. 10:06 in the morning.)

The lamps inside Azkaban burned gas, because magic didn’t last long in this place. They lit steel corridors and metal stairs going down and down, and huge grey doors with plain iron padlocks on them; and behind the doors there were sounds.

The Cloak kept the Dementors from seeing her, but it didn’t keep them from being there. The cold came up the stairwells to meet her, and with it came the feeling she remembered from January, of something drinking—every warm thing she had being drawn out of her, gently, toward the middle of the building. If she stopped walking, Hermione knew, she would sit down on the floor and never get up, and it would seem like a perfectly sensible decision; so she didn’t stop.

On the second-lowest level, in the middle of the corridor, there was a door where no other door was. From behind it a woman’s voice was saying, in the worn-smooth way of words that have been said far too many times: “No, I didn’t mean it, please don’t die. No, I didn’t mean it, please don’t die…”

Hermione put her hand flat against the steel, which was so cold that it burned.

“Not yet,” she whispered, and went on down.

On the lowest level the inside wall was colder than anything she had ever touched, because behind it was the pit. She found the seam between two of the big riveted plates, dug her fingernails into it—they didn’t even scratch—and pulled, and the plate tore off its rivets with a long scream of metal and curled back like the lid of a sardine tin.

She stepped through the gap, and down, into the bottom of the pit.

It was mud, black and soft, smelling of nothing at all. The three steel walls went up and up around her, and at the very top there was a little triangle of grey sky the size of a postage stamp.

And all over the floor of the pit stood the Dementors: more than a hundred tall shapes in tattered black cloaks, motionless in the mud. Hermione didn’t look under the hoods; she didn’t need to, because she could feel what was under them, the same thing under every one—a small bald grey head, and a mouth.

Her legs started shaking, and she let them shake, and walked forward into the middle of them.

At the top of a tower that couldn’t be seen from the ground, Harry Potter sat at his desk and didn’t move.

In front of him, in a neat row, lay the Philosopher’s Stone, a parchment covered in cramped writing, and a small dark book; and beside them a hand-mirror, through which Mad-Eye Moody’s voice had said She’s gone down, nineteen minutes ago, and nothing since. Professor McGonagall had made tea, which neither of them had drunk.

We could go, said Gryffindor, for about the fortieth time. The second spoon’s right there—

She asked us to stay, said Hufflepuff, and that was the argument that kept winning.

Three days ago, on his roof, with the sun coming up, Hermione had crossed her wand with his and sworn to help him, and trust in him, and sometimes go where he can’t go. At the time Harry had heard that as a figure of speech.

You’d have liked my first plan, he thought at the little green stone on his finger. You’d have called it efficient, and you wouldn’t have asked her either—why would you ask a piece? But it was a worse plan, and she found a better one in five minutes, because she was the one who’d have had to die in mine.

The emerald didn’t answer, being an emerald.

“Albus used to sit like that,” said Professor McGonagall, from the window.

“Like what?” said Harry.

“As though keeping very still,” said the Headmistress, “were something one could do on someone else’s behalf.”

Harry thought about that before he asked, “Did it help?”

“Sometimes,” said Professor McGonagall, “they came back.”

In the middle of the pit, surrounded by them, Hermione stopped.

I don’t have to do this, she thought. I could go back to the hole in the wall. I could tell Mr Moody it didn’t work. In January, in the snow, with the Headmaster’s Patronus guarding the cage, she’d tried to cast the Patronus Charm and nothing had happened at all. She had turned and run—and then turned around and run back, for Harry.

She took off the Cloak and folded it up quickly and neatly—she folded everything—and put it in her pouch, so that whatever happened next wouldn’t happen to it. Then she raised her wand and said “Expecto Patronum,” very softly, and made it small.

A little silver figure stood beside her in the mud, about her own height and no brighter than a candle. It cost almost nothing to keep it there, just a thin steady trickle, the way Harry had promised.

Every hood in the pit turned toward her at once.

Then they came from every side, and they didn’t stop at the light: they pressed up against it, a hundred black shapes leaning in against the edges of her little candle-flame, closer and closer, until the light was the only thing in the world that wasn’t them. Then the cold came down on Hermione like a weight, and the pit went away, and it was April.

It was a sunny day, and the floor was marble, and there was blue fire burning in pools on the floor, and the troll had her around the middle in one enormous hand and was lifting her toward its face, and she could see the sunlight on its teeth. And there was the noise, the noise she’d tried to tell Harry about and couldn’t, the wet crunching noise of something being eaten, and the something was her, and the pain went through her like light, and she was screaming, and nobody was coming, nobody was coming—

—and the memory stopped, and started over, on a sunny day, on a marble floor.

Far away, her Patronus flickered, and something came through the light where it had gone thin and touched her sleeve, and the cold of it was like nothing in the world.

Burn it now, said a small part of her that was still thinking, before it goes out!

But they weren’t all close enough yet—she could feel the far ones still coming—and if she burned it now, it would cost too much; and if she didn’t, the light would go out, and then there would be nothing left of her for any book to keep. She reached for the thought she’d used in the tower, the one she’d told Harry wasn’t quite his, and it wasn’t there—or it was there, but it slid off her like rain off a window. It had been a good thought, a true one: the stars, and the future, and all the people who would come after and weep to learn that death had ever existed. But it had been Harry’s thought after all, with her name written on it in pencil. Down here, with the sun on the troll’s teeth, it was nothing.

It only knows what you know, said a gruff voice, very far away. Everything it shows you, it took out of your head.

And Hermione, screaming on the marble in the sunlight, thought:

Then why does it always stop there?

Every time, it stopped in exactly the same place, at the teeth and the noise and nobody coming, and then it started again; it never showed her what came next. But she knew what came next—it was right there in her head beside all the rest of it. Somebody had come: Harry, late, kneeling on the marble in her blood so that she wouldn’t be alone. And then, nine weeks later, a loud bang, and a stone altar, and moonlight on a graveyard, and her legs, back.

The thing had rummaged through her head and taken out the troll and put it on. And it had left the ending behind, because the ending was the one thing in there that it couldn’t wear.

You had me, thought Hermione Granger.

You had me on a sunny day on a marble floor. You had all of me, every last bit. And you couldn’t keep me.

That’s your whole argument, isn’t it, the only argument you’ve ever had: everyone you take, you keep, everyone, for as long as there have been people. That’s what makes you a law of nature instead of just a monster.

But one counterexample is enough to disprove a universal statement—every book on logic says so in the first chapter—and I’m standing here.

So losing is something you can do. And we are going to make you do it again—for the woman upstairs, and her children whose names she can’t remember, and Mum at the cooker, and Dad not answering the telephone, and everyone in every one of those cells, and everyone else, everywhere, one at a time if that’s what it takes, until there’s nobody left in the world that you get to keep—

Her light had nearly gone out, and the nearest of them was right there, stooping over her, close enough to touch; they were all close now, every one of them.

Hermione opened her eyes, looked straight at it, and burned.

At the bottom of the triangular well that is Azkaban, on mud where nothing has ever grown, a twelve-year-old girl in black robes stands with her wand raised; and all around her, close enough to touch, more than a hundred hooded shapes are leaning in toward her face.

Then there is light, which doesn’t come out of her wand so much as out of all of her at once. It has the shape of a person, and at first it is not large; it is about the size of a twelve-year-old girl. And the hooded things that are leaning in toward her lean straight into it.

They don’t burn; they aren’t that sort of thing; they just stop. The light goes out from her through the whole pit in one ring, faster than sound, and climbs the three steel walls, and goes out through the top of the triangle into the grey North Sea sky, a pillar of white fire that the Aurors on the roof will afterwards describe in their official reports without being able to agree on a single adjective.

And it costs her. Harry’s arithmetic had said that close enough would be almost free, but Harry’s arithmetic had only two data points. The light pours out of her and keeps on pouring, past the little she had budgeted for, past a lot more than that, and she feels the jug tip, and keep on tipping, and she thinks, quite calmly, oh—the model was wrong—

—and then she isn’t anything, for a while.

In the tower, the mirror on the desk spoke.

“Potter,” said Moody’s voice, very rough. “Something happened down there. Biggest light I’ve ever seen. Whatever was in that pit, it’s gone, the whole lot.” Then, after a pause: “And the button’s gone warm.”

Harry went cold all over, because a warm button meant that her heart had stopped, and if it stayed stopped for thirty seconds the button would bring whatever was left of her to Moody.

His hand was already reaching for the mirror, to shout into it—get her out, go down there, do something—and it stopped an inch from the glass, and this time the Vow had nothing to do with it.

She had asked him to stay. She had taken the spoon out of his fingers, one finger at a time, so that this would be her choice and not his. And there was nothing Moody could do in thirty seconds that the button wouldn’t do better.

Harry put his hand down flat on the desk next to the book, and counted.

Eleven one-thousand. Twelve one-thousand.

Professor McGonagall had turned around from the window. She came and stood behind his chair without saying anything, and put her hand on his shoulder, and left it there.

Nineteen one-thousand. Twenty one-thousand.

The book lay on the desk in front of him, small and dark, and Harry stared at it and thought, if you’re in there, if you’re awake in there right now, I’m sorry, I’m so sorry, I’ll get you out, I swear I’ll get you out—

Twenty-six one-thousand—

“It’s gone cold,” said Moody’s voice, and then, “Crap. Crap. Komodo’s down there now, he’s got her. She’s breathing. She’s—hang on—she’s sitting up and arguing with him.”

Harry put his face down on his arms on the desk, next to Roger Bacon’s diary, and stayed there for a while.

The first thing Hermione did, when she was something again, before she had even thought about it, was reach down with both hands and touch her legs, and find that they were still there.

She was sitting up in the mud at the bottom of Azkaban, and somebody with an orangutan was trying to make her lie back down. All around her in the mud, in every direction, lay empty black cloaks, more than a hundred of them, with nothing inside them at all. Everything was so quiet that she could hear water dripping somewhere a long way up.

On the level above the lowest, a woman had stopped saying anything at all.

Chapter 6: Names

(Thursday, June 18th, 1992. 10:31 in the morning.)

Harry arrived on the roof of Azkaban in a gust of salt wind, with the Headmistress holding his shoulder rather harder than the portkey really required, and the first thing he saw was Hermione.

She was sitting on the edge of the steel platform with mud up to her knees, holding a steaming mug and gesturing with it at Auror Komodo in a way that strongly suggested she was explaining something to him at length.

Harry discovered that he had sat down on the steel roof, quite suddenly, without having decided to.

The second thing he noticed was the absence. In February he had felt this place from a mile off, the wounds in the world waiting at the bottom of the pit like a drain. Now there was nothing there at all, and Azkaban was a large, ugly, triangular metal building in the North Sea, and that was all it was.

Hermione saw him, and put down the mug, and came over and pulled him to his feet, very carefully.

“There’s someone I want you to meet,” she said.

They went down together without the Cloak, past the hissing gas lamps and the great blank doors. Behind the doors there was silence now, or crying, or once a man’s voice saying, over and over, in a tone of complete bewilderment, “It’s stopped. It’s stopped. It’s stopped.” Harry found that he still remembered every turning, and wished he didn’t.

Auror Komodo came down with them, and at the door in the middle of the second-lowest corridor he stopped and spoke for the first time.

“Mrs Hughes,” he said, in the same colourless voice he had once used to arrest Hermione. “Killed her husband in January. Blasting Hex, at home, in an argument. She told the Wizengamot she’d only meant to break a window, and they gave her life.”

Nobody said anything to that. Then Hermione took hold of the padlock and closed her hand, and there was a sound like a walnut cracking, and the lock fell on the floor in two pieces.

There was a short corridor behind the door, with cells along one side. Most of them were empty, but in the third one, on a pallet against the wall, sat a thin woman with her arms wrapped around her knees. She wasn’t saying anything; she was staring at the wall opposite her, as if she’d been listening to a noise for so long that she didn’t know what to do now that it had stopped.

Hermione broke the second lock, and then, instead of going in, stepped aside.

Harry went in and knelt down on the steel floor in front of the woman, so that his face was level with hers.

“I walked past you in February,” he said. “You were begging for help, and I walked past. I’m sorry.”

The woman looked at him without any recognition at all, which was fair enough; she had never seen him. Then her eyes went past him to the doorway, where Hermione was standing, glowing faintly in the gaslight with the aura she couldn’t turn off, looking like a picture of an angel from a children’s Bible, if the angel had just been rolling in a ditch.

“But somebody came back,” said Harry, and looked at Hermione too.

Hermione came in and knelt down next to him.

“Mrs Hughes,” she said gently. “Can you tell me your children’s names?”

The woman’s mouth opened, but nothing came out, and Harry watched her reach for the names, and miss, and reach again. He was just opening his own mouth to say it’s all right, it doesn’t matter, they’ll come back to you—

“Owen,” said the woman, and then, “Bethan. Owen and Bethan.”

She put her face in her hands and said the two names over and over. She said them as though they hurt, and she kept on saying them anyway.

Harry stayed where he was, on his knees. Beside him, Hermione didn’t say anything at all, which was, Harry thought afterwards, one of the cleverest things he had ever seen her do.

They climbed back up the stairs slowly, side by side, while Aurors hurried past them going down with blankets and chocolate and, in one case, a very confused Healer from St Mungo’s who kept asking where all the Dementors were.

“So,” said Harry, around the third flight, because if he didn’t say something light soon he was going to say something that wasn’t. “On a scale of zero to a hundred. How literal a descent into Hell, in the end?”

Hermione thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything.

“Ninety-one,” she said.

“I said eighty-seven!”

“I know,” said Hermione, with a sideways look at him. “You were close. For you.”

Chapter 7: Something to Protect: Roberta Granger

(Thursday, June 18th, 1992. 7:02 in the evening.)

On the evenings when she couldn’t face the cookery book, Roberta Granger made shepherd’s pie, because it was the only thing she knew how to make without it.

She had cooked to a running commentary for seven years. From the age of five, Hermione had stood at her elbow and read the recipe aloud, and then, quite soon, not needed to read it, and then corrected her: two hundred grams, Mum, not two fifty, and it says fold. In September that voice had gone off to Scotland, and Roberta had missed it, and had said so to the Verreses at Christmas, laughing, because it was the kind of missing that has a train timetable attached. Since April it had been the other kind, and she had bought a cookbook stand, which was a small and practical thing to do, and which she had been unable to look at directly ever since.

She was browning the mince when she noticed, through the front window, that at the far end of the road a very tall woman in a pointed black hat and a small dark-haired boy in spectacles were standing beside the Pattersons’ privet hedge and examining it with tremendous interest. The boy appeared to be explaining something about it to the woman, at length, with gestures. The woman appeared to be enduring this.

Roberta knew what a pointed hat meant. She stood very still at the cooker with the wooden spoon in her hand, and the mince went on spitting in the pan, and she did not let herself think anything at all, until the doorbell rang.

“I’ll get it,” said Leo, from the sitting room, and did not move.

At Christmas, when the Verreses came, it had been Roberta calling out Honey, can you get it? from the kitchen, with her hands full of turkey. For nine weeks now it had been Leo saying I’ll get it, about the telephone and the post and the cards from the practice, and somewhere in the second week Roberta had understood that it meant please don’t make me. She turned off the gas, and dried her hands on a tea towel very thoroughly, both sides, and went.

The first place her eyes went, when she opened the door, was to the girl’s legs. She would think about that afterwards, and not like it. They had stood together in a stone room in Scotland, she and Leo, and looked at a sheet, and at the shape the sheet made, and at where the shape stopped.

The girl was standing on the step on two legs, in black school robes edged with blue, in scrubbed shoes that had plainly been very muddy quite recently. Her hair was doing the thing it did in wet weather. She was holding, for some reason, a library book. And there was something about her, a sort of light that was not quite in the air, that made Roberta want to apologise for things she had never done.

Roberta had read the books, and she knew what could wear a face. She knew what had happened to the body, nine weeks ago, the morning after, and that nobody had ever told them who had taken it, or why.

“Hello, Mum,” said the girl. “I’m sorry I’m late. I’ve brought the library book back, it’s been in my trunk since Christmas, I know it’s overdue, I’ll pay the fine myself…”

Roberta held on to the door frame and said “Leo,” not very loudly. And then “Leo,” and then a third time, in a voice that brought him out of the sitting room at a run; and then he was behind her, and then he was past her, down on his knees on the step with his big gentle hands on either side of the girl’s face, turning it up toward the evening light, the way he turned faces in the surgery, the way he had turned this face to the light a hundred times since it had had any teeth in it to look at.

“Herm,” he said.

“Hello, Dad.”

“Open,” said Leo Granger, because he was a dentist.

And their daughter opened her mouth, and he looked.

Roberta looked too, because she could not help it; it was twenty years of habit. She looked for the little amalgam filling in the lower left first molar that Leo had done himself, the year Hermione was eight, while Hermione lectured him on the history of dental anaesthesia, and it wasn’t there. Nor was the chip on the upper left central incisor, from the climbing frame, the summer she was six. There was no wear on any of them. There was none of the faint translucency there ought to have been at the biting edges, nothing of the slightly irregular, slightly crowded, entirely ordinary mouth of a twelve-year-old girl who would have needed a brace in a year or two. Every tooth was perfect, and every one of them shone faintly, with a lustre like polished horn, and not one of them was any tooth that Roberta had ever seen.

By every rule she knew, it was not her daughter’s mouth.

Leo went on looking, and his thumb touched her front tooth, gently, as if it might come away.

“They’re all here,” he said.
