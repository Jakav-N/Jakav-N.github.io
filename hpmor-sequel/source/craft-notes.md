# How *Where He Can't Go* was written

The brief was: get good at writing fiction, then write a 10,000-word sequel to *Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality*, and make it good. Partway through came a second instruction: imitate HPMOR's style on purpose. These notes record what that took.

## 1. Research before writing

I read HPMOR's final arc (chapters 113–122) and the passages it depends on (the Azkaban arc, the Patronus chapters, Hermione's death and trial, the Grangers at Christmas) from a public LaTeX edition, and built a continuity bible before inventing anything. Several plot ideas fell out of that reading:

- **The True Patronus runs on the caster's life, not magic.** Destroying Azkaban's Dementors from a distance nearly killed Harry (ch. 55): "if I'm going to do this, I should do it when I'm closer to the central pit, it will take less of my life that way."
- **Harry secretly holds Hermione's horcrux** and an honest resurrection ritual (ch. 112, 119): "She can't die. I have her horcrux. But Hermione doesn't need to know about that. Not for one more week."
- **Her death is a world-risk under the Vow.** Voldemort heard a prophecy that without Hermione, Harry becomes "force of vast destruction," and the Vow forbids Harry "any chances" with that.
- **The last chapter sets up the next one.** It ends with Harry about to send her into Azkaban, rating the descent into Hell "maybe eighty-seven," not nodding when she says "Risk my life?", and arranging for her grieving parents to get "the one pleasant surprise."

Put those together and you get the plan HPMOR never states outright: she pours her life into the spell at a safe distance, dies, and Harry brings her back, without telling her. The sequel is about that plan and what Hermione does when she works it out.

## 2. Diagnosing my own failure modes

Before drafting I wrote down the ways my fiction usually fails, so I could hunt for them:

- abstract emotion words in place of events;
- explaining subtext the scene has already shown;
- dialogue that sounds like a thoughtful therapist;
- rhetorical tics (“It wasn't X. It was Y.”, tricolons, one-line paragraphs for false weight, portentous scene-ending lines);
- default imagery;
- conflicts that dissolve in conversation;
- sentimental landings;
- rushing the scenes that matter;
- characters who are only as smart as the plot needs.

## 3. Measuring the target style

Matching HPMOR deliberately meant measuring it rather than going on feel. A short script compared HPMOR chapters with my writing:

| | HPMOR (all 122 ch.) | practice scenes | draft 1 | final |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| median sentence (words) | 12 | 7.5 | 9.0 | 13 |
| sentences under 6 words | 20% | 40% | 32% | 21% |
| 'the way…' similes /1k | 0.08 | 3.87 | 1.34 | 0.67 |
| 'as though/as if' /1k | 0.55 | 3.10 | 1.70 | 1.01 |
| 3+ 'and's in one sentence /1k | 1.16 | 3.10 | 4.10 | 3.52 |
| 'just/even/really/actually' /1k | 7.01 | 2.32 | 2.26 | 3.69 |
| exclamation marks /1k | 3.89 | 0.00 | 0.35 | 0.76 |
| em dashes /1k | 5.65 | 4.65 | 0.99 | 7.55 |
| semicolons /1k | 2.33 | 0.77 | 5.45 | 2.77 |

My instinct for “tight” literary prose turned out to be the opposite of HPMOR's. HPMOR writes long, chained, clause-heavy sentences, uses ellipses and semicolons, and delivers its jokes deadpan inside full sentences, not as fragments. Its distinctive devices include:

- Harry's labelled inner voices, written as tagged italic prose;
- epithets ("the Headmistress," "the scarred man");
- calibrated numbers and named biases;
- the deadpan zoom-out ("There was a pause, during which…");
- big italic declarations of conviction at the Patronus moments;
- a present-tense tableau at a peak.

## 4. Practice before the real thing

I wrote two calibration scenes and had two independent critics tear them apart: a line editor, and an HPMOR expert who checked every claim against the text. They caught things I would not have caught myself:

- **Wrong inner-voice format.** I had written Harry's inner voices in script format. HPMOR never does that.
- **The Vow blocked something harmless.** My version stopped Harry from saying a name. The canon Vow only blocks acts that risk the world, and the reason arrives together with the block.
- **The key deduction was invalid.** "One pleasant surprise" is also what Harry would say if he thought she might die. The sound version had to be rebuilt from Harry's exact words in canon: the only *if* in his plan was her consent; at her trial he said breaking Azkaban "may cost me my life"; and he was calm about her.
- **The prose was choppy and full of stock phrases.** 40% of my sentences were under six words, against HPMOR's 20%, and there were stock phrases everywhere ("for a long moment," "her mouth did something complicated").
- **The ending could be stronger.** The dentist father's last line worked, but it could land harder: his professional identity check should *fail*, and he should believe anyway.

## 5. Drafting and revision

The first full draft ran about 14,100 words. I sent it to four independent critics: a structural editor, an HPMOR continuity-and-logic auditor that checked every claim against the text, a line editor, and an HPMOR fan reading for pleasure, who gave it 8/10. The main changes that came out of their reports:

- **Harry gets a real case and a real win.** He argues for his plan at full strength. Once Hermione takes the portkey out of his Vow-locked hand, the Vow *lets* him help, and he improves her plan: light the Patronus dim first and hold the Dementors off while they gather, then burn.
- **Canon things he'd actually said are used against him.** On the last morning he warned her he'd have to "Dumbledore" her, and he told her the mission had to be freely chosen, "an actual mission requirement". Hermione throws both back at him. She also uses the Vow's own escape clause ("the friend in whom I have confided honestly") to argue that a friend kept safe by secrets is a hostage, not a check.
- **The climax costs something.** Harry's inverse-square model was built on two data points and turns out to be wrong. Her heart stops, and Moody's dead-man button goes warm while Harry counts to twenty-six in the tower. His hand stops an inch from the mirror. This time that's his own choice to keep his promise and stay, not the Vow.
- **Canon errors fixed:**
  - McGonagall told the Grangers in her office, not their kitchen.
  - Hermione died "glad not to be dying alone", so the memory the Dementors replay now stops at "nobody was coming", and what it can't show her is that Harry came.
  - The diary darkened when it became a horcrux.
  - Destroyed Dementors leave empty cloaks.
  - Harry has two data points, not one.
- **Plot holes closed:**
  - Why the Dementors can't just be brought to her one at a time in cages.
  - Why a Kiss is the real danger, given that Dementors don't normally Kiss anyone unless ordered to.

Then came the most useful note, from the person I was writing for: *"There's AI ticks. You rarely even notice them. They aren't like HPMOR. And over-editing often worsens it. Your intuitions for what is good is off."*

That was right. My critics share my blind spots, so I stopped trusting taste and compared my text with HPMOR's 641,000 words, construction by construction. What I had been writing was polished literary fan fiction, not HPMOR:

- "the way…" similes at 17× HPMOR's rate;
- three-"and" sentences at 3.5×;
- "very" at 3×;
- past-perfect summary at 2×;
- HPMOR's conversational "just / even / really / actually" at a third of its rate;
- a tenth of its exclamation marks.

Some of my "fixes" had made things worse. I had cut em dashes because folklore calls them an AI tell, but HPMOR uses more of them than I did.

So I didn't polish again. I rewrote each chapter once in HPMOR's register, after rereading the HPMOR scenes closest to it (Harry and McGonagall in ch. 14, Hermione's point of view in ch. 21, the final rooftop in ch. 122, the Patronus in ch. 45, Azkaban in ch. 52–55). After that I made only deletions and canon fixes.

Then came a third note, after the reader had seen chapter 2: *"Avoid short, punchy sentences."* The numbers had been saying the same thing, since my median sentence was still 9 words against HPMOR's 12. The one-line dramatic beat ("The doorbell rang." "They were there." "Not the Vow, this time. Him.") is my habit, not HPMOR's. HPMOR puts that kind of beat inside a longer sentence, so in chapter 7 "The doorbell rang." became the end of the sentence before it: "…and she did not let herself think anything at all, until the doorbell rang." My first rewrite of chapter 2 overcorrected: its median sentence went to 22 words, and a third of its sentences ran past 40. So I rebalanced that chapter and made one pass over the others. In that pass I folded each beat into the sentence next to it, deleted the beats that only restated what the scene had already shown, and split the few sentences that had grown past 40 words. Across the whole story the sentence-length distribution now matches HPMOR's: a median of 13 words against 12, 21% of sentences under six words against 20%, and 5% over forty against 4%.

A last check, for errors only, caught a few more, which I fixed:

- Hermione came back eight weeks after she died, not nine.
- Portkeys don't work inside Hogwarts' wards, and Azkaban's wards allow "a few fast ways *into* Azkaban, all of them highly restricted, and *no* fast ways out" (ch. 55). The spoons now leave from the gates, and Moody's button calls an Auror instead of carrying her body out.
- In January the Headmaster's Patronus had winked out just before Hermione cast. It wasn't guarding the cage at that moment.
- A chip on a six-year-old's front tooth would have been on a baby tooth, so the climbing-frame accident now happens the summer she was nine.

**Gaps that remain, measured:**
- Sentences with three or more "and"s still come up three times as often as in HPMOR, and "the way…" constructions about eight times as often.
- The story has a fifth of HPMOR's exclamation marks and half its "just / even / really / actually".
- At about 11,800 words it runs over the 10,000-word brief. I kept story beats rather than cut them to hit the number.
