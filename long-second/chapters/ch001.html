Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 1

Two Months on a Doorstep

The first thing I ever learned was the cold. The second was that someone, somewhere, had already disagreed with it. —the journals, vol. I (begun much later, remembering)

Interlude: Privet Drive, 1 November 1981, shortly before midnight

The cat had been watching the corner of Privet Drive since dawn, and the cat was not a cat.

When the man appeared—elderly, silver-bearded, dressed in a purple travelling cloak that no one on Privet Drive would have approved of, had anyone on Privet Drive been awake to disapprove—the street lamps went out one by one, politely, as if they had been asked. The man put a small silver device back into his pocket and sat down on the garden wall beside the cat.

“Fancy seeing you here, Professor McGonagall.”

The cat gave him a flat look and became a severe-looking woman in square spectacles. They talked in low voices about the thing the whole hidden world was celebrating that night, and the two deaths at the centre of it that rather fewer people seemed to be mourning, and about the child.

“You can’t mean the people who live here,” said Professor McGonagall, rising to look at Number Four as though it had said something rude. “I’ve watched them all day. They are the worst sort of Muggles. Albus, this boy will be famous. Every child in our world will know his name. And these people will never understand him—”

“Which is rather the point,” said Dumbledore gently. “Famous before he can walk and talk. Famous for something he won’t even remember. Can you not see how much better off he will be, growing up away from all of that, until he is ready to take it?”

McGonagall opened her mouth, and found it full of objections too large to fit through, and closed it again.

The motorcycle fell out of the sky at a quarter past, carrying a man twice too large for it and a bundle of blankets much too small. There were tears, most of them Hagrid’s. There was a letter, tucked inside the blankets with care. There was a minute, perhaps longer, when the greatest wizard of the age stood over the sleeping child on the doorstep of Number Four and looked very old, and said something quiet that neither of the others heard.

Then the street was empty. The lamps came back on. A cloud crossed the moon, and on the doorstep the bundle of blankets shifted, a small hand working free into the November air.

Albus Dumbledore was, that year, the most accomplished wizard alive, and he had thought the matter through with very great care. He had considered the wards that would grow from a mother’s sacrifice and an aunt’s grudging roof. He had considered fame and its particular poison. He had considered Voldemort’s followers, the Ministry, the press, and his own heart, which he trusted least of all.

He had considered everything, in short, except the one thing that no one alive could have considered, because it had never happened before, to anyone—and it was lying on the doorstep in a basket of blankets, and it was about to wake up.

The cold came first.

It came in slowly, the way everything came in now—and that was the thing, that was the enormous thing he had no words for, because he had almost no words at all: everything had gone slow. Or he had gone somewhere the slowness lived. The cold crept up through the blankets like a tide that took hours to cross an inch, and he had time to feel it arrive, time to dislike it thoroughly, time to grow bored of disliking it, all before it finished arriving.

He was fifteen months old. What he had, in the way of a mind, was what any small child has: a warm bright patchwork of faces and sensations, a handful of almost-words, a limitless appetite, and no idea whatsoever that he was anyone in particular. On an ordinary night he would have cried, and slept, and remembered nothing.

This was not an ordinary night. Something had happened to him the night before—a green that was not like other greens, a voice, an ending—and whatever else that something had done, wherever else its marks had landed, it had reached into the small soft clockwork of him and spun the one wheel that mattered up to a speed no human wheel had ever turned.

He woke on a stone doorstep in Surrey, in the dark, in the cold, inside a single second that went on and on and on.

He cried, of course. He was a baby; it was the tool he had. And this was the first lesson the new world taught him, before language, before names, before anything: the cry left him and nothing happened. The sound itself came out wrong—long, bending, a foghorn heard through deep water—and then it was gone into the dark, and no one came. No one came for what felt like days. He lay in the vast silence between his own sobs, each one a slow tide going out, and somewhere in that silence the crying stopped being something happening to him and became something he was doing, a thing with a shape, a lever that was not attached to anything. He let go of the lever.

That was the first night of his life, and in one sense it lasted about seven hours, and in the sense that counts it lasted two months, and he spent almost all of it learning the three objects available for study.

The first was the cold, which has been mentioned, and which repaid study poorly.

The second was the light. There was a lamp somewhere above and to the left—orange, steady, humming a note so low and stretched that it was less a sound than a place. The light did not change, but the sky behind it did, so slowly that watching it move was like watching the minute hand of a clock, if a minute hand were the lid of the whole world. He watched a star cross a gap between rooftops. It took an age. He had an age. It was the first thing he ever watched all the way to the end on purpose, and when the star slid at last behind the chimney he felt something small and fierce that, years later, with a vocabulary, he would identify as satisfaction.

The third object was the warmth, and the warmth was the strange one.

The blankets were ordinary blankets. He knew nothing about blankets, but even to a baby, cloth is cloth: it keeps the warmth you bring to it and gives nothing of its own. These blankets gave. When the cold tide crept too far up, something in the blankets rose to meet it—a soft pushing-back, a warmth arriving from nowhere, exactly where the cold bit worst and nowhere else. He did the experiment before he knew what an experiment was: pushed one hand out into the night air until it ached, pulled it back in. The warmth found the hand. He did it again. The warmth found it again, patient, unbothered, as many times as he asked.

Somebody had disagreed with the cold. Somebody had left the disagreement here, folded into cloth, working all night with no one to see it work. He had no words for any of this and would not for another year, but the shape of it went in deep and stayed: the world had rules, and something in the world could tell the rules no, and the no was kind.

He fell asleep testing it, and in his sleep the green came back—the green, and the voice, a woman’s voice, two slow syllables he had used to know—and even the dream ran long now, long enough to live in, and this, too, he would keep.

Much later—years later, when he had clocks, and numbers, and a cupboard to be systematic in—he would measure it properly, with a pendulum made of washers and string and a counting trick for the gaps. Call it two hundred to one. Two hundred seconds of him to every second of the world. Two hundred days inside each day; a fat subjective fortnight in every hour; between any tick and its tock, time enough to think a thought the whole way through, turn it over, and file it. But on the doorstep he had none of that, no numbers at all. He only knew that the night was an ocean, and he was in it, and it could not be cried at, and the star had crossed the gap, and the warmth kept coming.

By morning he had stopped being afraid of the dark. There was too much of it, too steadily, for fear to keep its footing; fear is a sprinter, and the night asked for something with longer legs. What settled in fear’s place had no name yet either. Patience is close, but patience implies effort. It was more like the beginning of a long professional acquaintance with time itself.

The sky went grey, then pearl. A bird started up somewhere, its song unrolled into a slow bright procession of separate notes, each one arriving like a scheduled train, and he was listening to it with his whole mind—there was so much room in a sound, nobody could have guessed how much room—when the sky wheeled, enormous shapes crossed it, and a face came down at him like a falling moon.

The face had curlers in its hair and an expression he would come to know professionally. The mouth opened. The scream took a very long time to arrive, and when it arrived it went on, a siren winding slowly up through its registers, and he lay in the warm disagreeing blankets and watched the sound happen the way he had watched the star.

That was his aunt. That was the first morning. The letter lay under his hand, and seventy miles away a hidden world was toasting his name, and he was two days old, or two months, depending on how you counted.

Depending on how you counted would turn out to be the story of his life.

Of the time that followed—the cot years, the kitchen-floor years—he remembered, afterwards, all of it. Whatever had spun the small wheel of him up to its impossible speed had built the shelves to match. The days did not run together the way anyone else’s second year runs together. They stayed where he put them. Years later he could walk back into any afternoon he chose and find it waiting, down to the weather on the window.

The house at Number Four resolved slowly, the way a photograph develops. He assembled it by sound long before he was allowed to walk its rooms: the hall clock with its fat dry tick (an event; a landmark; the metronome of the universe), the boiler’s whoosh at seven, the letterbox’s double clack, the particular grief of the stairs under his uncle’s tread. He knew the house the way a sailor knows a coastline from offshore.

The people took longer, because the people were enormous and arbitrary and mostly cross. There were three. He sorted them long before he could name them.

The Sharp One moved fastest and was afraid of almost everything, though it took him a long time to work out that the thing she was most afraid of was him. She looked at him the way one looks at a stain that might spread. But she fed him, on a schedule as reliable as the boiler, and reliability was currency in his economy; he could do a great deal with anything that repeated.

The Purple One was a weather system: slow to build, loud at landfall, gone by supper. The trick with weather is not to argue with it. He had worked that out by the second winter, which for him was the second half-century, and after that his uncle's rages passed over him like fronts over a well-built shed.

The Small Loud One was, he eventually accepted with real surprise, the same kind of thing as himself—another child, two months older by the calendar and getting on for a thousand years younger by any measure that counted. Dudley wanted things, screamed, got them, and wanted new things, a loop that ran all day with the dull dependability of the hall clock. He was also, and this was the useful part, a torrent of experimental data: everything Harry was not permitted, Dudley was given, so everything—toys, telly, tantrums, trips—could be observed in trial without the cost of running it himself. Other people, he understood very early, were experiments the world had already paid for. You only had to watch.

And he watched. Great heavens, how he watched. There was nothing else to do.

This is the part that is hard to say lightly, and it should be said anyway, once, so that it never needs saying again: a mind is an engine, and an engine without fuel does not idle politely. It eats itself. Boredom—real boredom, the deep kind, boredom at two hundred to one in a playpen in a spotless silent lounge—was the first enemy of his life, the first thing he ever met that could actually kill him, and he knew it the way a child knows a hot stove, wordlessly and absolutely. The afternoons were a hundred days long. The nights were worse. No one was coming with fuel. If the engine was to run, it would have to run on what it could make.

So he made things. It began as flailing—counting games, at first with nothing to count but heartbeats and the hall clock, and the games grew rules, and the rules grew consequences, until by the time he was three the counting had become a kind of kingdom, with provinces (the sevens, the dozens, the lovely treacherous elevens) and laws and border disputes that could be prosecuted for a profitable week inside a rainy morning. He mapped the sounds of the house and set them to work as a calendar, a clock, an early-warning system. He took the day’s thin events—a milk float, a dropped fork, one of Dudley’s cartoons overheard from the hall—and rebuilt them at leisure, better: the fork falling in twelve ways, the cartoon’s story repaired where it was stupid, which was mostly everywhere. He discovered that a remembered thing could be walked through if you fixed it to places—he hung his numbers on the cot bars and his words, when the words came, in the rooms of the house like coats on pegs—and so, in the second year of his life, in a terraced house in Surrey, a small boy in a hand-me-down babygro independently reinvented the memory palace of Simonides, and furnished it, and moved in.

It was not a sad childhood, whatever it looks like from out here at one-to-one. That is the other thing that must be said once and then left alone. From inside, it was a crowded childhood: crowded with projects, with campaigns in the counting kingdom, with the slow delicious assembly of the great jigsaw of English. He was never alone in there. He was the king and parliament and census of a country of one, and the country was always busy, and when he thought about the stain-look his aunt gave him it was mostly with the distant interest of a meteorologist logging one more kind of weather.

But the engine needed fuel, and the richest fuel in the house was language, and language is where he spent his treasure.

Speech, at two hundred to one, is geology. A sentence is an era. Between the first syllable of a word and its second, there was time to take the sound apart, hold the pieces to the light, compare them with every neighbouring sound in the collection, and shelve them, and wait, refreshed, for the second syllable to lumber in. He heard perhaps an hour of honest talk a day—the Dursleys were not a talkative family, except at the television—and that hour was two hundred hours long, and he mined it to bedrock. Grammar surrendered slowly, a border province at a time. Words he could not crack he stored whole, labelled with their contexts, three hundred of them waiting in a lobby of the palace like strangers at a visa office, each one eventually processed, stamped, admitted.

By his second birthday, by the calendar, he understood effectively everything said in that house, including most of what was said over his head in the deliberate spelled-out code the Sharp One used for secrets—P, A, R, K was an early, valuable break—and the television had become what the lamp and the star had been on the doorstep: the brightest object in the sky. The television! A firehose of worlds. He was not often allowed in its room, but walls are nothing to sound, and he lay in the lounge playpen or sat small and forgotten in the hall and drank it: adverts, snooker, the shipping forecast, Dudley’s cartoons, the nine o’clock news with its grave slow music. The news was the best thing in his universe. The news said, every night, in the voice of final authority: the world is enormous, and full of events, and it is out there.

Speaking was another matter. His mouth was not fast; nothing about his body was. The tongue is a slow student even for ordinary children, and he had to drill it in real time like everyone else—silently, at night, under the blanket, shaping soundless syllables over and over while the house ticked around him. He was in no hurry. He had learned the shape of his situation by then, learned it from a thousand small returns of the same lesson: his advantages lived inside; outside, he was a very small person in a house of large suspicious weather, and anything the weather noticed, it rained on.

He did not yet have the rule in words. The words arrived the day he made his first real mistake.

He was two and a half. It was a Tuesday in spring, the back door open to the garden for the first warm day, and the Sharp One had spent twenty minutes—a two-month expedition, from where he sat—turning the kitchen upside down for her shopping list, with rising notes in her voice that promised weather by lunchtime. He knew where the list was. He had watched Dudley, that morning, in the way he watched everything, peel it off the pad and post it with ceremony into the gap behind the cooker, for no reason whatever, because Dudley was two and a half.

The calculation that followed was quick, which is to say it took him most of an hour and she had barely crossed the kitchen in it. Distress, cause known, remedy cheap. Helping would cost nothing and might purchase goodwill, and goodwill converted, at a miserable exchange rate but reliably, into the outdoors. He had been drilling the sentence silently for weeks, as he drilled all his sentences, a long patient queue of them waiting for their moments. He stood up in the playpen and deployed it.

“Behind the cooker, Aunt Petunia,” he said. “Dudley posted it there this morning.”

Clear as a newsreader. Consonants finished, the way no child of two finishes consonants. Posted. He was proud of posted.

His aunt did not move for one and a half seconds, which gave him a long, long time to study what was happening to her face, and what was happening to her face was not gratitude, and it was not even surprise. It was the stain-look, arrived suddenly at flood stage. It was fear, white and total, and her hand came up to her collarbone, and she said—quietly, which was the worst part, she was never quiet—

“Just like—”

—and stopped the sentence with her own mouth, as if a syllable more might let something out of a drawer. Then she took the list out from behind the cooker without looking at it, and took Dudley upstairs on her hip, and the kitchen door clicked, and he stood alone in the playpen in the first warm day of the year, listening to the house tick, with an era of uninterrupted time in which to review his results.

The data, he found, were quite clean.

Item: she had not been pleased. Item: she had been afraid, and of nothing in the room but him. Item: the fear had an old shape, worn smooth, a groove her face already knew the way the stairs knew his uncle’s tread—this was not the first time she had been afraid of a child who spoke too well. Item, from the archive: just like. Just like whom? There was a whom. Somewhere upstream of him there was a whom, filed in the same drawer his aunt had just slammed, and the drawer had a label, and he had heard the label through the wall at night in his uncle’s weather-voice, and the label was freak.

He turned the word over for a while, there in the slow Tuesday light. It did not hurt. He examined it carefully to see whether it would hurt and found only taxonomy: the house contained a category for people whose insides did not match their outsides, the category was feared, and he was in it. Noted. Everything useful he had, he had because his insides did not match his outsides; the mismatch was the whole estate. The error had not been having it. The error had been showing it, spending a two-month drill on a sentence that bought him nothing and cost him the one thing he could not afford, which was the attention of the weather.

So he made the rule, the first law ever passed in the country of one, and he made it in words, because by then he had plenty:

Be small.

Not forever, he amended at once—even then, even at two, the amendment went in before the ink was dry. The world was enormous and full of events and it was out there; the news said so every night; smallness was a tactic, not a destiny. Be small here. Be small for now. Fold the whole estate down until it fits behind a toddler’s face, pay out the difference in patience, and wait.

He was, as has been mentioned, very good at waiting.

The waiting had a shape, and the shape was a campaign, and the campaign took a year.

He became, by degrees too fine for the Dursleys to see as degrees, a convenient child. Not a charming one—charm drew the stain-look; he had the data on that now. Convenient. Quiet in the right ways, at the right hours. A finder of lost things, provided the finds were spaced out and plausibly accidental: the car keys in the fruit bowl, the chequebook under the Radio Times, each discovery costing him nothing but surveillance he was doing anyway, each one lowering, by a fraction of a degree, the temperature of suspicion that followed him around the house. He learned to be where work disappeared from: shoes paired by the door, toys herded to Dudley’s end of the lounge. The Sharp One never thanked him and never quite caught herself ceasing to mind him, which was the intended product. You cannot make such people love you, he had concluded, watching her the way he watched everything; her drawers were shut and nailed. But fear needs fuel, and a sufficiently boring child starves it.

He gave her eleven months of boring, and then, on a bright cold October morning, standing in the hall in his duffel coat at precisely the moment the logistics of taking Dudley to the shops collided with the horror of leaving Harry in the house unsupervised, he looked up and deployed—in the flat Surrey vowels of the house, consonants carefully unfinished, a sentence rehearsed so many hundreds of times that it had worn its own groove in him—

“I c’n hold the basket.”

Four words. A year of engineering. His aunt looked down at him, and he watched the cost-benefit run across her face like cloud shadow crossing a lawn, slow, slow, and he kept his own face toddler-blank through all of it, and at the end of it she said, “You’ll walk and not dawdle,” and took down his reins from the peg.

And the door opened, and the world came in.

He had seen the front garden. He had seen the car’s windows, on trips to Mrs Figg’s at the end of the road, who smelled of cabbage and kept a great many cats and of whom much more would need thinking later. He had not seen this: the whole street at walking pace and his own eyes free to spend. It was two hundred yards to the parade of shops and he passed through them the way other men pass through a decade. Every gate a different latch, every car a different badge with different words on it, a postman whose bag's settling told him roughly what the post weighed, pavement cracks running their own slow civil engineering seminar, and the people, the people—every face doing a dozen things at once and all of it legible at his speed, boredom and hurry and small kindnesses, a man lying cheerfully to a neighbour about a hedge. The visit to the greengrocer's alone was a university. The basket was heavy. He held it the whole way, both arms, because he had said he would, and because the saying and the doing being the same thing was going to be, he had already decided, part of the estate.

And then, outside the newsagent’s, the world—which had already given him more that morning than the whole house had given him in a year—leaned down, unasked, and handed him the key to everything else.

A man was standing at the rack with a newspaper open. Harry knew newspapers; one came through the Dursleys’ letterbox every day with its double clack and was read by the Purple One at the kitchen table like a wall being held up. He knew the marks on them, rank upon rank of grey marks, and he had long filed the marks where he filed most mysteries, in the lobby, pending. Now he stood at knee height in the cold bright morning and watched the man’s eyes, and the eyes were moving—jump, rest, jump, rest, little precise saccadic hops along the ranks of marks, line after line, the exact motion of his aunt’s eyes crossing a letter, his uncle’s crossing the sports page, the newsreader’s crossing the thing below the camera. The eyes walked along the marks, and at the end of some lines the man’s face changed, a flicker of the same traffic that talk produced.

The marks were speech. Fixed speech. Speech that stood still and waited for you, that could be read as slowly as you liked, as often as you liked, speech from people who had never set foot in Little Whinging, from people—the thought arrived with the force of the doorstep cold—who might even be dead, and it would keep. Every rank of marks was a voice that could not be used up. The newsagent’s rack alone held more fixed speech than the Dursleys produced in a month. And somewhere—the news had shown him pictures, shelves and shelves in buildings built for nothing else—somewhere there were rooms of this.

His aunt took his hand, and he walked home at the end of the reins, three years old or six hundred depending on how you counted, past the gates and the latches and the slow seminar of the pavement, carrying the first page of the rest of his life.

The marks could be learned. Dudley owned books he never opened; the house took a newspaper every day; the television wrote words at the bottom of its pictures and spoke them aloud at the same time, which made it, he saw now, a patient tutor that would drill him for hours and never once notice him learning, never once look down and be afraid.

Be small, said the first law, and he would be, he would be the smallest thing on Privet Drive, a convenient, boring, duffel-coated nothing, for exactly as long as it took.

A second was a long time, after all. And he had so very many of them.
