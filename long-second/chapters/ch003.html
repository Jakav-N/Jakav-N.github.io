Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 3

The Lending Library

People speak of hunger for knowledge as a figure of speech. I should like them to spend one year at two hundred to one in a house with a phone book, and then we can discuss figures of speech. —the journals, vol. I

The sentence, when it finally came, was even better than the one he had designed, because he had not written it. Miss Henmore had.

It arrived in July, on the last parents’ afternoon of the nursery year, in front of two other mothers and the vicar’s wife, which Harry could not have arranged better with a year’s notice and suspected Miss Henmore of not arranging at all; kind people, he was learning, wandered into perfect positions the way rivers found the sea. She had taken Aunt Petunia’s hand in both of hers and said, warmly, audibly:

“You must be taking him to the library every week, Mrs Dursley—a reader like that. It’s the single best thing a family can do.”

And there it was. Not a request from Harry, which would have been refused on principle; not advice in private, which could be quietly binned; but a public assumption of virtue, witnessed by the vicar’s wife, with only two available replies: yes, of course we do, which became a commitment the moment it was spoken, or a correction, which was unthinkable—what sort of family would one have to be? Harry, standing at knee height with his face arranged into the solemn middle distance of the odd clever child, watched his aunt say, “Yes—well—of course,” and felt the latch of the last door between him and the sea lift with a small, silent, well-oiled click.

The following Saturday, in her second-best gloves, wearing the expression of a woman discharging a tax obligation, Petunia Dursley walked her nephew the long quarter-mile to the Little Whinging branch library and signed, at the counter, the form that changed the economics of his existence.

He behaved very badly in the first minute, by his own standards, and forgave himself later. He stopped dead on the threshold. He simply stood, holding up foot traffic, a small boy in Dudley’s cut-down shorts, and looked.

It was one medium-sized room of a suburban branch library: parquet, radiators, a smell of polish and old paper, perhaps nine thousand books. It was the largest concentration of recorded human thought he had ever been in a room with, by a factor of roughly two hundred, and the symmetry of the number did not escape him. Shelf after shelf after shelf of fixed speech, voices that could not be used up, arranged—he could see the system from the door, numbers on the spines, order breathing through the whole room like the ticking of a vast calm clock—for retrieval. Somebody had not merely collected the sea. Somebody had indexed it.

“Well,” said Petunia, already at the door, gloves twitching, “you’ve seen it. Choose your books. Quickly.”

Junior members were entitled to three tickets. Three books a week. He had done the arithmetic before they arrived and done it again, bleakly, standing in the children's corner with its wire carousel of picture books: at his reading speed—eye-speed, the eyes were always the bottleneck—three books was an afternoon, and the week had six more days in it. The sea, and a teaspoon to drink it with.

He solved the teaspoon inside a month. Strictly, two other people solved it for him, which was the shape, he was coming to understand, of all his best solutions: the trick was never to pull on the world directly, but to arrange matters so that the world, pursuing its own comfort, pushed.

Petunia solved the supply side. The Saturday walk, it emerged, was insupportable—twenty-five minutes of being seen in public with the nephew—and by August she had discovered that the boy could be trusted to go alone, a discovery Harry had administered in careful doses: the first trip with her waiting at the corner, the second with her waiting at home with the kitchen clock running, the third unsupervised, each return punctual to the minute, each stack of books displayed on request, blameless as bread. Of course he may go alone, it’s only the library, Vernon; it keeps him from underfoot and it’s the sort of thing one might mention, lightly, at the Rotary. By September the library run was policy, and the policy was a standing grant of the one resource the house had never before issued him: unsupervised time, in bulk, every week, with a destination so respectable that suspicion itself signed the chit.

Mrs Prentice solved the demand side.

Mrs Prentice ran the branch the way a good stationmaster runs a junction: nothing escaped her, and almost nothing moved her. She was fifty-some, grey-bunned, built like the radiators, and she had watched, across her counter, forty years of children pretending to read above their age to impress mothers who were pretending to care. She had Harry filed within three visits. He knew the moment it happened—he was at the returns desk, and she looked at his stack (the three books: one picture book for the record, The Observer’s Book of Birds for cover, and the first volume of a children’s encyclopedia for actual caloric content), and then she looked at him, one beat too long, and said nothing at all.

On the fifth visit she conducted the test. She did it properly, without warning, which earned her his permanent respect: as she stamped the encyclopedia’s return she said, idly, eyes on the stamp, “Anything worth knowing in there about cuckoos?”

The courtroom convened; the courtroom, by now, could convene, hear argument, and rise inside a second and a half. Underplay, urged the standing policy, the odd-clever-child fortification, two years in the building. But he had watched this woman reunite a lost toddler with its mother using nothing but a biscuit and two sentences, watched her repel a loud man demanding the removal of a book she disliked more than he did, on principle, with the Dewey decimal calm of a woman who had seen off worse; and the shelf had a growing section, started the day of Miss Henmore’s telephone call, titled allies are leverage you don’t have to hold, and the section said that the fortification had a gate for a reason.

“They don’t build nests,” said Harry. “They lay in other birds’ nests, and the chick pushes the real eggs out, and the parents feed it anyway, even when it’s four times their size. The book says it’s remarkable. I think the interesting part is the parents—why can’t they tell? It’s four times their size. I think the parents must have a rule they can’t stop running even when it’s wrong. Lots of things seem to work like that.”

Mrs Prentice stamped the last book. She looked at him for a long moment—a genuinely long one, nearly four seconds; he had time to inventory the whole shape of her attention and find, nowhere in it, the flood-stage fear, nothing of Petunia at the cooker, only something drier and infinitely more useful: a professional, recognising stock.

“Hm,” she said. And then: “The junior tickets are a nonsense for you. You’ll take adult tickets on your aunt’s member­ship—she won’t be using them—and you’ll have the run of that side of the room, and if anyone asks, the books are for your uncle.” A beat. “Your uncle has catholic tastes.”

“Thank you,” said Harry, with great care, because the sentence was carrying more weight than any sentence he had been issued in years.

“Mondays and Thursdays we’re quiet after four,” said Mrs Prentice, already turning to the next borrower, as if the single most consequential administrative act of his life to date were a stationmaster waving a train through a junction, which, to her, it was. “Mind the stepladder. It sticks.”

Six tickets, both sides of the room, a standing timetable, and a cover story, in eleven seconds, unasked. Allies, he said to himself that night, under the heading that was coming to organise everything: the first set of rules says three tickets. The second set of rules is called Mrs Prentice.

What does a mind that lives two hundred days inside each day do with a public library?

It eats it, is what it does—but even eating has an order, and the order he imposed on himself that autumn would still be legible, decades later, in the architecture of everything he built. The temptation was to gorge: stories, dinosaurs, volcanoes, the lurid and lovely children’s shelf of Amazing Facts. He permitted the gorge for exactly three weeks—even the king of the country of one, he noted, must sometimes throw a festival—and then, the first Monday of October, after four, in the quiet, he climbed the sticking stepladder in the adult section, took down the first volume of the shelf-worn Children’s Britannica the branch kept beside the proper one, and began, with the first page of the letter A, the project he privately titled the Survey.

Not to learn everything; he knew better by now. To map everything. To walk, once, at speed, the whole coastline of what humanity knew, noting the harbours, noting above all the places where the map said here be nothing—because a hole in a map was the most informative mark on it, and he had personal reasons, filed on a shelf in his head and boxed as expensive if true, to care about one particular species of hole.

The Survey ran eight months. He took it in strict rotation with the gorging shelf (the festival clause; also, three fat reference books a week would have drawn even Mrs Prentice’s eye, and the first law was still the first law), and what it did to him was not what the books themselves did. Any single volume was a water glass. The Survey was the first time he saw the shape of the sea: that knowledge clumped; that the clumps had unguarded borders where nobody seemed to hold title (the chemistry books and the biology books described the same world and barely spoke); that everything had turned out to be made of smaller things that obeyed stranger, simpler rules, every single time anyone had looked hard enough, atoms under matter, cells under creatures, grammar under speech; and that the books written for children stated as settled what the books across the room confessed, in their final chapters, to be open. He developed a fixed habit that never left him: read the last chapter first. The last chapter was where the authors kept their honesty.

And in the whole eight-month Survey, in nine thousand books, there was no entry—not one line, he checked the indexes of everything plausible, supernatural, see folklore; magic, see conjuring—for warmth that answered need in a blanket on a doorstep. Nothing for photographs that turned their heads. The map of everything had a hole in it, and the hole was shaped exactly like his life, and nobody else’s map seemed to show it at all.

He was five years old, and he had conducted a literature review and found the field unclaimed.

School proper began that September—St Gregory’s Primary, reception class, full days—and school proper brought Dudley, and Dudley brought the gang, and the gang brought the game that was played at every school everywhere by boys shaped like Dudley against boys shaped like Harry. It had no name yet. Dudley would eventually coin one—Harry Hunting, he had a gift for nomenclature if nothing else—but by the time he coined it the game was already dead, strangled in its cot over one patient autumn term, and nobody, least of all the hunters, ever understood that it had been strangled, which was the entire art of the thing.

The courtroom had considered and rejected grander options. Turning the gang on some other child was effective—he modelled it in an afternoon—and went into the small but growing locked drawer in him, the options one does not use, with a note that lengthened each year he lived: the machine is easiest to steer toward the worst things; this is a fact about machines, not a licence. Adult intervention was worse than useless; he had watched it tried on other victims—it fed the game. What killed a game, every game, was the same thing that had nearly killed him in a playpen: boredom. A game survives on reliable fun. Make the fun unreliable, and make the unreliability look like the weather, and the game dies of natural causes with no fingerprints on it anywhere.

So the hunt simply never prospered. The quarry had an unsporting knack of being elsewhere—through doors that had been about to close, in the one corridor with a teacher in it, absorbed blamelessly into the skipping-rope crowd whose churn no pursuit could penetrate. Cornered—he permitted a cornering roughly monthly; total success would itself have been a pattern—the quarry was bafflingly dull: no chase, no tears, no satisfying squeak, just a small boy looking politely past them at something which, when the gang turned to check it (they always turned; he had tested each boy’s turning latency separately and could play the five of them like a bell-rig), was never quite anything. Twice that term, pursuits ended in front of witnesses so inconvenient—the head outside her office; the vicar, improbably, both times—that Piers Polkiss developed a private theory that Potter was unlucky to chase, which Harry, who had assembled the vicar coincidence with a fortnight’s planning apiece, considered the finest review his work had ever received.

By December the gang hunted other things. He spent a further month, in the locked-drawer accounting he forced himself to do honestly, on the other children it might hunt instead, and found the game, deprived of its founding quarry, had lost altitude generally—Dudley’s boredom now discharging mainly into football, at which, Harry noted with the pleasure of a well-fed meteorologist, his cousin was actually, legitimately, not bad.

The school itself he ran on the St Gregory’s protocol, third revision: the odd clever child, reading age politely off the charts, maths carefully two years ahead instead of the truth, questions calibrated, friendships—

Friendships were the file with nothing in it. He was aware of the file. At two hundred to one, a playground conversation was a correspondence chess game in which only he could see the board between moves, and the other children, bless them, were children: he liked them, rather, the way one likes ducklings, and a duckling is not company. The file stayed open. The shelf, which required honesty in both directions, took the following, the night of his sixth birthday, between an anomaly and a run of Dewey numbers: Mrs Prentice asked what I'd been reading, and I told her about map holes for four minutes before the desk bell went, and she said, Hm, bring the cuckoo energy to the holes, and I have been in a good mood for eleven days about it. Query: what is the thing of which this is the small dose?

Interlude: the staff room, St Gregory’s Primary, a wet Tuesday lunchtime in November

“I’m only saying,” said Miss Weald, who had the reception class, “that I’ve been doing this nine years, and the reading cards don’t go up that far, and when I gave him the Year Six comprehension for a lark he finished it and asked—nicely, mind—whether the passage about the lighthouse keeper was meant to contradict itself about the tides, because if it was, it was quite good.”

“Gifted children exist,” said Mr Ollerton, from behind the sports pages. “There’s a register for it.”

“I put him on the register in September.”

“Well then.”

“The mother—the aunt,” Miss Weald corrected herself, and frowned at her tea, because the frown had been waiting all term for somewhere to go, “came in for ten minutes, said they didn’t want any fuss, no assessments, no county people, nothing in writing, thank you, and sat the whole time with her handbag on her knees like I was going to bite her. And the boy.” She stopped.

“The boy what?”

“He manages her,” said Miss Weald. “Four years old—five, now. She started twisting her gloves before she’d even sat down, and he put his hand on the chair next to her—didn’t say anything, just—staged it, like a little equerry, and she sat, and she was better. He does it to me. He does it to all of us. Watch him at home time tomorrow, by the gate, when the mums come: he stands where the traffic sorts itself. I counted. He adjusts four collisions a week out of existence, and nobody’s ever seen him do anything.”

Mr Ollerton lowered the sports pages a careful inch. “Nine years,” he said, “and your considered professional alarm about this child is that he’s polite in a load-bearing way.”

“My considered professional alarm,” said Miss Weald, “is that I’ve taught four hundred children, and every single one of them, by November, I knew. What they wanted, what they feared, which drawer they went in. And this one—I know exactly which drawer he goes in. It’s labelled odd clever little boy, and it’s the right drawer, everything fits, and some mornings I catch him deciding to fit.” She drank her tea. “You’ll say that’s not a thing.”

“It’s not a thing,” said Mr Ollerton, going back to the football. But he did, as it happened, stand by the gate the next afternoon; and he watched; and that evening he told his wife, over the washing-up, that the funny thing was you couldn’t see anything at all, and his wife said then there was nothing to see, and Mr Ollerton dried the plates and did not quite agree.

The haircut happened in February, and the haircut changed everything, and he slept through it.

Not the haircut itself; that he attended in full, with the resignation of a man watching cost-cutting arrive at his own department. Petunia’s war on his hair was a standing seasonal campaign—it grew wrong, it stuck up at the crown exactly the way, though he did not yet know it, a dead man’s had—and that Saturday she lost patience in a single escalation and took the kitchen scissors to the whole estate, cropping him to the skull but for a ragged fringe left, she said, “to hide that horrible scar.” The result, inspected in the dark window of the oven, was genuinely alarming; he filed Monday, school, and the word convict under problems with a forty-eight-hour fuse, shelved the day’s observations, and went to sleep in the smallest office in England.

And woke, at six, in the grey, with the weight on his scalp wrong, and lay perfectly still for one full real minute—a subjective working afternoon—while his hands reported, hair. All of it. To the half-inch, by later measurement against the door frame where he’d marked the cropping: the whole black unruly estate restored, overnight, including—he checked with both hands, twice—the stubborn crown that had never once obeyed a comb in its life.

What he felt first, and he kept it because the shelf required honesty and he never afterwards pretended otherwise, was not wonder. It was outrage. Three years. Three years the anomalies file had sat on its shelf, boxed as expensive if true, starved: one warmth he’d been fifteen months old for, one photograph with a kettle shrieking over it, a handful of maybes—a fall off the garden wall that had hurt less than physics suggested, a jam jar that he was almost sure had been out of reach. Three years of pending, unreliable, do not build on. And now the largest magical event of his life—measurable! repeatable-sounding! his own body the apparatus!—had happened six inches above the finest observation instrument in Little Whinging, while the instrument was switched off. He had slept through his own experiment. The universe had, at last, submitted data, and the recording equipment had been unconscious in its camp bed, dreaming—he had checked the dream log too, it was the first thing he checked—of absolutely nothing relevant.

Downstairs, Petunia dropped the milk bottle. The weather that followed was severe—how did you, what did you, you wicked— cut off, as always, at the drawer’s edge—and he stood in it with his face arranged into frightened bafflement, which required no arranging whatsoever for once, and took his week in the cupboard without protest, and was grateful for the dark and the quiet, because he had work to do and the office door was shut and the universe had finally, finally, blinked first.

The anomalies file came down off the shelf that night and did not go back up. By Sunday it had become the Programme, with its own shelf, its own order, and its first honest casefile, because the hair was different in kind from everything before it: bounded (overnight), targeted (the hair, the whole hair, nothing but the hair), corrective (the universe had not given him something new; it had put something back the way it was supposed to be), and correlated, like the warmth, like the soft landing off the wall, with a need. He had gone to sleep dreading Monday in his skin. Something had audited the dread, identified the cause, and quietly reversed it—while he slept, which was the pattern’s cruellest feature and, the Programme noted in its first formal hypothesis, perhaps its most informative one:

H1. It does not run while I am watching for it. Corollary: it uses the same machinery I watch with—and when that is busy elsewhere (asleep, or flooded), it answers need directly.

Which would mean the power was not out in the world, in blankets and photographs, visiting him.

Which would mean the power was in here. Standing behind the watcher. Waiting, with two hundred times a lifetime’s patience, for the watcher to leave the room.

Harry Potter sat in the dark of the smallest office in England, five years old, or a thousand, depending on how you counted, with the Programme laid out where he kept it and his outrage cooling into something with much longer legs, and began—under a staircase in Surrey, in his head, where everything lived and nothing was ever lost—to draft the experiments.

The watcher, he thought, can be sent out of the room on schedule. I do it every night.

We are going to need a better dream log.
