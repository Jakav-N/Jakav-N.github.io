Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 4

The Man in the Violet Hat

It was a Saturday in June, and the three of them were walking back from the parade of shops, Petunia first, then Dudley eating the end of a sausage roll, then Harry with the bags, when a very small man in a violet top hat stepped out of the queue at the bus stop and bowed.

It was a real bow, from the waist, hat swept off and everything. Nobody in Little Whinging had ever bowed to anybody. Three people in the queue stopped talking to watch him do it.

“Mr Potter,” said the little man, beaming up from somewhere around Harry’s own height, “an honour. A very great honour.”

Then the bus came in between them, and when it pulled away the little man was not at the stop, and was not on the bus either, because Harry had watched every window go by, and the windows went by slowly indeed.

Aunt Petunia took the corner into Magnolia Crescent at a pace that made Dudley jog. She bought Dudley a second ice lolly from the van on the corner, which was against the rules of Saturday, and never said one word about the man all the way home, except once, at the gate, half to herself:

“They oughtn’t to be allowed.”

Harry carried the bags in and put the tins away and said nothing, which was his usual arrangement, and that night he took the whole thing down off the shelf and went over it properly.

The man had known his face. Not guessed—known. He had looked across a Saturday pavement at one boy among the shoppers and recognised him the way you recognise the King on a stamp. And he had said Mr Potter to a six-year-old, and he had said an honour, and he had meant it. The voice goes different when people mean it. Harry had two hundred times as long as anyone else to listen to a voice, and the little man had meant it the way Mrs Prentice meant mind the stepladder.

And Petunia had not been puzzled. That was the part worth the shelf space. A stranger bows to her nephew in the street and vanishes, and her reaction was not how odd but they oughtn’t to be allowed. They. She had a category for the man, with a word for it, and the word made her walk fast.

He went back along his memory for others, and found them at once, because everything he had ever seen was still exactly where he had left it. A woman all in green, two summers ago, on the top deck of a bus going past—she had looked straight at him in the queue below, and waved, delighted, as if she’d spotted an old friend. A man in a long cloak outside the post office last winter, wrong for the weather, wrong for Surrey, who had stared and stared and then shook his own hands together over his head like a footballer. At the time Harry had filed them as the ordinary oddness of adults. Lined up now, all three faced the same direction.

Strangers knew him. Not strangers in general—a particular kind of stranger, dressed off the second set of rules, the kind his aunt knew a word for and wouldn’t say. They knew his name and his face, and when they saw him their faces did the same thing, every one.

It took him a while to name what the faces did, and he was honest with himself when he got it, because it was not a thing anyone’s face had ever done at Number Four.

They were glad to see him. All of them. Glad the way people are glad about something they thought they might never get to see.

He was six years old, and he sat with that for a long time, in the dark, in the smallest office in England, listening to the house tick.

Mrs Figg’s front room smelled of cabbage and cats, and on the third Sunday of July it also smelled of a seed cake that had not gone entirely to plan, and Harry sat on the good end of the settee with a slice of it and a tabby called Mr Tibbles watching him from the armchair opposite.

Mr Tibbles always watched him. The other cats slept, or washed, or went about their business. Mr Tibbles sat like a man at a lecture.

“More milk in that?” said Mrs Figg, from her chair by the window.

“No thank you, Mrs Figg.”

“And school’s going along all right. You’re keeping up.” She said it the way she always said it, not quite a question, her eyes on the knitting. “No trouble. Nothing out of the ordinary.”

There it was. Fourth visit running, the same little gate at the end of the same little path. Nothing out of the ordinary. He had counted, going back: she had asked it, in one dress or another, at every single visit since he was old enough to answer, which is not a thing people do. People ask whether school is all right. Nobody asks a small boy, month after month, in exactly the same place in the conversation, whether anything out of the ordinary has happened, unless they have somewhere to put the answer. Unless it goes in a report.

He had decided, the night of the violet hat, to post something through the gate and see where it went. He had chosen the item with care. True, small, already half-known to the house, harmless to confirm.

“My hair grows fast,” said Harry. “Aunt Petunia cut it right off in February and it was all back by morning. She was annoyed about it.”

The knitting stopped. One stitch, two. Then it went on, and Mrs Figg said, comfortably, “Lovely thick hair like yours, I’m not surprised,” and the conversation turned to Snowy’s bad paw, and that was all.

But the next visit there were chocolate biscuits instead of seed cake, the good kind, the kind from the tin with the picture of Edinburgh on it, and Mrs Figg asked him—third sentence, long before the gate usually came—whether anything else like the hair had ever happened, anything at all, he could always tell her, some children were just like that and it was nothing to worry about and nothing to be ashamed of either.

Harry ate a chocolate biscuit and gave her a dropped jam jar that hadn’t broken, which was true, and years old, and the least of everything. Mrs Figg nodded and said jars were tougher than people thought, and her eyes behind the glasses were kind and sharp at once, and he understood that something had gone up a flagpole somewhere and been read.

So. The channel was real, and it ran through a cabbage-smelling front room two streets from his cupboard, and it had been running his whole life. Somebody received those reports. Somebody had been receiving them since before he could talk.

He looked at Mr Tibbles. Mr Tibbles looked at him.

“I expect you take the notes,” Harry said to the cat, quite quietly, while Mrs Figg was out refilling the pot. “Tell them the biscuits were a good touch.”

Mr Tibbles turned his head slowly away and watched the window, like a professional caught out in nothing.

He walked home the long way, past the recreation ground, thinking it through, and the thought he arrived at surprised him, and he kept it.

He was being watched, and had always been watched, and the watching was gentle. The questions were always about whether he was all right. The warmth on the doorstep, the first night of his life, had been left there by somebody who minded whether a baby was cold. Mrs Figg took ten pence an hour from the Dursleys and gave him the good biscuits out of her own tin. If this was surveillance, it was surveillance by people who were worried about him, and no one at Number Four had been worried about him once in five years, and it turned out that mattered. It mattered a great deal more than he had budgeted for.

Somewhere out there was a world where strangers were glad to see him, and it had posted a sentry two streets away to make sure he was all right.

He decided not to poke it any further. Not because of the risk, although he wrote the risk down honestly. Because you don’t take a thing like that apart while it’s still the only thing you’ve got.

The dream journal started in September, and it was Mrs Prentice’s fault.

He had been reading about sleep, in the quiet after four on a Monday, because the hair had grown back at night and the night was the one part of his life he couldn’t see. The book said, in a footnote, that some people learned to notice they were dreaming while the dream was still going on, and could look around inside it, and even steer. The book called it a curiosity. Harry sat on the sticking stepladder with his heart going quick and thought: an observatory.

If he could stand inside his own sleep with his eyes open, then the night was not closed to him after all. Whatever had fixed his hair had done it while he lay six inches away with the lights off. Next time, he could be standing there watching.

It took him four months to learn, which by his own clocks was a long apprenticeship, and he did it the way the footnote suggested and then some. He kept a stub of pencil and a pad by the camp bed and wrote down every dream he caught, in cipher, by feel, in the dark. He got in the habit of asking himself, hourly, all day, am I dreaming now?—checking the hall clock twice, counting his fingers—until the question wore a groove deep enough to follow him under. The first time it worked he was standing in a dream of the library where all the books were about him, which even at the time he thought was a bit much, and he heard himself think this is a dream, and the whole place steadied around him like water settling, and he stood there inside his own sleeping head, awake, delighted, the first visitor in the first observatory ever built on that particular coast.

What he learned, across that winter, was mostly what every observatory learns first: the sky does not perform on schedule.

Dream-magic turned out to be everywhere and worthless. In dreams he could fly, walk through the cupboard door, turn the ceiling to water. It proved nothing. Dreams were liars; they'd been promising him things all his life. The real thing, the thing that mended hair and caught falling boys, never once showed itself while he stood watch. Either it didn't work the nights he was looking, or it had nothing to do with dreams at all and the hair had been mended by something that didn't need him conscious or unconscious, just quiet.

He logged it all anyway. Negative results, the books said, were still results, and he believed them, and it was even true.

And the observatory earned its keep another way, one he hadn’t planned for and never put in the Programme, though he wrote it in the journal, once, and drew no box around it.

There was an old dream that came back every few months. It had been coming back all his life. Green light, and a woman’s voice, and the voice was saying a word that he had slowly come to understand was his own name. For five years the dream had simply happened to him, the way weather happens.

The first time he came awake inside it—lucid, standing in the green, her voice around him—he had a list ready. Note the light's direction. Note the word's exact shape. Fix it all for the record.

He didn’t take the list out. He stood very still inside the dream, and listened, and stayed as long as it would let him.

Some nights the observatory was for science. Some nights it was for hearing his mother say his name, slowly, at two hundred to one, which was long enough, almost, to pretend she was going to say something after it.

He never told anyone that. There was no one to tell. But he stopped minding, quite so much, when the question-groove pulled him under on an empty night and no dreams came at all. The observatory was there. She was in it sometimes. That was more than he'd had the year before, and he was a great believer in more than the year before.

He asked the question in November, on a Sunday evening, drying up while Petunia washed. He had been waiting six weeks for the right shape of evening: Vernon at the golf club dinner, Dudley asleep in front of the television, the kitchen warm, the two of them working at the sink in the nearest thing the house ever had to peace. She liked how he dried up. He did the glasses properly, by the stems. He had learned it for exactly this reason, two years ago, and the investment had been paying small dividends ever since.

“Aunt Petunia,” he said, to the glass in his hand, “where are my mum and dad buried?”

The washing-up stopped. Water dripped from her gloves into the bowl, once, twice. Three whole seconds, which gave him longer than a school morning to watch the side of her face, and the side of her face was not angry. He had braced for angry. This was the other thing, the thing from the cooker, from the kitchen when he was two. She was looking out the dark window at the garden as if something might be standing in it.

“Up north,” she said finally. “A little village. There’s no visiting. It’s much too far.”

“All right,” said Harry, and dried the glass, and put it away, and handed her the next one, and began to talk about whether the frost would take the bedding plants, which was the subject she liked best in all the world. Her shoulders came down a half-inch at a time. By the forks she was telling him about next door’s dahlias, and he was listening, and also he was not.

A village. Not a road, not a hospital, not a crossroads on the A-something where a car had gone wrong. He had been told the car crash story once, years ago, in a voice that dared him to ask anything further, and the story had sat on its shelf ever since looking exactly like what it was, which was a parcel done up in a hurry.

That night he laid it all out, end to end, everything he had.

A crash, they said. But car crashes do not make little men in violet hats bow in the street six years later. Car crashes do not leave one baby with one neat scar shaped like lightning and nothing else, not a mark. Nobody posts a sentry on the survivor of a road accident. No warmth waits in the blankets of an ordinary orphan. And his aunt did not look at a dark garden like that because of ice on a road, and the word she would not say out loud was not a driving word, and the sister she would not name had been a person in a world where strangers wore cloaks in June and were glad, glad, glad to see the son she’d left behind.

His mother and father had belonged to the second set of rules. Whatever had happened to them had happened in that world, not this one, and it had been important enough that the whole hidden country seemed to know his name on sight. Mr Potter. An honour. People said that to the sons of people who had mattered.

And they watched him. Kindly, patiently, monthly, with biscuits. You watch over something precious, he thought. You also watch over something you are afraid for. Both lines went in the journal, side by side, and he looked at the second one for a long time before he closed the book.

Somewhere behind the world, something had happened, and had not entirely finished happening. He was six, and a careful person, and he could wait to find out. But the shape of his life had turned over that November like a card coming face up: he had spent five years believing he was nobody's, raised on sufferance in a house that counted him like a utility bill. The truth was stranger and better and more dangerous. He was somebody's. Two of them were gone, and a village up north knew where they lay, and he would stand there himself one day and read their names.

The rest of them—the hats, the cloaks, the glad faces, whoever Mrs Figg mailed her knitting-and-biscuits reports to—were out there, two streets and one whole world away, keeping an eye on him until it was time.

He could be patient. He had the time in hand; he always did. Let it come when it came. He would be ready, and he would be worth the watching.

The ice came in January, and the ice gave him the morning he had been waiting five years for.

It had frozen hard in the night, then thawed a little, then frozen again, which is the recipe for the worst kind of path. Harry went out at half seven in the grey to bring the milk in, two bottles, one in each hand, his breath standing in the air around him. The front path at Number Four ran six paving slabs from the step to the gate, and the milk stood at the gate end, and he had got all the way there and halfway back when the fourth slab, which looked like all the other slabs, turned out to be glass.

His feet went out from under him all at once, both together, the complete classic, nothing to be done. He had two bottles and no hands and the back of his head was suddenly pointed at a frozen paving slab, and fright went through him like a struck bell.

And everything opened.

He had lived his whole life slow, but this was something else again. Fear, it turned out, had a gear below all his other gears. The garden went still as a painting. His own breath hung in front of him, a white sculpture. He could see one bottle leaving his right hand, turning, the milk inside it banked up the glass like a little tilted sea, and he had time—that was the thing, he had all the time there has ever been—to be afraid, and to notice being afraid, and to notice what the fear was doing.

It was calling.

He had no better word, then or afterwards. Something in him, down under the thinking, under the watcher, under everything he knew how to be, had put out a call—wordless, shapeless, nothing but no and falling and help—and the call was answered.

The warmth came up through him like the doorstep blanket, like being stood behind by someone enormous and friendly, and he watched it work. That was the gift of the fear-gear: this time, the first time in his life, he was quick enough to watch. It didn’t grab him. Nothing pushed. It was more as if the world had been asked to reconsider, just here, just for a moment, and had agreed. The path shrugged. His fall went softer and longer the way a dream goes, the frozen slab receiving him like a settee cushion, and the bottle—he watched it the whole way—came down standing, rocked once, and was still. A splash of milk the size of a penny landed on the ice beside it. That was all.

Harry lay flat on his back on the path in the grey morning, perfectly unhurt, holding one bottle upright on his chest, and looked at the sky.

Then he started to laugh. He couldn’t help it and didn’t try. It came up out of him in clouds. Five years of census tables and cipher and negative results, four months building an observatory in his own sleep, all of it aimed at catching the thing in the act—and the thing was not hiding in the night, had never been hiding in the night. It had been here all along, down under the floor of him, waiting by its bell. It did not come when he watched for it. It came when he needed it, the moment the need was real, and it had been coming since a doorstep in November before he could speak, and it was not out there at all. It was in here. It had always been in here. The warmth, the hair, the gentle landings—one worker, the whole time, and the worker lived at his own address.

“Hello,” said Harry Potter, out loud, flat on the ice, to the inside of himself and the inside of the world, which were apparently, on mornings like this, the same place.

A blackbird flew over and said nothing. The milk steamed faintly at the neck of the bottle. In the kitchen, far away at one-to-one, his aunt was calling that breakfast did not fetch itself.

He carried the bottles in with great care. He ate his toast looking so ordinary that nobody looked at him twice, and all the while, under the table, in the country of one, there were flags out on every building.

That night the Programme got its shortest entry ever. He wrote it in cipher, by torchlight, under the stairs, and the plain text of it said:

It’s real. It’s kind. It lives here too. It answers when I truly call. So the whole question of my life is just this: learn to truly call on purpose.

He looked at the words a long time, and then, because honesty went both ways, he added the last line, the one the whole year had been teaching him and he had been slow to say:

I am not on my own in here. I don’t think I ever was.
