Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 4

The Week

The week in the cupboard was the best thing Aunt Petunia ever did for him, and she never knew it, and he never told her.

Seven days with the door shut. Meals passed through. No school; she had rung Miss Weald and said a chill in the voice she used for the neighbours. Nothing to do but lie on the camp bed under the slant of the stairs and listen to the house tick. Seven days by the hall clock, and by his own the better part of four years, and he meant to spend all of them on his hair.

He kept no notes. He never had. A page took him an hour to fill at the speed of his hand, and he had never forgotten anything since a doorstep in November, so a notebook would have cost him hours to tell him what he already knew. Everything he had — every strange event since the warm blanket, every rule, every face — sat in his head in order, where he could look along it like a shelf. He looked along it now.

The hair was good work and he kept it. Cropped to the skull on Saturday, whole by Sunday morning, every hair back to the half-inch. Nobody had touched it. He had slept through the only interesting thing that had ever happened to his own body, and he was still annoyed, and the annoyance was useful; it made him careful. By the time the boiler clicked off that first evening he had a year of experiments laid out, a better way of recording his dreams, and a table of every odd event in his life with the hair at the bottom of it, and he lay in the dark feeling the way a department feels when its filing is straight.

Then, with nothing left to do, he asked himself a question he had never once asked in five years, and it was so simple he was ashamed of it before he had finished thinking it.

Why am I hiding?

Not from Petunia. He knew why he hid from her. He had her face at the cooker when he was two, and just like—, and the drawer she kept nailed shut. That was a real fear with a real address, and the rule he had made at two to live with it — be small — had been right. He had never doubted the rule.

He doubted its reach.

Because he had been small everywhere. At nursery, where he had built a persona with a ceiling two years above his age and two errors in ten, and when it failed had built a lower one. At the branch library, where he had taken three books a week for two years before Mrs Prentice gave him six without being asked, because he had never once walked up to the desk and said I have read all of these. At St Gregory’s, where Miss Weald had put him on a register and his aunt had sat with her handbag on her knees and said no fuss, and he had let her. None of those rooms had his mother in a drawer. Miss Henmore had wanted to give him more. Mrs Prentice had been holding a door since he was four. He had made a rule for one frightened woman in one kitchen and had been paying for it in every room in Surrey.

He did the sum. Three years since he could read; six hundred of his own. Three books a week when the whole building was his for the asking. A ceiling two years above his age when the county had people whose whole job was children it could see, and he had made himself invisible to them on purpose. He had rebuilt arithmetic from Dudley’s old textbook under these stairs at four and gone on from there into places he had no names for, and had never shown a page of it to anyone alive.

He had been running the cuckoo’s rule. The parents who go on feeding the thing in the nest because the rule says feed. He had said that himself, at five, at a returns desk, about birds, and been pleased with it.

“Oh,” said Harry Potter, out loud, in the dark, five and a half years old. “Oh, I’ve been stupid.”

It was the first time he had said that about himself and meant it. It went through him clean. It did not hurt at all. It felt like a window going up.

The costs took him about as long as the boiler took to run its evening cycle, which was an afternoon by his own clock, and he thought afterwards that it was the most expensive afternoon of his life — not for what it found but for how long he had waited to spend it.

He went through every room. Nursery: a year of calibrated errors and a kind young woman who would have given him the moon and had been given a boy who read to the hamster. The branch: two years of a teaspoon when there was a sea. School: Miss Weald, who could see him deciding to fit, and had been given a reading age politely off the charts and maths two years ahead instead of the truth. The county, hidden from, by policy, for a year.

He put the costs in a column and added them. He did not like the total and he kept it anyway, where nothing could soften it.

Then he did the other column, because an audit with only costs in it was a confession, not an audit. The rule had got him the cupboard. It had kept him out from under his uncle’s temper. It had kept Petunia’s fear at a temperature he could live in, and that was real, and he would keep that part of the rule for exactly as long as he lived in her house.

Outside the house it had bought him nothing, for three years, at the price of everything, and he had never noticed because he had never once reviewed it. A two-year-old had made it in an emergency, and two-year-olds do not schedule reviews.

He scheduled one. Every rule I have, I made in a hurry. Every year — my year — I will take them all down and ask whether the boy who made them is still here. Mostly he will not be.

The plan took longer, because a plan has other people in it, and other people have to be run forward through every thing you might say to see what they do. He had five years of six people to run, and he ran them, and by the hall clock it took perhaps twenty minutes.

A child of five who walked into a library and announced he had read it would be patted on the head. A child of five who could prove it would frighten people, and he knew from a shopping list what frightened people did. So the question was not whether to stop hiding. It was who could bear the truth, in what order, and how much of it.

Mrs Prentice could bear all of it. She had tested him with a cuckoo and given him the adult shelves without blinking. She had a face that filed things rather than fled them. He would go to her first and he would not go small.

Mrs Bell could bear most of it, if it came through Mrs Prentice. The county could bear a great deal, if someone with letters after their name wrote it down. The newspapers could bear anything and should be given nothing yet. Uncle Vernon could bear exactly as much as made him look good at the Rotary — a lever, not a limit, and he had been pulling it for a year without admitting what it was for.

And Petunia.

He had meant to keep the rule for her whole. Small at home, large everywhere else. But the rule had a hole in it the size of her kitchen: she was going to find out anyway. The school would write. The county would telephone. Vernon would boast. She would hear in her own hall that the boy was being tested by a lady from Kingston, and she would hear it without his having told her, and the not-telling would frighten her far more than the fact.

He had managed her like weather for five years and had never once tried the front door. The sentence he would say to her arrived whole, in the same instant he saw the hole — one sentence, true and short — and he turned it over once to be sure and put it away until Friday.

Then the hair again, because the hair was why he was in here, and the audit had just told him he had been treating it like a hobby.

He had. A table of odd events in his head, worked at between library books, as if it were a crossword. Something in him could regrow hair overnight and warm a blanket on a doorstep and set a jam jar down soft. People in cloaks knew what it was. One of them had bowed to him in the street. One of them lived two streets away and asked him every month whether anything out of the ordinary had happened, and wrote the answer down after. He had been filing all of that under pending for years, and going to school.

“Stupid,” he said again, more gently. He was getting used to it.

So it went first. Not before the library — he could not live without the library — but beside it, every day, with the same hours, not only when a cat was poorly. He did not know how to make it come. His one hypothesis, that it ran when he was not watching, had been made by a boy who had just slept through his own experiment and was annoyed, and he did not trust it. He set out the questions instead. What is it for? When does it come? Who else has it? How do they make it come? And under the last one, after a long time, the plainest answer, the one he had been avoiding for two years:

Ask Mrs Figg.

She knew. She had known since the photograph of a cat turned its head on her mantelpiece. She had a report to make every month and somebody to make it to. At three he had decided not to poke her, and had called that wisdom. It had been the rule in a coat.

The rest of the week he waited.

That was what the week actually taught him, and it was not what he had gone in to learn. Everything — the hair, the question, the costs, the plan, the sentence for the kitchen, the question for the garden — was finished by the second morning. The door would not open until Monday. He lay under the stairs with five days ahead of him and a plan he could not start, and he was not bored.

He had never in his life waited for anything. He had waited; he had done ten centuries of it, in a playpen, at a bus stop, in this cupboard; but it had been the way a stone waits, with nothing on the far side. This had a Monday on the far side of it. There was a woman with a stamp, and a sentence for a kitchen, and a garden with cats in it. He spent the five days on the mathematics, which asked nothing of anyone; on the hair table, which needed rebuilding now that the hypothesis was suspect; and on the seven things he was going to say in seven rooms, composed to the word, because his mouth could manage perhaps a hundred and twenty words in a minute and would need every one of them ready.

On the Thursday, in the dark, he very nearly laughed.

On the seventh day he went through the whole plan once more and it held, the way a good proof holds, with no soft place anywhere he could lean on and feel it give.

Then he said the new rule. Out loud, in a whisper, in the dark. The old one had been passed in silence by a boy of two. He wanted this one to have been said once in that house, in his own voice, so that the house had heard it even if nobody in it had.

“Be small in one room. Everywhere else, be the size you are, and let them deal with it. That’s their job. Mine is to stop making it hard.”

He listened to it with the air round it, to see if it still sounded true. It did.

He slept the whole night through and did not dream. It was, he would say later, the best week of his life, and he had said that about several, and this time he meant it.

“Mrs Prentice,” he said, at four on the Monday, with the stepladder between them, “I need to tell you some things. All at once, I think. I’ve been not telling you them for two years and it was a mistake and I’d like to stop.”

She was shelving. She did not stop, but she slowed, and he had learned in two years that the slowing was the thing to watch.

“Go on, then.”

He had not rehearsed it. Rehearsing was for being small well, and he was done with that. He told her. That he had read every book in the building, both sides of the room, including the ones on the top shelf behind the stepladder, and could tell her what was in any of them. That the Survey she knew about had been a map, and he had walked the map for a year looking for its edges and had found some. That he had rebuilt arithmetic from his cousin’s old textbook under the stairs at four and gone on, and some of where he had gone was in the fat books on the adult side with German names, and some of it he had not found in any book and did not know whether that meant it was new or that the branch did not have the book. That he had been pretending at school for two years by a rule he had made at two, and had spent last week in a cupboard working out the rule was wrong, and was not going to pretend any more anywhere except at home, where there was a reason.

That he needed a bigger library. Not the county’s reserve stock. A university.

Nine minutes at the speed of his mouth. The longest he had ever spoken to anyone. Partway through she had stopped shelving and sat down on the second step of the ladder with her hands in her cardigan pockets, listening the way she listened to nothing else in that building.

When he finished she said nothing for a while.

“Well,” said Mrs Prentice. “What took you so long?”

He had expected a dozen things and not that. He found he was smiling.

“I was two,” he said. “When I made the rule.”

“Yes. I did gather it was something of that order.” She got up off the step and dusted her skirt, which did not need it. “I knew about the top shelf, dear. I knew in October. I moved the Hilbert to the bottom one so you’d stop risking your neck for it, and you noticed, and you never said, and I never said, and I have been waiting since October for you to walk up to this desk and say.” She went to the desk. “I shall ring Mrs Bell. I know her from the churchyard committee. She has a drawer with your name on it that she has never liked and I am going to tell her she may open it.” She wrote on a date-due slip. “And I shall ring somebody else.”

“Who?”

“My husband’s brother lectures at the university in Egham. Chemistry. He is a tiresome man and I have not spoken to him since the funeral, and I am going to telephone him tonight and tell him there is a child of five in the Little Whinging branch who has read Hilbert off the top shelf, and ask him what the university does about that, and he will say nothing, Edith, universities do not take children, and I shall say then find me the person who decides that, and he will, because he has always been afraid of me.” She squared the slip. “It may take a fortnight.”

Harry stood by the stepladder with his hands at his sides and found, for the second time in his life, that he had nothing ready.

“Thank you,” he said. “For the door. For October.”

“Hm,” said Mrs Prentice. “Go and do the crossword. There’s one in the bottom corner I can’t get and I shall be very annoyed if you can.”

The lady from the county came on the second Thursday in March with a big folder, and he did it straight, and she lasted forty minutes before she had to go and stand in the corridor.

Her name was Dr Prosser. She had glasses on a chain and a cardigan the colour of oatmeal and a stopwatch she worked with her thumb under the table where children were not supposed to notice it. He noticed it and did not pretend not to, and that was the first thing she wrote down.

He was gentle with her. He answered at the speed of his mouth, not his mind. He did not finish anything before she had finished asking it. When the strings of numbers went past the end of her sheet he said them back carefully, as if they were a shopping list. But he did not stop. Not at fourteen, where the folder stopped, nor at twenty, nor at thirty, where Dr Prosser stopped reading them out and looked at him across the little table for a long moment and said, “Right,” and squared the cards, and said “Right” again, and said, “Would you excuse me one moment,” and went and stood in the corridor, and he sat with the orange squash and listened to her breathe out there, slow and even, like a woman doing an exercise from a leaflet.

She came back and did the rest with great steadiness. She was very good at her job. She had simply not known there was a far side to it. At the end she closed the folder and took the chain glasses off and looked at him the way Mrs Prentice had looked at him over a cuckoo: a professional, given cause.

“When I was training,” she said, “a long time ago, there was a girl in one of the studies. Up in the valleys. Read the back of the medicine packets in the surgery for something to do, and came out ordinary on every test we gave her, test after test, until one of us noticed she was ordinary the way a swan is clumsy. On land, you follow me. Because it suited her to be, where she lived.” She clipped the rubber band back round her second pile. “I have wondered on and off for thirty years what became of her. I rather think she’s still in the valleys.”

Harry looked at the box of blocks.

“I was being a swan,” he said. “Until last month. On purpose. It was a rule I had, and it was wrong, and I’ve stopped.” He looked up. “Dr Prosser, I don’t know what you can write. I know the forms stop. But I’d like you to write the true thing and not the kind one, because the kind one is what I’ve been getting for two years and it’s cost me.”

Dr Prosser was quiet a moment. Then she opened the folder to the last page, where there was a box for Recommendations, and he watched her, upside down across the table, cross out something she had already written — very able; enrichment — and write underneath it, in a hand that did not hurry:

Ceiling not reached. In my professional judgement not reachable with any instrument available to this authority. Recommend immediate referral to university-level provision, with whatever precedent has to be invented to do it. I will put my name to that.

She signed it. She turned it round so he could see.

“That,” said Dr Prosser, “is the true thing. Heaven help us all.” And at the door, holding it open for him, in the voice people use for remarks about the weather: “It gets easier to find them, you know. The swans. Once they decide to be found.”

He said the sentence to Petunia on the Friday, at the sink, after Vernon had read the letter from the school aloud and taken it into the hall to ring Marge.

He had watched the side of her face while Vernon read — exceptional, the county’s most senior educational psychologist, the university at Egham has expressed an interest — and the white had come up as he had known it would, and her hand had gone towards her collarbone, and he had put down the tea towel and said quietly, under Vernon’s voice in the hall:

“It’s only school, Aunt Petunia. Being good at school isn’t the other thing. I promise you it isn’t. I’ll never let it be, in this house.”

She did not answer. Her hand came down. She stood with it flat on the letter and read it again, and what was in her face when she finished was still fear, but under the fear was something that had been there for five years and that he had never once given her a reason to show.

She was tired. She had been frightened for five years of a thing she could not say, in a house where nobody would have let her say it, and here was the boy at the sink telling her in one sentence that he knew what it was and would keep it out of her kitchen.

“Well,” said Petunia, to the letter. And then, to the hall, in the voice for the neighbours: “Vernon. Tell Marge the university.”

Vernon told Marge the university. Harry picked up the tea towel and finished the plate. Forty seconds, which for him was a working day, and he spent the rest of the washing-up quietly amazed at how little it had cost. Five years of being a convenient nothing to spare one woman’s fear, and when he finally told the fear the truth, it smoothed a letter flat and asked Vernon to say university down the telephone.

The front door. Even with her. The whole time.

He asked Mrs Figg on the last Saturday in March, in the garden, with the cats, because he had saved her for last on purpose, the way you save the thing you most want.

“Mrs Figg. What are you?”

She was deadheading the geraniums. She did not stop, but she slowed, and her shoulders settled the way Petunia’s had at the sink — a woman putting down something she had carried a long way.

“Well,” she said. “There it is.” She snipped a stem and dropped it in the trug. “I wondered when. I had money on next year. Tibbles said this spring.”

“I know there’s a second set of rules,” said Harry. “I’ve known since I was three. I know the man who bowed to me runs on it, and the photograph on your mantelpiece that moved. I know my parents were part of it and didn’t die in a car. I know you watch me for someone, and ask me every month whether anything out of the ordinary has happened, and write it down after. I know my hair grew back in a night, and something warmed my blanket on the doorstep when I was a baby, and whatever does that isn’t in any of nine thousand books at the branch, because I looked.” He stopped. “I don’t know the word. I’ve never had the word. I’ve spent two years not asking you for it because I thought asking would make you choose between me and whoever you write to, and I’ve just spent a week in a cupboard working out that not asking has cost me more than anything in my life. So I’m asking.”

Mrs Figg put the secateurs down on the wall, slowly. She sat on the garden bench and patted the slats beside her, and he sat, and Mr Tibbles came down off the wall and sat at their feet with his lecture face on, as if the examiner had finally turned up.

“I can’t tell you,” she said. “Not today. Not because I don’t want to — Harry, I’ve wanted to since the cat with the markings, and you were three. Because it isn’t mine to tell. There’s a man I write to, and he’s a good man, and he asked me five years ago to watch and report and not interfere, and telling a child what he is counts as interfering, and I’ve kept to it. I’ve told him he was wrong to my face once already and he listened and did it anyway.” She looked at her hands. “But I’ll write to him tonight. I’ll tell him you asked. I’ll tell him how you asked — the two sets of rules and the nine thousand books, all of it — and I’ll tell him that if he says no I shall think a great deal less of him, in those words.”

“How long?”

“A fortnight. He’s a busy man. Sometimes less.”

He was not disappointed. That surprised him, and he looked at the not-being-disappointed to see what it was made of, and it was made of something he had never had.

She had said yes to being asked. She had not given him the word, but she had sat down beside him and told him plainly that there was a word, and a man, and a letter going tonight, and that she was on his side and would say so in writing. For five years he had been the only person in the world who knew there was a question. Now there were two of them, and one had a stamp.

“Thank you,” he said.

“Oh, get on,” said Arabella Figg, and picked up the secateurs, and her voice had gone odd.

The reply came in eleven days. She told him at the gate on the Wednesday, in passing, with Tibbles under her arm: “Saturday, dear. Come at two. Bring an appetite; I’ve opened the good tin.”

He came at two. The good tin was on the table — chocolate biscuits, the Edinburgh one, the one he had seen on the top shelf for three years and never been offered from — and she poured out with her left hand as she always did, and sat, and looked at him across the little table.

“He said yes. He said—” she took a folded paper from her apron pocket and read from it in a different voice “—if he asks you what you are, tell him the truth. I suspect he already knows. I suspect that is the least of what he knows.” She folded it away. “He had the right of that, I think.”

“What’s the word?”

“The word is magic, dear. Just that. You’ll think it’s a silly word. Everybody does, the first time. It isn’t.”

He had expected to feel something and he did, but not what he had planned for. He had known the thing for years; a word was only a label on a drawer. What he felt, at a table in Wisteria Walk with a biscuit he had not touched, was smaller and older than that. It was company. Somebody had said it to him out loud, as a plain fact, the way you tell a child the name of a bird.

“Tell me,” he said. “Please. Everything you’re allowed.”

She told him. Not everything; he could feel the gates she kept shut and he left them shut, because she kept them with the face of a woman under orders she thought were right. But a great deal. That there were people who could do what had been done to his hair — thousands of them, a whole hidden world with its own streets and shops and newspapers, living in the same country behind a kind of curtain, which was why nobody at Number Four had ever met one except by accident. That they were called witches and wizards and the words were not insults where they came from. That she was what they called a squib, born to a family of them — two brothers who could, and she who couldn’t — and so she lived on this side and kept cats and did what she could, and what she could, for five years, had been to watch over one small boy for a man who had asked her to.

That there was a school, in Scotland, where children like him went at eleven to learn to do properly what his hair had done by itself. That a letter would come, on his eleventh birthday, because it always came on the birthday. That his mother and father had both gone to that school and had been very good — “your mum especially, dear; everyone said” — and that what had happened to them had happened in that world and not this one, and was not hers to tell, and he would learn it, she promised, soon enough.

“Eleven,” said Harry.

“The thirty-first of July, 1991. I’ve had it in my diary five years.”

Five years and four months. A world with schools and streets and newspapers was going to send for him, a world that had sent a man in a violet hat to bow to him and a kind woman to watch him, and had taken his parents in some way he was not yet allowed to know. Five years and four months by the hall clock. By his own, a thousand years and more. And nobody on the far side of the curtain, by the sound of it, had the least idea that the boy on this side had just spent a week in a cupboard deciding to stop hiding, and had rebuilt arithmetic at four, and was about to be given the run of a university.

He had wasted six hundred years being small. He was not going to waste one more.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “Thank you. For all of it. For writing to him.”

“Eat your biscuit,” said Arabella Figg, and turned to the window so he would not see her face, which he had already seen, and would keep.

That night the plan got its last line, and the line was a date: 31 July 1991.

Under it, what he had and what he meant to do with it. Five years and four months. A university, soon, if Mrs Prentice’s brother-in-law was as frightened of her as she said; everything in it, as fast as his eyes would go, every subject to the edge and past it where he could. The hair, and whatever had grown it, every day, until he knew what it was for and when it came and how the cloaked people made it come. Mrs Figg, asked everything she was allowed to say, and the rest worked out from the shape of what she wasn’t.

Be the size he was. Let them deal with it.

Then he did a thing he had never done, and it was not for remembering.

He took a square of paper from Dudley’s old exercise book and a pencil and wrote the date on it, alone, large, in the round careful hand that was the only hand he had. It took most of a minute, which was three hours of his own. He pinned it to the inside of the cupboard door with a drawing pin he had been saving, at the height of his eyes when he sat up in bed, which was also the height of Petunia’s eyes when she opened the door in the morning with the plate.

She would see it. She would know what it was; she had known that date since before he could read. He had said one sentence to her this week, across a kitchen table, and this was the second, and it did not need her to answer.

He lay down in the smallest office in England, five years and eight months old by the hall clock and well over a thousand by his own, and for the first time since a doorstep in November he was not waiting for nothing.

He had a date. He had a thousand years. He was going to go and get ready.
