Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 5

The Lady from the County

The lady from the county had a flat box of wooden blocks, red on some faces and white on others, and she tipped them out on the little table between them and said, “Now then. I’m going to show you a picture, and you make the same pattern out of the blocks. Some of them are quite hard. Nobody gets them all, so don’t you worry about that.”

The room was the one off the school office where they kept the sand trays, and it smelled of poster paint. Harry had been pulled out of handwriting for this with no warning at all, which had told him most of what he needed to know before he was through the door. No warning meant the school hadn’t wanted the Dursleys forewarned. Which meant the school had met the Dursleys.

“All right,” he said.

Her name was Dr Prosser. She was somewhere past fifty, with glasses on a chain and a cardigan the colour of oatmeal, and she had a stopwatch she worked with her thumb under the table where children weren’t supposed to notice it. He noticed it. He noticed it the way you’d notice a lighthouse.

He had a policy for this, worked out years ago and kept oiled. Two years ahead, no more. Miss a few on purpose. Be likeable, be ordinary, be filed and forgotten.

The trouble with the policy was Dr Prosser.

She was good. She didn’t gush at him like a teacher and she didn’t talk to him like a dog that might bite, which were the two usual settings for adults in that school. When he got a pattern right she just said “mm” and wrote nothing at all, and when he fumbled one — deliberately, two blocks transposed, a nice honest-looking seven-year-old error — she looked at him for exactly one second longer than she needed to, and then said “mm” in precisely the same voice, and turned the card.

She knows, he thought, halfway through the blocks. She doesn’t know what she knows. But she can smell it.

The patterns went up and up. Somewhere past where the cards were supposed to stop for his age she kept going, without comment, into a second pile she had brought in a rubber band. The stopwatch thumb kept moving. And here was the thing he hadn’t planned for, the thing that had no line in the policy at all: it was the first test anyone had ever given him that had a top end somewhere above his head, and it was fun. The cards got strange. The cards got genuinely strange, patterns with little traps in their corners, and the fumbles he owed the policy began to cost him something to make.

Then she put the blocks away and did remembering-numbers instead. She read out digits in a flat voice and he said them back, and then she had him say them backwards, which is harder, and the strings got longer, and he was enjoying himself, and that is his whole excuse for what happened.

“Nine,” said Dr Prosser. “Four. Seven. Seven. Two. Eight. One. Five. Nine. Three. Six. Two. Four. Eight. Backwards, please.”

He said it backwards. All fourteen. He heard himself do it, calm as a clock, and he saw her thumb stop on the stopwatch, and there was a silence in the little room like the silence after a plate breaks.

Fourteen digits backwards is not two years ahead. Fourteen digits backwards is not any years ahead. He knew the tables in her folder as well as she did by now, upside down across the little desk, and there was no row on them for what he had just done.

“I’ve forgotten the next bit,” said Harry, into the silence.

“Of course you have,” said Dr Prosser.

She didn’t write anything. She squared the cards on the table, taking her time about it, and for a moment she looked less like a lady from the county and more like somebody’s grandmother deciding whether to say a thing at a funeral.

“When I was training,” she said, “a long time ago, there was a girl in one of the studies. Up in the valleys, this was. Little scrap of a thing, read the back of the medicine packets in the surgery for something to do. And every test we gave her, she came out ordinary. Dead ordinary, every time, test after test, until one of us noticed she was ordinary the way a swan is clumsy. On land, you follow me. Because it suited her to be, where she lived.” She clipped the rubber band back around her second pile. “Her teachers never knew. I’ve wondered on and off for thirty years what became of her.”

Harry looked at the blocks box and said nothing. There was nothing in the policy for this either.

“Now,” said Dr Prosser, brisk again, glasses up, “I think I shall write that you’re a very able boy, which is true, and that you’re best kept with your own age for the social side, which is also true, and that the school ought to give you proper library time and harder work instead of moving you up, because moving children up mostly ends in long trousers and misery. How does that sound?”

It sounded like everything he had come into the room intending to engineer, handed to him free, by a woman he had failed to fool in the slightest.

“That sounds fine,” said Harry. “Thank you.”

“Mm,” said Dr Prosser.

At the door, holding it open for him, she said one more thing, not looking at him particularly, in the voice people use for remarks about the weather.

“It gets easier to find them, you know. The swans. Whatever water it is they’re waiting for.” And then, “Off you go, break’s nearly done,” and the chain glasses came off, and she was the county again, and he never saw her twice.

He thought about her, on and off, for years. He never did decide how much she’d known. There wasn’t any way to decide; that was the lesson, in the end, and he was glad to have it early. Some people can read you a little, and like you anyway, and fold their hand on purpose, and you will simply never know. It put a floor under how he thought about adults that the Dursleys had spent five years taking away.

The letter from the school came on a Friday and sat on the hall table until Vernon had eaten, because letters about Harry were opened on a full stomach as a matter of policy.

“Enrichment,” said Uncle Vernon, as though reading the ingredients off something foreign. “Extension materials. Supervised library afternoons, Tuesdays and Thursdays.” He turned the page over to see if the cost was hiding on the back. “Who pays for all this, that’s what they never say.”

“The library’s free, dear,” said Petunia, from the sink.

“Nothing’s free.”

But there was no figure anywhere on the letter, and no county people coming to the house, and no moving him up into Dudley’s year — Petunia had gone very still over the washing-up at that thought, the two of them in one classroom, the comparing that would go on at the school gates — and slowly, over the course of the weekend, the arrangement revealed itself to Uncle Vernon as his own idea. By Sunday lunch he was explaining it to Aunt Marge down the telephone. Keep the boy with his own sort, feet on the ground, none of this hothouse nonsense, but the school does what it can for him, naturally. We insist on that. Marge’s answer crackled on for some time, and Vernon said, well, quite, exactly, bad blood will out but you can keep it busy.

Harry dried the glasses, by the stems, and kept his face down over the tea towel.

Tuesdays and Thursdays, in school hours, by order of the county, with the school’s blessing and Vernon boasting about it down the phone. He hadn’t had to arrange a single piece of it. He’d walked into a small room smelling of poster paint, made the worst blunder of his public life, and come out with more than his best plan had ever aimed at.

He put it in the notebook that night, and he was honest about it, because the notebook was no use otherwise: Fourteen backwards. Never again. But— and then he sat with the pencil for a while, listening to the rain get going on the little slant of roof above the stairs, working out what the but actually was. It took him a long time, even his kind of long time.

But it went to a person who was kind, and nothing bad happened, and a swan story came back. Error noted. Not all error.

Mr Tibbles stopped doing the stairs that autumn.

Harry noticed it before Mrs Figg said anything, because he had been watching that cat with professional attention for three years. Tibbles had a spot on the landing window sill, first sun in the morning, and all through September the spot stayed empty, and the old cat lay instead on the folded blanket by the kitchen radiator and watched the doorway as if the stairs had personally let him down.

“He’s nine, is Tibbles,” said Mrs Figg, on a grey Saturday, setting down the tea. “Ten in March. It’s his hips, the vet says. There’s a tablet, but he won’t have it in sardines, he won’t have it in butter, he knows it’s there, don’t you, you old fool.” She said it to the cat, fondly, and the cat ignored her with the whole of his back. “Old cats get stiff. There’s nothing for it really. You just keep them warm and don’t move the furniture.”

She went out to see to the kettle. The rain ticked on the window. Harry sat down on the floor by the radiator, which he was allowed to do, and Mr Tibbles looked at him with his lecture-hall face, and Harry looked back, and what he felt, watching the old cat decide whether getting comfortable again was worth the trouble of moving, was not curiosity.

He’d had three years of this cat. Three years of being watched by him from the armchair, politely, like a colleague. Tibbles had sat on his feet once, the whole visit, the winter Harry was five, on the day — he worked this out much later — that the hair report would have gone wherever reports went. Cats know a bad week when they see one. Whatever this household was for, whoever got told what, the cat had been company, real company, on afternoons when company was thin, and now he was old in his hips and wouldn’t eat the tablet, and there was nothing for it really.

Harry put his hand on the cat’s side, in the deep fur over his ribs, which was permitted on quiet days. He wasn’t trying anything. He’d learned better than trying months ago — the thing in him did not come when you wanted it, wanting was the wrong doorway entirely, he had wanted plenty and it had never once come for wanting. He was just sitting with an old cat in the rain, sorry, properly sorry, the way you’re sorry about a thing you can’t fix.

And it came.

Quietly, this time. No fear, no falling, none of the bell-struck hugeness of the ice. It rose up through him the way the warmth had risen through the doorstep blankets, patient and friendly and completely unhurried, down his arm and out through his palm into the old cat’s side as if it had been waiting years for somebody to ask properly. He felt it go looking. That is the only way he could ever write it afterwards: it went looking, the way Mrs Prentice went looking along a shelf, hand out, sure of the general area. It found the hips. Something there was the wrong shape of warm, and it made it the right shape, gently, taking its time, and then it sat back, so to speak, and was done, and the rain went on ticking.

Mr Tibbles raised his head. He looked at his own back legs for a moment with an expression of deep suspicion. Then he stood up, stretched himself the whole length of the blanket, back feet trembling with the luxury of it, stepped off, crossed the kitchen, and went up the stairs like a kitten, straight to the landing sill, where there was no sun at all because it was raining, and sat in the grey light looking extremely pleased with himself about nothing.

Mrs Figg came in with the pot and stopped.

The kitchen was very quiet. Harry stayed where he was, on the floor by the radiator, hand still warm, heart going, face arranged — he had to build the arrangement by hand, piece by piece, and for once he had barely time enough to do it — into a boy looking at a cat on a landing.

“He just got up,” said Harry.

Mrs Figg looked at the cat for a long time. Then she looked at Harry, over the top of her glasses, and there passed over her face something he had no file for at all: surprise, and arithmetic, and underneath the two of them, unmistakably, like the radiator under the blanket, gladness.

“So he did,” she said. “Well. There’s hope for us all.”

She poured the tea. Her hand had a small shake in it that hadn’t been there before and was gone by the second cup. They talked about Snowy’s ear. On the landing, all afternoon, an old cat sat in the rain-light at the top of stairs he owned again, and if either of them looked at him rather more often than a cat usually warrants, neither of them said so.

He took the long way home again, past the recreation ground, in the last of the rain.

It answered caring. That was the finding, and he turned it over all the way past the swings and found it held. Not wanting — he had wanted, hard, carefully, dozens of times since January, standing over cold tea and wilting cress experiments, and the thing in him had stayed in like a cat in weather. Not fear only, either, or it would never have come today, because nothing today was frightening. He had simply, truly minded about something that wasn’t him, with his hand already out, and it had come through him like the post.

Years afterwards, in front of people whose faces he would have found very hard to imagine from the recreation ground in Little Whinging, he would say that everything he ever built was downstream of a cat’s hips, and the people would laugh, and he would not exactly be joking.

That night the notebook got a long entry, and the last line of it was short: It isn’t a dog, to come when it’s whistled. It’s more like a colleague. The work has to be real.

The money was Mrs Prentice’s idea, is the truth of it, although she maintained for years that it was his.

What happened was this. The library kept the newspapers on wooden spindles, and on the Saturday ones there was a prize crossword, a proper one, cryptic, twenty pounds to the first correct entry drawn. Harry had been doing it in his head since the summer — you couldn’t write on the library copy, so he kept the grid upstairs, so to speak, and filled it in walking home. It was good exercise and better fun. Cryptic clues were the first things he’d met outside Dr Prosser’s second pile that were built on purpose to fight back. Whoever set them was playing fair and playing to lose, both at once, like a parent at draughts, and Harry had developed, over the autumn, real affection for a person of whom he knew nothing at all except that they were called Custos and lived, presumably, in London.

One Tuesday afternoon — a school library afternoon, Tuesdays and Thursdays, by order of the county — Mrs Prentice came past the reading table, where the Saturday paper was still on its spindle from the weekend, and found him looking at the crossword with his hands in his lap.

She stopped. She looked at the crossword, which was blank, and at him, and at the clock, in the way she had, as if the three of them had been introduced before.

“How long does it take you,” she said. Not a question so much as an opening bid.

“The top half’s all right,” said Harry. “There’s one in the bottom corner I’ve been stuck on since Saturday. Seven letters. Sweet course the French interrupt in audible disorder.”

“Dessert,” said Mrs Prentice.

“That’s six.”

“Desserts, then.”

“It’s not desserts either,” said Harry, “but it wants to be, that’s the trap in it,” and the two of them looked at the grid together in a silence that had the quality, new to him and never afterwards forgotten, of two people being stuck in the same place at the same time on purpose.

They got it on the Thursday. Strictly she got the first letter and he got the rest, and there was a short argument about the apportioning that both of them enjoyed more than the clue. And when it was in, and the grid stood finished in his head and in her pencil on a date-due slip, Mrs Prentice sat back and looked at him over her glasses and said the sentence that paid for most of the next four years.

“You know they give twenty pounds for this.”

“I haven’t got a stamp,” said Harry. “Or an envelope. And they don’t pay out to people who are seven.”

“No,” said Mrs Prentice. “They pay out to retired library staff, I should think, week in and week out.” She took off her glasses, polished them on her cardigan, and put them back, which he had learned by then was her way of signing things. “I’ll stand the stamps. If anything ever comes of it, the stamps come off the top, and I’ll want to see your working. All of it. That’s the fee.”

“Why?” said Harry. It came out before the policy could sit on it. He meant: why would you, what’s in it for you, where’s the catch, every drying-up and found-key and year of boring had been priced, nothing in Little Whinging came free, and here was the second adult in a month folding a winning hand in his favour for no visible stake.

Mrs Prentice considered the question with the seriousness it hadn’t deserved.

“Forty years,” she said, “I’ve stamped books in and stamped books out, and watched what people carry off, and it’s wallpaper and slimming, mostly, dear, I don’t judge, it’s their library too. And once in a long while somebody small comes through the door eating the place alive, and you think, ah. There you are. Somebody’s got to hold the door, haven’t they.” She stood up and squared the date-due slip on the table. “My husband used to do the crossword,” she added, in a completely different voice, a filing-things voice, and went back to the returns desk, and that was the whole of what he ever learned about Mr Prentice.

The first win came in March, the fourth entry they sent. A postal order for twenty pounds, made out to E. Prentice, arrived at the library in a long envelope with the newspaper’s name on it, and she showed it to him at the quiet end of a Thursday, flat on the desk between them, like a fish they’d caught.

Minus the stamps, and entered in pencil in a system of her own devising on the backs of old catalogue cards, it came to nineteen pounds and twelve pence, held in trust in a toffee tin she kept under the returns desk, for purposes unstated, by agreement unsigned, between a widowed librarian and a seven-year-old boy who, between them, over the years that followed, would send Custos of London some four hundred correct solutions and never once meet him.

Nineteen pounds and twelve pence. He walked home past the newsagent’s in the blue dusk with his book bag on his shoulder, and he didn’t gloat, particularly. It wasn’t the money. He couldn’t have spent the money, anyway, not without questions he had no answers for. It was that the world had turned out to contain doors held open, swans noticed and let swim, cats mended, colleagues — upstairs ones and downstairs ones, inside ones and library ones — and that none of it, not one piece of it all winter, had needed to be engineered.

It was the first year of his life that had simply come to meet him. He was seven. He noted, scrupulously, in the private alphabet, in the smallest office in England, that the sample was small.

But it was a start.
