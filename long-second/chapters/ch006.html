Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 6

Real Work

He did the cats first, one a Saturday, through the whole of July.

Not because they needed it most. Because they were there, and because the magic — he had the word now, and used it, carefully, the way you use a new tool — answered what was wrong, and old cats are nothing if not a catalogue of small wrongs. Snowy’s ear, torn in a fight the spring before and healed crooked. Mr Paws’s eye, which wept. Tufty’s limp, which Mrs Figg said was from a fall off the shed in 1983 and which Harry, with his hand in the fur over the haunch and his mind on nothing but sorry, felt go looking and find a place where a bone had set a quarter-inch wrong and been aching about it for three years.

He did them on the kitchen floor with Mrs Figg in the room every time. She never said anything. He never said anything. The biscuits improved each Saturday until by August he was eating things from Fortnum’s off a plate with a gold rim, and the two of them had still not discussed it once.

Then the frost came early, in September, and took the roses at Number Six, and he stood at the fence on the way to the station with Mrs Figg waiting at the corner and minded about one in particular until it was true, and it came back black and then green over a fortnight, from the bottom up, in a way roses don’t. Then Mrs Prentice’s teapot, with the crack down one side that had been there since the Silver Jubilee, which he held one Thursday to help with the drying and let himself truly mind that it leaked on her, and when he handed it back the crack was a line of nothing. She turned it to the light. She said “Hm,” and went on drying, and the next Thursday the teapot was in use and no word was spoken.

The door opened easier every time. That was what the summer taught him. Not wider — the things stayed small, an ear, a rose, a crack — but easier, like a hinge working loose, and once or twice towards September he caught himself able to find the caring on purpose: to look at a thing and choose to mind about it so truly that the choosing and the minding were the same, and the warmth came up to meet it.

Still nothing for wanting. He had tested that honestly, four times, and nothing had come.

And nothing for himself. He tried that too, once, properly, on his own cold feet one night in the cupboard with the purpose built as true as he knew how — and the magic declined, politely, the way Mrs Prentice declined to lend the reference section, as if to say that it would be delighted to help with the roses, and the cats, and the teapot, but that this was simply not what it was for.

He kept that under the heading he used for the facts he liked least.

The door faces outward.

The first thing he ever did on purpose that wasn’t mending was Mrs Figg’s tea, in the café at Egham, on the fourth of October, and it took him a month to build.

It had been bothering him since the roses. Everything he could do, he could do because something was wrong — a bone, a crack, a frost — and the wrongness gave him the handle. But the thing that had warmed his blanket on the doorstep had not been mending anything. He had not been broken. He had been cold, and somebody had minded, and the minding had come down to him through cloth. If it could do that — if it could answer a need that was not a wound — then wrongness was not the handle. It was only the easiest one.

So he changed the question. He stopped asking what was wrong and started asking what was for.

And it turned out — this was the month — that purpose was not something a thing had or didn’t have. It was something you could build. He built the warming of Mrs Figg’s tea the way he had built the plan in the cupboard: honestly, in pieces, over weeks, on the four-floor stairs of the library with his hands in his pockets. She sits in that café from nine till five for me. She has a pot at nine and it is cold by half past and she drinks it cold until eleven because she will not ask for another; she told me once that asking for a second pot was what people with no manners did. If it were warm, the sitting would be a little better. She has given me four hundred and six books’ worth of sitting since May. He did not know whether that would be enough. He only needed to mind about it so truly that the minding and the wanting were the same — and on the fourth of October, coming down to the café at eleven for the biscuit she always had waiting, he found that he did.

He put his hand on the side of the pot, as if steadying himself against the table.

It came. Slowly, like the roses, but it came: the warmth rising up through him and down his arm and into the china, and he felt it find the tea, and find the cold in the tea, and go about undoing the cold without any hurry at all, and then he took his hand away and sat down and took his biscuit.

Mrs Figg poured. She looked at the steam. She looked at Harry.

“That pot,” she said, “was stone cold at a quarter to.”

“Yes.”

“You did that.”

“Yes. I’ve been trying to for a month.” He ate the biscuit. “It doesn’t only do broken things, Mrs Figg. It does needed things. I just have to find out what the need is and mind about it properly. The broken ones are only easier because they tell you.”

Mrs Figg sat with the pot in her hand for a long moment, in the university café, with the students going past with their trays, and then she poured herself a cup, and drank, and put it down.

“I have known a great many witches and wizards, dear. My brothers. Their friends. The man I write to. I have watched them do magic since I was in a pram.” She looked at the steam. “I have never once seen any of them do it like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like you’re asking it a favour.” She picked the cup up again. “They tell it. With a stick and a word. You — sit on the floor and feel sorry for a cat.” She drank. “I shall have to write that down. I don’t know how I shall put it.”

“Put it like that,” said Harry. “I think that’s exactly right. I’ve been trying to find the words for a year.”

The watching came after, and it could only have come after, because he had never before been calm while it happened.

The cats, the roses, the teapot — he had been in those, inside the minding, with nothing left over to look with. But the tea had been built with his head as well as his hand, and there had been a moment, with the warmth going down his arm into china, when he had found himself standing far enough back inside himself to see.

So he practised seeing. That was the whole of October.

It needed a quiet effect and a long one, and he used the roses at Number Six, which went black with every frost and could be minded about honestly, and he stood at the fence on the way to the station with his hand on the cold wood and Mrs Figg at the corner not looking, and when the warmth began to rise he did not go with it. He stayed behind. He let it pass through him the way you’d let a train pass a platform, and he watched it go by.

One rose a morning. That was the pace, and there was nothing he could do about the pace, because he could only watch the warming when the warming happened and it only happened when something was wrong and he minded, and a frost came when it came. For once the slow part was not his hand. It was the weather.

What he saw he could not have told anyone. He tried, in English, in his head, and the words went round it like water round a stone. But he saw it, every frost for a month, and it did not change, and it had a shape.

Not a feeling. He had expected a feeling — warmth, kindness, the bath filling, the words he had used for it all his life — and those were there, but they were what it felt like, not what it was, the way the heat of a flame is not the flame. Underneath the warmth there was structure. The thing that passed through him went past in an order, and the order was the same every time, and parts of it were about the rose — not a picture; a grammar, the way a sentence about a rose has a rose-shaped hole in it where the word goes — and parts were about green and about back and about now, and the parts were arranged, and the arrangement was what did the work. It was a sentence. A sentence in a language he had never heard spoken, going past at a speed nobody else on earth could possibly have stood still enough to read.

He read it for a month, a frost at a time. Then he started to take it apart.

Not in English. English had no words for the parts, so he gave them names of his own, the way he had given names to the Dursleys at two. The pointing part, which said this one. The pushing part, which said so. And the bracket in the middle, which was the hardest, because the rose-shaped hole was not a rose; it was what a rose was for, and it took him four frosts to see the bracket whole and be sure of it. He laid the tea beside the rose and found they shared a spine and differed in the middle, as he had hoped. He laid Tibbles’ hips beside both — he could; he had the whole of that afternoon on the shelf — and found that one was longer, and had a part the others didn’t, a part that went looking before it pushed, and he lay on the camp bed in the dark and understood that he was holding the difference between warming a thing and mending it, taken apart, in a grammar nobody had ever taken apart before, because nobody else had ever been slow enough inside themselves to see it go by.

The boiler whooshed. He said, very quietly, to nobody, what he had been circling since the cat:

“It has parts.”

If it had parts it could be taken apart. If it could be taken apart it could be put together — differently. On purpose. He did not yet know how to make the sentences; he could only read the ones that came. But he had learned to read before, under this same staircase, off cereal packets, with nobody to teach him, and it had taken five months.

He gave himself till Christmas.

The pencil took till the second week of December.

The difficulty was that a pencil did not need anything. A rose needed to be green and a pot needed to be warm and a cat’s hips needed to be the right shape, and he could find the purpose in those and build on it honestly. A pencil lying on a camp bed under a staircase needed to lie there. That was the whole of its wants. And the magic would not come for a lie — he had proved that forty times now; I want the pencil to move got nothing, every time, not a flicker — so if he was ever going to do anything but mend and warm, he was going to have to find out what real purpose a pencil could possibly have for being an inch to the left.

He found it in the end by being honest about what the pencil was for. The pencil was for this. For the language. For finding out whether a sentence he had read off the roses could be written — made from scratch, with the pointing part and the pushing part and the bracket, and sent down his arm on purpose, and whether the world would read it back. That was not a lie. That was the truest purpose in the house; he had built his whole life on it since the doorstep. If the pencil moved, he would know. If he knew, he could learn. If he could learn, then every rose and every teapot and every pair of old hips in the world got a little nearer to being mended by someone who understood what he was doing, instead of by a boy minding on a kitchen floor.

He built that, over three weeks, until it was true in him all the way down. Then he sat cross-legged on the camp bed with the sentence he had composed held ready — his first, three parts and a bracket, this pencil, an inch, so — and put his hand flat beside the pencil, and held the sentence the way you hold a note in singing, and asked.

Nothing happened for two seconds, which was a long bad afternoon.

Then the pencil moved an inch to the left.

Not fast. Not far. It slid, the way a pencil slides when a table is nudged, except that nothing had been nudged, and it stopped exactly where the bracket had said, and lay there, and he sat and looked at it, and the house ticked, and he found that he was shaking.

The warmth had been different. That was the first thing he noticed, when he could notice anything. Thinner. Pushed, not risen; he had made the sentence and sent it, and the sending had cost something the mending never had. He was tired — actually tired, the way he had been once after a bad week, as if he had carried the pencil up a hill. The magic had not come to meet him this time. He had gone to it, with a sentence he had composed himself, and it had read the sentence, and done what it said, and let him feel exactly how much it had cost.

An inch. One pencil. He did it four more times to be sure, and the fifth time fell asleep sitting up with his hand flat on the blanket beside a pencil that was now five inches from where it had started, and dreamed, lucidly, standing in the green with his mother’s voice saying his name, and said — for the first time in five years of visiting that dream — “I moved a pencil,” and the voice did not answer, because it never did.

He had the next thing before he was properly awake, and it was so obvious that he lay on the camp bed in the dark feeling stupid for a quarter of an hour, which by his own clock was most of a season.

The slowest thing in his life was his hand.

It had always been his hand. He read at the speed of his eyes, which was five times a grown man’s and not one page faster, and he thought at a speed that had no name, and between those two and the world there was a hand that made letters the way a five-year-old’s hand makes letters, one careful loop at a time, and in May he had told a professor across a long table that he was sorry it was only forty pages because his hand was slow and he had not known how long he would have. Forty pages. He had the rest of it — hundreds of pages, the whole of what he had rebuilt from Dudley’s textbook under the stairs — in his head, in order, every line, and it was no use to anyone there, because the only way out was a pencil in a fist, and the fist belonged to a child.

A pencil had just moved without the fist.

He did not sleep again that night. He sat up with the pencil and a sheet from the back of Dudley’s old exercise book and built the purpose, which was the same purpose as before, only larger — this is for the language; this is for Carrow; this is so that what is in here can get out — and composed a sentence that was not an inch but where I look, and sent it, and the pencil stood up on its point like a dancer and fell over, and he sent it again, and it drew a line, and the line cost like a hill, and he was done for the night.

That was the problem for a week. Every stroke was a sentence, and every sentence was sent, and every sending cost. He could make the pencil draw one letter and then he had to sleep. At that rate the fist was faster.

He found the answer on the fourth night, and he found it because of the tea.

He had never let go of the warming in Mrs Figg’s pot. He had put his hand on the china and the warmth had risen and he had stayed with it while it worked — he had thought of that as watching, and it was, but it was something else as well. He had been holding the sentence open while the world read it. Not sending it, once, like a stone thrown; holding it, like a note. And the holding had not cost. The sending cost. The holding was only attention, and attention was the one thing in the world he had more of than anyone alive.

So he did not send the pencil sentence. He composed it — this pencil, where I look, so — and he held it, open, the way he had held the warmth in the pot, and he looked at the paper, and he looked at the place where the first letter should go, and the pencil went there, and he looked at where the second letter should go, and it went there, and the third, and it was writing, in his own round careful hand, because the hand it had was the one he knew, and it was writing e and v and e and r and y and he looked and it followed and he looked and it followed and the cost did not come. He was holding a note, and the note was this is for the language, this is for Carrow, and holding it was no harder than meaning it, and he meant it.

It wrote a line. He looked at the line. It was his handwriting and it had taken four seconds.

He did a page. It took two minutes, and most of the two minutes was the pencil, which was a pencil, and could only go so fast over paper before the point skidded or the paper tore. He did a second page to be sure. Then he put the pencil down and sat with his hands in his lap and looked at two pages of number theory that had been in his head since he was four and had never been anywhere else, and he understood that the thing which had been slow about him since the doorstep was over.

He gave himself to Christmas to learn the pencil properly, and it took till Christmas, because the pencil had a hundred small lessons in it. The point blunted and he learned to see it blunt and lift and turn. The paper was not flat on a camp bed and he learned to hold the sheet as well as the pencil, a second sentence beside the first, and found that he could hold two, and then that he could hold three — the sheet, the pencil, and a small warmth in his own cold fingers, which the magic allowed, it turned out, when the fingers were the thing that held the paper for someone else. He learned to look ahead of the pencil instead of at it, so that the point was always arriving at a place he had already left, and once he had that the pencil was as fast as a pencil could be, and no faster, and that was a page a minute, and he found that he did not mind the limit at all, because for once the limit was not him.

By New Year he had written three hundred and twelve pages.

He wrote them for Carrow, in English, for a reader who was slow, the way she had taught him on Thursdays a result should be written — from what you knew, towards what you wanted, with every step shown — and it was the whole of the number theory, from the first page he had worked out under the stairs with Dudley’s textbook propped against the wall to the place where he had stopped, that autumn, because he had run out of anyone to tell. He kept the pages in the biscuit tin from Edinburgh, which had been empty since the last of the good biscuits, and the tin filled, and he started a second tin.

He thought, while the pencil went, about what else was in his head that had never been anywhere else, and the list was long, and he found he was not frightened by the length of it. He had been living with a locked door between what he knew and what he could give, and the door was open, and on the far side of it was everyone.

Carrow got the tin on the first Thursday in January, in her office, with her feet on the desk.

He put it on the desk beside her feet. She looked at the tin, and at him, and took the lid off, and took out the first inch of pages, and read the first page, and the second, and put the pages down and took her feet off the desk.

“How many.”

“Three hundred and twelve. It’s the rest of what I gave you in May. All of it, from the start, so you can see where it came from. The last forty pages are new since the summer.”

She turned a page. She turned another. She looked at the handwriting, which was the handwriting from May, and at the thickness of the stack, and at him.

“In a month.”

“Since the second week of December.”

“Your hand was slow,” said Carrow. “You stood at the end of my table and apologised for it. I have had forty pages a fortnight out of you since, and I thought that was the child working all night, and I was going to say something about it.” She put her hand flat on the stack. “I write about four pages a day, Potter, on a day when I have nothing else to do, and I have been at it thirty years.”

“I write faster now,” said Harry. “I found a way.”

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide not to ask, and the deciding had the shape of a woman who had been handed a thing too large for her office and had chosen to be glad of it rather than frightened.

“Right,” she said. “I can’t read this in a term. Not properly. Not checking. I shall need people.” She put the lid back on the tin. “I am going to give some of this to Pole, who is sound, and some to a woman at Cambridge who owes me a favour and will hate every minute of it, and I am going to keep the number theory for myself because it is mine, and by Easter the three of us between us may have got through half.” She pushed the tin an inch towards him and then pulled it back. “You understand what you have done. You have made yourself faster than the people who are meant to be checking you. That is going to be the whole of your life from now on, and I should like you to be ready for it, because most of them will not be me.”

“I know,” he said. “I’ve known since I was four. I used to think it was the price. It isn’t. It’s only the shape.” He looked at the tin. “Professor. There’s more. Not mathematics. Other things, that I’ve worked out and never written, because the hand was the hand. I should like to write them. I don’t know yet who they’re for.”

“Write them,” said Carrow. “Find out who they’re for afterwards. That is the usual order.” She put her feet back on the desk. “Page nine is still wrong, by the way. You have it right in here on page two hundred and six and you have not gone back and crossed out the May version. Do it in front of me. I want to watch.”

The candle took a further month and was the hardest thing he had done, because a candle needed to be lit even less than a pencil needed to be moved, and because fire was a different bracket altogether — a thing that happened rather than a thing that was. He got there. On the eleventh of January, in the cupboard, with the door shut and a saucer under it and a stub from Dudley’s Christmas cake, a small blue flame stood up out of nothing on the wick, and burned, and he blew it out at once because of the smoke and the smell and the household, and sat in the dark that smelled faintly of Christmas and grinned.

It was not the candle he wanted. He had wanted it for a different reason, and the reason was hands.

He had found, with the pencil, that he could hold three sentences at once, and that the cost of holding was attention, and he had thought about that for the whole of one night with the pencil going beside him on a page about primes, and had seen where it went. It went to lenses. It went to the small screws in Mrs Figg’s clock that she could not see to turn. It went to threading a needle, which Petunia did with her tongue between her teeth, and to the pages of a book, which could be turned without a hand if the sentence was this page, over, so, and he tried that on the twelfth of January with the enchantment of a candle still in his fingers and read a chapter of the library’s book on optics without touching it, lying on his back on the camp bed with the book open in the air above his face and the pages going over one by one as his eyes reached the bottom of each, and understood that his reading, too, had been waiting on a hand.

It went further than that, and he made himself stop at the edge of it, because the edge was where you fell. He was six. He could move a pencil and a page and warm a pair of fingers and light a candle and mend a cat, and every one of those was a small thing, and the magic had declined, politely, every time he had tried to make it a large one. A small thing done at the speed of his attention was not a small thing. He had a shelf in his head now that had not been there in November and he called it, in English, things a steady hand could make, and he put on it, that month, a telescope, and a microscope, and a clock, and the instruments in Lindqvist’s physics lab on the second floor that he had looked at through a window and not been allowed to touch, and he did not know yet how any of it was done, and he did not need to know yet. He needed to know that it was reachable. It was. Everything was. He had thought, for five years, that what he was for was finding out. It was that. It was also, now, making, and he had not been ready for how much larger the second word was than the first.

He put it into English, in his head, in the words he would use for whoever he one day told:

I can read it. I can make four sentences of it; three cost like a hill and one costs nothing, because it is held and not sent, and the held one writes. Nobody else can read it at all, I think; Mrs F says they tell it with a stick and a word and never look. I am the only one who looks.

And I have been slow my whole life in exactly one place, and the place was my hand, and that is finished.

He did Mrs Figg’s hands on the last Saturday in January, and it was the first time he did a person, and the first time the magic came like a tide.

He had seen her hands before. He had seen everything before; that was the whole trouble with him. But he had never looked, not the way he looked now, with the hinge loose and four sentences of his own and the new rule running — every day; find what is wrong; look. Her knuckles were swollen. The joints of two fingers on the right hand went off at an angle, and she poured with the left, and he went back along four years of Saturdays in a quarter of a second and found she had always poured with the left, and he had never once asked himself why.

“Your hands look sore, Mrs Figg,” he said.

The teapot paused. “They’re old, dear. Old hands get sore. It’s the damp.”

“May I hold them? Just for a minute.”

He had never asked her for anything. He had never, he realised, asked anyone in that town for anything, except Mrs Prentice for books and Mrs Figg herself for a word, and the asking stood in the warm cabbage-smelling room like a fourth cat.

Mrs Figg looked at him over her glasses for a long moment. Then she put the pot down, and sat, and held out both her hands across the little table, palms down, the way you’d show a child a trick, and he took them.

They were cold, and dry, and the knuckles under his thumbs were hot in the wrong way, the way the old cat’s hips had been. He did not try anything. He had learned better than trying. He only sat there in the January light holding an old woman’s hands and let himself mind — not about a thing, this time, a rose or a crack, but about her, the whole of her, four years of ten pence an hour and biscuits off her own plate and nothing out of the ordinary, dear every month in the same place, and a mackintosh and a castle in a café from nine till five since May, for someone who had never once thanked her properly because he had been too busy being clever to notice there was anything to thank her for.

It came up through him like a tide.

Not the slow trickle of the roses. A tide — warm and sure and enormous, as if whatever it was had been waiting for him to mind about a person and had rather a lot to say on the subject. It went down his arms and into her hands and he felt it go looking, the way it did, hand out along a shelf, and find the knuckles, and find the two bent fingers, and consider them with great patience, and undo something, slowly, like a knot being worked loose by someone who had all day.

Mrs Figg made a small sound, the kind a person makes when something stops hurting.

He let go. Her hands lay on the table between them. The knuckles were knuckles. The two fingers on the right hand lay straight beside the others, and she looked at them, and turned the hand over, and made a fist, and opened it, and made a fist again.

Mr Tibbles, on the sugar bowl, had stopped looking like a man at a lecture and started looking like a man who had seen the examiner handed the answers.

“Well,” said Mrs Figg, very quietly, to her own right hand.

“I’m sorry I didn’t notice before,” said Harry. “I should have. I notice everything. I just wasn’t looking.”

She looked up at him then, and it was the face from the day of the cat — surprise, and arithmetic, and gladness — but with something added, something careful, like a woman closing a gate behind her to keep a thing in.

“Harry,” she said. It was the first time in four years she had used his name as the start of a sentence rather than the end of one. “You be careful who you show that to. You hear me? There are people who’d — well. You be careful. Not everybody who’d want to see it would be kind about it.”

“I know,” he said. “That’s why I showed you.”

Mrs Figg looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up the teapot, with her right hand, and poured, and her hand did not shake at all.

“Chocolate biscuit,” she said. “The good tin.”

That night he took stock, under the date on the door, in the words he would have used if there had been anyone to use them to.

Can warm. Can mend, if it is truly wrong and I truly mind, and today a person. Can move a pencil and light a candle by building the purpose honestly and composing the sentence; sent, each one costs; I sleep more after. Held, a sentence costs nothing but attention, and I have more attention than anyone alive. Cannot do anything for myself, except warm the fingers that hold a page for someone else, which is the same rule from the other side. The door faces out.

Have the beginnings of the grammar. Nine parts and a bracket. Can read what comes. Can make four sentences.

The pencil writes. Three hundred and twelve pages in a month, a page a minute, and the minute is the paper’s. Carrow has them and cannot read them alone and is finding people who can. The hand was the whole of what was slow about me, and the hand is done.

What is in here can get out now. All of it. That is the fact of the year and I have not got to the bottom of it yet. Mathematics first, because it is the thing I have most of and the thing they can check. Then the rest, in the order it is useful to somebody.

Four and a half years. Nine hundred of mine, give or take.

And under it, the word he had added to every plan he had ever made and would go on adding to the end of his life:

Faster.

Then he turned the light off, and lay in the smallest office in England with a candle stub in a saucer and a date pinned to the inside of the door at the height of his eyes, six years and six months old by the hall clock and the better part of a thousand by his own, and did not sleep, and did not mind, because there was a man in a brown jacket on BBC2 at ten past one who was going to explain the structure of the cell to nobody, and Harry Potter had nine hundred hours to get ready and intended to use every one.
