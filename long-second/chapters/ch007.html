Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 7

Interlude: Arabella and Albus

Wisteria Walk, Little Whinging, a Thursday evening in February 1987

Arabella Figg had written sixty-three letters from the kitchen table at Number Two, Wisteria Walk, over the past five years, and had never before got stuck on the first line.

The pad was the same pad. The pen was her mother’s, a fountain pen with a lever, and she filled it, this evening, holding the barrel steady in her right hand while the left worked the lever, and caught herself doing it, and stopped to look at the hand in the lamplight. Straight fingers. She made the fist and opened it. Forty years of pouring with the left and the thing was simply gone, as if she had dreamed the mornings when she couldn’t do the buttons on her own coat.

Mr Tibbles sat on the table by the sugar bowl, which he was not allowed to do, and watched her not write.

“Don’t you look at me like that,” said Arabella. “You were there. You tell me how to put it.”

She had been posted here to watch for them. For a cloak in the wrong season, a stranger asking after the house, the wards shivering in the night the way Albus had said she might feel them even without a wand, in her teeth, like weather. Five years she had watched for that and seen none of it — a few tourists, the Diggle man bowing in the street like a fool — and every month she had written it down, and at the end, in the space Albus had never asked her to fill, she had put the boy. Quiet. Clever. A reader. Too thin but they feed him. He smiles at the cats.

And then last March he had sat on her garden bench and asked her what she was, and she had written to Albus that night with her heart going, and Albus had said tell him, and she had told him, and since then every letter had been a different kind of letter, and this one was the most different yet.

She uncapped the pen.

Dear Albus, I write with the right hand. You will not know what that means, so I shall tell you first, and then the rest in order. Since the autumn of 1946 I have not been able to hold a teacup in my right hand. You have seen my hands; you have been kind enough never to mention them. Last Saturday the boy looked at them across my kitchen table and said they looked sore, and asked — asked, Albus, very politely, he has never asked me for a thing in five years — if he might hold them for a minute. And he held them, and said nothing, and I felt it. I cannot do magic, but I grew up in a house full of it and I know the feel of it in a room the way you know when the kettle is about to go. I felt it come up through him and into my hands like a bath filling, and it went to the knuckles as if it knew the way, and when he let go my fingers were straight. They are straight now. I am writing this with them. He is six years old. He has had no wand, no teaching, no one to show him. He has worked it out for himself on my kitchen floor, and I have watched him do it since June, one cat at a time, and I have said nothing, and nor has he, and we have both known exactly what the other was not saying.

She stopped there, and looked at what she had written, and found that her eyes had gone hot, which was absurd at her age over a sentence.

And here is the part I do not know how to put. He does not do it the way they do. I have watched witches and wizards do magic since I was in a pram — my brothers, their friends, you — and they TELL it. A stick and a word and the thing is done. He does not tell it anything. He sits on the floor and is sorry for a cat until the cat gets up. He told me, in a café, that it answers "caring", and that the broken things are only easier because they tell you what is wrong, and then he warmed my teapot so that I should see. I have watched magic done for sixty years and I have not seen it done like that, and I do not think you have. He is also, since last spring, at a university. I do not think I have told you that properly. I have been taking him on the train every weekday since May. The professor there found a mistake on page nine of forty pages he had written and he thanked her for it, and she has told the county, in writing, that the university will take him or she will know the reason why. He is to sit Muggle examinations this summer that children sit at sixteen and eighteen, all of them at once, because the woman from the county cannot think what else to do with him and nor can anyone. He reads six books a day. I have counted. I sit in the café and they bring me the slips. He knows I write to you. He has known for years. He asked me what I was so that I would not have to choose between him and you, and he waited two years to ask it, and when I told him he said thank you, for writing. You asked me once to tell you if he was happy. He is. He is happier than I have ever seen him, and he is six, and he can light a candle with nothing in a cupboard, and he has pinned the date of his letter to the inside of the cupboard door, and I think he has decided to spend the next four and a half years getting ready for you in a way that nobody in the history of that school has ever been ready, and I do not think any of you have the faintest idea what is coming up the drive. That is what frightens me, Albus. Not him. He is the gentlest creature I have ever met. The rest of you. I remain, as ever, at my post, which is now a café in Egham with a pot of tea. Arabella

She read it through twice, blotted it, and folded it. Mr Tibbles watched her address the envelope with the right hand.

“There,” said Arabella. “Now he knows.”

The cat looked at the envelope, and at her, with the expression that Harry Potter would have recognised at once and that she had never managed to name in nine years: the look of a creature who has sat through the whole lecture and is waiting, not optimistically, to see whether anybody was listening.

The Headmaster’s office, Hogwarts, the following Monday

Minerva McGonagall had the letter in her hand. She had read it standing, twice, and was now reading it a third time sitting down, which Dumbledore had observed over sixty years to be the sequence in which she received news she did not like. Behind her, Fawkes shifted on his perch and made a small enquiring sound, and the hundred small silver instruments on the side tables went on turning and puffing in their mild way, and the February light came in thin and white across a desk that had five years of Arabella Figg’s letters on it, in a stack, in order, with the newest on top.

“Her hands,” said McGonagall.

“So she writes.”

“I have known Arabella Figg since 1961. She has not been able to hold a teacup in her right hand since the Grindelwald years. A child of six has mended them on her kitchen floor by being sorry for her, and you are about to tell me about animals.”

“I was about to tell you,” said Dumbledore mildly, “what I believe. Which is that the boy is extraordinarily gifted, as we had every reason to expect, and that accidental magic in a strong child can take the shape of wishes, and that a child who loves an old woman—”

“She says it is not accidental. She says he does it on purpose, every Saturday, one cat at a time, and that he warmed her teapot in a café to make a point. Albus, that is not a wish. I have taught a thousand first-years. I know what a wish looks like.” She put the letter down flat on the desk and her hand flat on the letter. “And she says he does not do it the way we do. No wand. No word. He asks it. Have you ever — in a hundred years — have you ever once heard of anyone—”

“No,” said Dumbledore.

The office went quiet. He had turned a little in his chair, towards the window, and the thin light caught the half-moon spectacles and hid what was behind them, which she had also observed over sixty years to be a thing he did at the exact moments he least wished to be read.

“No,” he said again, more slowly. “I have not. I have read of it. There are accounts — very old, mostly unreliable — of what the writers called petitionary magic. Asking rather than commanding. It was thought to be a thing of the very first wizards, before wands, before incantations, and to have been lost when the tools came, the way a people loses a dialect. I had always assumed it to be a story.” He turned back. “I begin to think I was reading the wrong shelf.”

McGonagall looked at him sharply. “Then do something.”

“I am doing something, Minerva. I am waiting. It is the hardest thing I do and I am very good at it.” He took his spectacles off and polished them on his sleeve, which she had seen him do perhaps four times in sixty years. “Four and a half years. He has pinned the date to a door. Arabella says he is getting ready for us. I find I am inclined to let him. If I walk up that garden path now, he stops getting ready and starts performing — for the man who left him on the step — and whatever it is he is building in that cupboard, I should rather see it finished than see it bent round me. I did enough to him on that step. I will not do that as well.”

“And the university? The examinations? Albus, he is six, and the Muggles are about to—”

“The Muggles,” said Dumbledore, “are about to do what Muggles do when they find a child they can see, which is a great deal, clumsily, with forms. Arabella says the professor found a mistake on page nine and the boy thanked her. Minerva, I have had letters from Ministers. I do not think I have ever read a sentence that reassured me more.” He put the spectacles back on. “He is being seen. He has decided to be. By a librarian, and a county psychologist, and a professor of mathematics with a knife for a face, if Arabella’s description is to be trusted, and every one of them has looked at him and stood a little to one side and held a door. That is not nothing. That is, I rather think, the thing I hoped for and could not arrange. I will not spoil it by arriving.”

McGonagall sat with that. Outside, a crow went over the lake.

“A café,” she said at last. “She sits in a café. From nine until five. Every day.”

“With a pot of tea, which he warms. Yes.” The corner of his mouth moved. “She has turned the post into a commute. I shall have to raise her stipend. I had not budgeted for scones.”

“Albus.”

“Yes.”

“The last paragraph.”

He read it again. She watched him arrive at it — not him; the rest of you — and stop there, as she had, three times.

“She is not afraid of him,” he said slowly.

“She is afraid for us. Which is a very different thing, and in my experience the more sensible.” McGonagall stood, and smoothed her robes. “Four and a half years, you say. I shall hold you to it. On his eleventh birthday I am delivering that letter myself, in person, by hand, and I am going to look at that child with my own eyes.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “I think you had better.”

She put the letter down on his desk and went to teach the third years.

He sat a while after she had gone. The light moved. Fawkes sang two notes and stopped. On the desk, in a hand that had changed since the autumn from a careful left-handed slant to something freer, a squib in a housecoat had written he sits on the floor and is sorry for a cat until the cat gets up, and Albus Dumbledore, who had read every book on magic in the castle and most of the ones outside it, sat with the sentence for a long time and found that he had no shelf to put it on.

Then he took a fresh sheet and wrote to Arabella. He thanked her. He told her to go on exactly as she was, and to be kind to him, and to let him see nothing in her face but what was there. He raised her stipend by four galleons a month and said it was for the scones. And at the end he added one line that he had not planned, and did not cross out.

If he asks you what the first wizards did, before wands — tell him I do not know, and that I should very much like to.

Wisteria Walk, the Thursday after

Arabella Figg read the reply at the kitchen table with Mr Tibbles on the sugar bowl, and when she got to the last line she read it twice, and put the letter down, and looked at the cat.

“Well,” she said. “He heard that much. And he wants to know.” She made a fist of her right hand, and opened it. “That’s new. I don’t think I’ve ever had him ask me a question.”

Then she got up and put the kettle on, and took the good tin down from the top shelf, where it had been for five years waiting for somebody worth opening it for, and found it was nearly empty, and wrote biscuits on the pad with the right hand, and underneath it, after a moment, another tin.
