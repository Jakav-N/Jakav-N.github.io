Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 8

Open University

The second man from the county was called Mr Dyer, and he had brought the big folder, and he lasted forty minutes before he had to go and stand in the corridor for a bit.

Harry felt for him. He had decided, walking down the same corridor behind Mrs Bell this time instead of Miss Weald, with the smell of poster paint getting stronger, that he was going to do this one straight — all of it, no forgetting the next bit, no two blocks transposed for the look of the thing — and he had also understood that doing it straight was going to be hard on whoever was holding the stopwatch. It wasn’t their fault. Their tables stopped where tables stop. He was about to walk off the edge of the paper in front of a man who had got up early and driven from Kingston.

So he was gentle about it. He answered at the speed of his mouth, not his mind; he didn’t finish anything before Mr Dyer had finished asking it; when the number strings went past twenty he said them back carefully and politely as if they were a shopping list, and when the vocabulary test reached the words nobody his age knew he defined them in plain English rather than better English, and looked, he hoped, like a child who had simply read a great deal. It was all true. It was just a great deal more true than the folder had room for.

At the end of the blocks Mr Dyer said, “Right,” and squared the cards, and said “Right” again, and then said, “Would you excuse me one moment,” and went and stood in the corridor, and Harry sat with the orange squash and listened to him breathe out there for a while, slow and even, like a man doing an exercise from a leaflet.

He came back and did the rest with great steadiness. He was, Harry thought, a good man at his job; he just hadn’t known there was a far side to it. At the end he closed the folder and did not say “mm,” because he wasn’t Dr Prosser, but he looked at Harry with much the same expression, and asked the one question that wasn’t on any of the sheets.

“Why now? You were tested in the autumn. You—” he glanced at his notes “—stopped. Dr Prosser was quite clear that you stopped. What changed?”

“I read about a girl who went to Oxford at twelve,” said Harry. “And I thought: they let her. All she had to do was let them see.”

Mr Dyer wrote that down. It was the only thing he wrote all afternoon that wasn’t a number.

The meeting was three weeks later, in Mrs Bell’s office, which had never held six people and did so now with the door open and a chair borrowed from the staff room.

Vernon wore the suit. Not the work suit — the other one, the one for Rotary dinners and the funeral of anyone senior at Grunnings — and he sat in it on Mrs Bell’s good chair with his hands on his knees and the expression of a man attending his own investiture. Petunia sat beside him in her gloves and said nothing for an hour and a quarter, which Harry, who had been waiting six years to see her sit through a conversation about him without her hand going to her collarbone, counted among the finest things he had ever watched. Mr Dyer had the folder. A woman from the county called Mrs Achebe had a different folder, and seniority, and a pen she clicked when she was thinking.

Harry sat on the borrowed chair and listened to five adults decide what to do with him, at the speed adults did things, and did not mind at all. He had set this table. The food on it was his.

The county, said Mrs Achebe, had no precedent. That was said early and then gone back to several times, like a bad tooth. The scores were — she looked at Mr Dyer, who looked at the folder — not interpretable in the usual way. The instruments, said Mr Dyer, with a face Harry would remember fondly for years, had ceilings. What the county could offer was this: they wanted him moved for mathematics and the sciences to the secondary school in September, to sit in with the fourth years, while remaining with his own age for everything else; they wanted him entered for examinations as soon as the boards would allow, which was probably the summer after next; they wanted a county tutor, one afternoon a week, to go over whatever the secondary couldn’t; and they wanted it understood by all parties that none of this had been done before, with anyone, and they would be making it up as they went.

Vernon said he thought that sounded very sensible. He said it twice. He asked whether the Rotary might hear of it, in due course, and Mrs Achebe said she couldn’t see why not, and Vernon sat a little taller in the funeral suit.

Petunia said nothing. Her hands stayed in her lap.

They asked Harry, at the end, as a courtesy, whether he had anything to add, and he had been waiting an hour and a quarter — a working year — for exactly that question, and had one sentence ready, because sentences were still slow and he wanted this one to carry.

“I’d like the library at the secondary as well,” he said. “Not just the classes. Their library. After school, if they’ll let me. I’ve read ours.”

Mrs Bell looked at him over her tea. Mr Dyer wrote it down. Mrs Achebe clicked her pen twice, which he had worked out by then meant yes, and I don’t know why I didn’t think of it.

“I don’t see why not,” she said.

Walking home, three paces behind the funeral suit, Harry turned the whole meeting over at leisure and found only one line in it he would have changed, and it wasn’t his. It was none of this has been done before. It had been said as a worry. He had heard it as a map.

The nights were the best of it, that spring, and the nights were a dead man’s.

Mrs Prentice’s boxes came into the returns cupboard on the Thursday as promised, four of them, cardboard gone soft at the corners, and she let him do the string. Inside were the Open University course units — slim pale books, dozens of them, with the course codes on the spine and the lectures’ dates pencilled inside each cover in a hand that wasn’t hers. M101. M203. M332. S101, the science foundation, all of it. A101, the arts one, which he hadn’t expected and which turned out to have the history of the Renaissance in it and a unit on Beethoven he read on the bus. And in the margins, in the same pencil hand, Mr Prentice.

Surely not — on page forty of a unit about infinite sets. And he had been right; the book had been careless. Lovely — beside a proof Harry had found himself two winters ago and never had anyone to show. Ask R. — he never found out who R was. Enough for one night, at the end of a chapter on something hard, and the date beside it, and the next date three days later, and Harry sat in the returns cupboard with the pale book open and understood that he was reading a man learning, slowly, at one-to-one, in the evenings, after work, for the pleasure of it, nine years dead, and that the man had been good at it, and had stopped at the hard bits and come back to them, and had never once written of course.

He read the margins before the text. He kept doing it, through all four boxes, for the whole of that year. It was the nearest thing he had ever had to a teacher who worked at his own pace rather than the world’s, and the man was dead, and had done it for nobody, and Harry never told Mrs Prentice about the margins because he suspected she knew, and that the boxes had sat in her spare room nine years for exactly that reason, and that some things were better carried than said.

The broadcasts themselves he took on the lounge carpet, eighteen inches from the screen, with the sound at two and the Radio Times open on his knee, every night there was one, which was most nights, and two or three on Saturdays before dawn. He did not fall behind. He could not have explained to anyone how a child of seven did not fall behind on four hours’ sleep, and nobody asked, because nobody knew, and he learned to be lying in the cupboard with his eyes shut by the time the boiler whooshed.

They were not, as television, very good. The men wore brown jackets and the women wore the sort of blouses that had been chosen by a committee, and they stood in studios that looked like the waiting rooms of provincial dentists and wrote on boards with felt pens that squeaked. He loved them past all reason. For six years every voice he had learned from had been on a page, fixed, finished, and now here were people teaching — thinking aloud, going back, saying in fact let’s do that again more carefully, hesitating before a hard step in a way that told you where the hard steps were. He had never had that. The first time a lecturer in a brown jacket got to a place Harry had got to alone under the stairs and went a different way — a shorter way, a way that walked through a wall Harry had spent a subjective month going round — he sat on the carpet and laughed out loud in the dark, and then clapped his hand over his mouth, and listened to the house for a full minute, and then laughed again more quietly, because it had been such a good wall.

He was wrong twice that spring, against the men in brown jackets. Twice they taught a thing he had derived for himself and taught it differently and he sat there certain, in the dark, that they had slipped — and worked it through, and found they hadn’t, and that he had, years ago, in a place he had never gone back to check because nobody had ever been there to make him. Two errors, in a city he had built alone and walked ten thousand times. He went back and fixed everything downstream of both, which took a fortnight, and he logged them in the notebook with a care bordering on affection, and they were — he wrote this and meant it — the two best nights of the year.

Because he had never been wrong before. Not like that. Not caught by someone who knew more. The Dursleys could not do it, the school could not do it, the branch library could only tell him what it had on the shelf. For six years the only person who had ever found an error in Harry Potter’s work was Harry Potter, and he had begun to suspect that this was a kind of poverty, and now a man in a brown jacket with a squeaking pen had done it twice before Easter, for free, at a quarter past one in the morning, without even knowing he was there.

There were, he thought, lying under the stairs with the boiler starting up, going to be people out there who could do that to him on purpose. Who would do it to his face, and enjoy it, and be right.

He could hardly wait.

He also began, that spring, to write.

Not the notebooks; those had always been there. This was different. He bought — with the first money he had ever spent on himself, two pounds forty from the toffee tin, Mrs Prentice making the purchase and raising one eyebrow at the newsagent — a stack of lined A4 pads, and he began, in the evenings before the broadcasts, in English, in his slow careful round hand, to set down the mathematics.

All of it. His own, from the beginning. Everything he had built alone under the stairs since he was four, re-derived properly, in order, with the proofs written out so that they could be followed — by someone who didn’t already know, by someone slow, by someone at one-to-one with only the ordinary number of hours in a night. He found this harder than the mathematics had been. His mind did not naturally move at a reader’s pace; it had never had to. Learning to write for a person who could not see all of a thing at once, who needed the steps laid down like stepping stones and no more than one stone at a time, was a discipline as strange and real as the handwriting itself, and he worked at it the way he worked at everything, which is to say for a very long time every evening and then again in his head on the walk to school.

He did it because of the margins. Because a man had sat in the evenings for years learning, and had died, and had left only pencil — surely not, lovely, enough for one night — and Harry had sat in a returns cupboard and read the whole of him off the sides of a stranger’s textbook and wished, hard, that the man had written more. He was not going to make that mistake. Whatever he found out past the lamp posts was going to be written down so that someone could read it who wasn’t him. That was what the writing was. It was the first thing he had ever made for people he hadn’t met.

By the summer there were four pads. By the autumn there were eleven, and a system. He did not show them to anyone. It was not yet time.

The secondary school was called Stonewall High, and it was a mile and a quarter from Privet Drive by the main road, and on the first Tuesday of September, aged eight years and five weeks, he walked into a fourth-year physics lesson with a county letter in his bag and twenty-eight fifteen-year-olds turned round in their chairs to look at him.

He had thought about this moment for the better part of a subjective decade and had concluded there was nothing to be done about it but to walk in. So he walked in. The room smelled of gas taps and old chalk. The teacher, a tall stooped man called Mr Haines with a cardigan the colour of a wet pavement and chalk on both elbows, looked down from the height of his own surprise and said, in a voice that had clearly been told about this and had not quite believed it until now:

“Ah. You’ll be Potter.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Right. Well.” He looked round the room as if a seat might volunteer. “Sit at the front, there’s a desk. We’re on moments. Do you know what a moment is?”

“A force times its perpendicular distance from the pivot,” said Harry. “Sir.”

Twenty-eight fifteen-year-olds made a sound that was not quite a laugh, and Mr Haines’s eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch and stayed there for the remainder of the autumn term.

What he had not foreseen — he had modelled fear, mockery, being ignored, being made a mascot, all the things a small child might be to a room of large ones — was that fifteen-year-olds would be kind. Not sweetly; they were fifteen. Clumsily, sideways, in the manner of people who had themselves quite recently been small and still half remembered it. The boy at the next desk, a big quiet one called Marcus with a rugby bruise down one cheek, slid a calculator across without a word when he saw Harry didn’t have one, and when Harry slid it back at the end of the lesson, unused, Marcus looked at the blank screen and then at him and said, “You didn’t need it,” not as a question, and from then on sat with him at lunch as if the matter had been settled by a committee. A girl called Rachel made it her business to walk him between lessons for the first fortnight, because the corridors at changeover were a river and he came up to most people’s waists. Nobody called him anything. Somebody tried, once, in the second week, a bigger boy from another class in the dinner queue, and three fourth-years Harry barely knew turned round at once with the flat territorial faces of a herd, and the bigger boy decided he had somewhere else to be.

He had expected to have to manage it. He never managed any of it. It came to meet him, like the year of the cat.

The lessons themselves were — he wrote this down honestly — too slow, and he had known they would be, and he had decided beforehand that slow was not the same as useless. Mr Haines knew things. Not things from books; things from thirty years of watching sixteen-year-olds misunderstand the same twelve ideas in the same twelve ways, and he taught against those misunderstandings, which meant Harry, who had never had a misunderstanding corrected by anyone in his life until a man in a brown jacket at one in the morning, was watching a master of a craft he hadn’t known existed. He watched the craft. He let the physics come at its own pace and watched Mr Haines teach, the way he’d watched Mrs Prentice reunite a toddler with a biscuit, and in the slow stretches he did the fourth-years’ homework in his head for the pleasure of seeing where they’d trip.

And there was the library. The county had kept its word. Stonewall’s library was three times the branch and open till five, and he was in it every day from the last bell, at eye-speed, with a sixth-former called Ben who was supposed to be supervising and who, after the first week, simply did his own revision at the next table and said “night, Harry” at five without looking up.

Twenty thousand books. Open till five. He had asked for it in one sentence in Mrs Bell’s office, and now he was in it, and by half-term he had read every book on the science shelves and was a third of the way down the history, and he found himself, one October evening, standing between the stacks with a book about the Thirty Years’ War in his hand, simply breathing, with the lights humming, because for the first time in his life the input was arriving faster than the hunger, and he had not known, until it stopped, what the hunger had sounded like all those years. It had sounded like the hall clock. It had been there so long he had taken it for silence.

The chess happened almost by accident, and then not at all by accident.

Stonewall had a club, Wednesdays in the geography room, run by a maths teacher called Mr Pole who had been quite good once and knew it. Harry went because it was there and because he had read every chess book in two libraries and had been playing both sides of a board in his head since he was five, and had never once sat across from a person. He wanted to know what that was like.

It was like this. Mr Pole, humouring the county’s small prodigy, set up the pieces and said, “Let’s see what you’ve got, then,” and Harry — who had, after all, spent the equivalent of some centuries at this, and had decided on the bus that he was finished with being small in any room that wasn’t his aunt’s kitchen — beat him in nineteen moves without appearing to think.

That wasn’t quite true. He thought a great deal; he had three minutes and twenty seconds, by his own clock, for every second of Mr Pole’s. He simply didn’t look as if he thought, because he had decided on the bus to stop pretending to find things hard, and so each move came out at the speed of his hand, a half-second after Mr Pole’s, calm and polite, and the effect — he saw it in the faces round the geography room, which had gone quiet — was of a very small boy playing a very large man the way a man plays a child.

Mr Pole, to his great credit, laughed. Then he set the pieces up again and lost in twenty-six, and laughed less, and set them up a third time, and on the Friday he drove Harry himself, in a Vauxhall that smelled of pipe, to the county under-elevens in Guildford, having rung the Dursleys to ask and been told by Vernon that the boy might as well, it was educational.

The under-elevens took a day. It was eleven rounds in a church hall with bad lighting and a tea urn, against children of nine and ten who had been playing in clubs since they could reach the board, and Harry won all eleven, and the last one was against a boy of ten called Tristan who was genuinely good — who saw things, who made Harry work, who had him for a full fourteen moves in the middlegame with a knight sacrifice Harry hadn’t seen coming and had to think his way out of at a speed he usually reserved for falling over on ice. He won that one in sixty-two moves, and when it was over Tristan held out his hand across the board, white-faced and furious and ten years old, and said, “You’re eight,” and Harry said, “I’m sorry,” and meant it, and Tristan said, “Don’t be sorry, that was the best game I’ve ever had,” and they shook, and Harry walked out of the church hall into the Guildford dusk carrying a cup with a plinth and the strange new knowledge of what it was to be hunted across a board by a mind he hadn’t made.

He wanted more of that. He hadn’t expected to.

The Surrey Advertiser had sent a photographer, because the county had tipped them off, because Mrs Achebe clicked her pen and things happened. The photograph, when it ran the following Thursday — LITTLE WHINGING BOY, 8, IS COUNTY CHESS CHAMPION — showed a small child in a school jumper holding a cup almost as tall as his head, with Mr Pole in his pipe-smelling jacket beaming over his shoulder, and underneath, a paragraph about the county’s special arrangements, and Stonewall, and a quote from Vernon Dursley, 43, the boy’s uncle, who said the family had always encouraged him.

Vernon bought six copies. One went in a frame in the lounge, between Dudley’s swimming certificate and the wedding photograph, where Harry’s face had never once appeared in six years. He stood in front of it, that Thursday evening, for a long time by his own clock — perhaps a second and a half by the hall’s — and examined what he felt, and found that it was not pride, exactly, and not the pleasure of a plan landing, though it was that too.

It was that he was in the frame. The house had a photograph of him on the wall now, and would keep it there because of what it said about the house, and that had cost him one sentence and a game of chess, and it had been available the whole time.

The second copy of the Advertiser, he knew for a fact, had gone to Mrs Figg, because he had watched her from the end of the road taking it out of her letterbox with her right hand, the good one now, and standing on her step reading it, and looking up the street towards Number Four with an expression he could read from forty yards — and then going inside, quickly, in the manner of a woman with a letter to write.

He let her. It was, he had decided, all part of being seen.

He was eight, and in the frame, and in the fourth year, and in the Advertiser, and in a dead man’s margins every night till one, and county champion with a cup on the sideboard, and the five-part plan at the back of the newest notebook had ticks against four of its lines in pencil. The fifth line, the one about every day and the loosening hinge, had no tick, because it was not the kind of line that ever got one. It just said look, and he was looking, and it was working, and that was a story for its own chapter.
