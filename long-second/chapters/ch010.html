Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 10

Real Work

The old woman at the bus stop had a flask of tea that had gone cold, and Harry had been standing next to her for four minutes, which was most of a day, and he had decided to warm it.

Not for her. That was the whole point, and it had taken him three months to arrive at it, standing at that same stop every school morning with the frost on the timetable. He liked her — she was called Mrs Oduya, she went to the hospital on Tuesdays and Thursdays to visit someone she never named, and she wore a hat with a cherry on it in all weathers — but liking was not what opened the door. He had tried liking. He had tried standing beside her minding about her cold tea the way he had minded about the cat’s hips, and nothing had come, because the cat’s hips had been wrong and cold tea was only cold. The thing in him did not come for comfort. It came for mending.

So he had changed the question. He had stopped asking what was wrong and started asking what was for.

And it turned out — this was the three months — that purpose was not something you had or didn’t have, like a cold. It was something you could build. He had built the warming of Mrs Oduya’s tea the way he had built the plan at the back of the notebook: honestly, in pieces, over weeks. She goes to the hospital to sit with someone. She sits a long time; the flask is to get her through. She drinks it cold now because the walk is long and she won’t waste it. If it were warm, the sitting would be a little better, and whoever she sits with would get a little more of her. He did not know who it was. He didn’t need to. He only needed to mind about it so truly that the minding and the wanting were the same thing — and on the fourth of January, standing in the frost beside a hat with a cherry on it, he found that he did.

He put his hand on the side of the flask, as if steadying himself against the shelter.

It came. Slowly, like the roses, but it came: the warmth rising up through him and down his arm and into the steel, and he felt it find the tea, and find the cold in the tea, and go about undoing the cold with the unhurried competence of a man who had done this a thousand times and saw no reason to rush, and then the bus came round the corner, and he took his hand away.

Mrs Oduya got on ahead of him. He sat three rows behind and watched her unscrew the flask at the first stop out of habit, and sniff the steam, and look at the flask, and look out of the window, and shake her head slowly in the manner of a woman who has decided not to understand something, and drink.

Harry looked out of his own window at Little Whinging going by and let the fact settle.

It had not been an accident. It had not been fear, or a cat, or a thing he’d found broken. He had stood at a bus stop and decided on a purpose and built it true enough that the door opened for it, and the door had opened, and warm tea was going to a hospital in a flask, and nobody on earth knew but him.

He was eight and a half, and the thing in him answered to the front door now, if you knocked properly.

The watching came after. He had not been able to do it before — the effects had always arrived under fear or under feeling, and in either state he was in the thing, not beside it — but the tea had changed that. The tea had been calm. He had built it with his head and his hand, and there had been a moment, with the warmth going down his arm into the steel, when he had been standing far enough back inside himself to look.

So he practised looking. That was the whole of February.

It needed a quiet effect and a long one. He used the roses at Number Six, which came back black every frost and could be minded about honestly, and he stood at the fence on school mornings with his hand on the cold wood and the purpose built — this one, the one by the gate, back to green — and when the warmth began to rise he did not go with it. He stayed behind. He let it pass through him the way you’d let a train pass a platform you were standing on, and he watched it go by.

What he saw he could not have told anyone. He tried, later, in the notebook, and the words went round the thing like water round a stone and never touched it. But he saw it, and he kept seeing it, every morning for a month, and it did not change, and it had a shape.

It was not a feeling. He had expected a feeling — warmth, kindness, the bath filling, the words he had used for it all his life — and those were there, but they were what it felt like, not what it was, the way the heat of a flame is not the flame. Underneath the warmth there was structure. The thing that passed through him went past in an order, and the order was the same every time, and parts of it were about the rose — he could feel the shape of a rose in it, not a picture, a grammar, the way a sentence about a rose has a rose-shaped hole in it where the word goes — and parts of it were about green and about back and about now, and the parts were arranged, and the arrangement was what did the work. It was a sentence. It was a sentence in a language he had never heard spoken, going past at a speed that nobody but him on earth could possibly have been standing still enough to read.

He read it for a month, a word at a time. Then he started to write it down.

Not in English. English had no words. He invented marks — a new notebook, a blue one, bought by Mrs Prentice with a raised eyebrow out of the chess money — for the parts of the sentence as he could pick them out: a mark for the about-this part, which pointed; a mark for the make-it-so part, which pushed; a bracket for the shape of the thing in the middle, which was the hardest, because the rose-shaped hole was not a rose, it was what a rose was for, and he spent a week on that bracket alone and threw out four versions. He drew the tea beside the rose and found they shared a spine and differed in the middle, as he’d hoped they would. He drew Mrs Figg’s hands from memory — he could, he had the whole of it on the shelf — and found that one was longer, and had a part the others didn’t, a part that went looking before it pushed, and he sat with the blue notebook on the camp bed and understood that he was looking at the difference between warming a thing and mending it, written down, by him, in a language he had made up that morning, and that nobody else had ever written down anything like it, because nobody else had ever been slow enough inside themselves to see it go by.

He put the pencil down. Under the stairs the boiler whooshed. He sat quite still with his heart going and said, very quietly, to nobody, the thing he had been circling since the ice:

“It has parts.”

If it had parts, it could be taken apart. If it could be taken apart, it could be put together — differently. On purpose. He did not yet know how to make the sentences; he could only read the ones that came. But he had learned to read before, under this same staircase, off cereal packets and the backs of adverts, with nobody to teach him, and it had taken five months.

He gave himself a year. He was eight. He was, he thought, being generous.

The pencil took till Easter and nearly took his sanity, and he loved every minute of it.

The difficulty was that a pencil did not need anything. A rose needed to be green and a flask needed to be warm and an old woman’s hands needed to be straight, and he could find the purpose in those and build on it honestly. A pencil lying on a camp bed under a staircase needed to lie there. That was the whole of its wants. And the thing in him would not come for a lie, he had proved that a hundred times — would not come for I want the pencil to move, sat polite with its hands folded at the mere suggestion — so if he was ever going to do anything but mend what was broken, he was going to have to find out what real purpose a pencil could possibly have for being an inch to the left.

He found it in the end by being honest about what the pencil was for. The pencil was for this. For learning the language. For finding out whether a sentence he had read off the roses could be written — made from scratch, with the pointing part and the pushing part and the bracket, and sent down his arm on purpose, and whether the world would read it back. That was not a lie. That was the truest purpose in the house; he had built his whole life on it since the doorstep. If the pencil moved, he would know. If he knew, he could learn. If he could learn, then every rose and every flask and every pair of bent hands in the world got a little nearer to being mended by someone who understood what he was doing, instead of by a boy minding on a kitchen floor.

He built that, over three weeks, the way he had built the tea. He built it until it was true in him all the way down, and then he sat cross-legged on the camp bed with the blue notebook open to the sentence he had composed — his first, three marks and a bracket, this-pencil, an-inch, so — and put his hand flat beside the pencil, and held the sentence in his head the way you hold a note in singing, and asked.

Nothing happened for two seconds, which was a long, bad afternoon.

Then the pencil moved an inch to the left.

Not fast. Not far. It slid, the way a pencil slides when a table is nudged, except that nothing had been nudged, and it stopped exactly where the bracket had said, and lay there, and he sat and looked at it, and the house ticked, and he found he was shaking.

The warmth had been different. That was the first thing he wrote down, when he could write. Thinner. Pushed, not risen; he had made the sentence and sent it, and the sending had cost something the mending never had — he was tired, actually tired, the way he’d been after Mrs Figg’s hands, as if he had carried the pencil up a hill. The thing in him had not come to meet him this time. He had gone to it, with a sentence he’d written himself, and it had read the sentence, and done what it said, and let him feel exactly how much it had cost.

An inch. One pencil. He did it four more times that night to be sure, and the fifth time he fell asleep sitting up with the blue notebook open on his knee and his hand still flat on the blanket beside a pencil that was now five inches from where it had started, and he dreamed, lucidly, standing in the green with his mother’s voice saying his name, and he said — for the first time in seven years of visiting that dream — “I moved a pencil,” and the voice did not answer, because it never did, but he had wanted to tell someone, and that was who there was.

The candle took a further six weeks and was the hardest thing he had ever done, because a candle needed to be lit even less than a pencil needed to be moved, and because fire was a different bracket altogether, a thing that happened rather than a thing that was. He got there. On the second of June, in the cupboard, with the door shut and a saucer under it and Dudley’s birthday-cake candle stub from the bin, a small blue flame stood up out of nothing on the wick like a thought arriving, and burned, and he blew it out at once because of the smoke and the smell and the household, and sat in the dark that smelled faintly of a birthday and did not even try to stop himself grinning.

He could warm. He could mend, a little. He could move a pencil an inch and light a candle, and each one cost him like a hill, and he could do none of it for himself — he had tried that too, properly, on his own short sight one evening, with the glasses off and the purpose built as true as he knew how, so that I can read without the frames, and the thing in him had sat with its hands folded and declined, politely, as if to say that it would be delighted to help with the roses, and the hands, and even the pencil, but that this was simply not what it was for. He kept the glasses. He suspected he would keep them a long time, and he wrote that down too, under the heading he used for the facts he liked least: the shape of the door is outward. It will not turn round and look at me. Perhaps nothing that answers caring ever can.

It was the best year of his life, and he was aware, writing that, that he had said so about the last two as well.

He asked Mrs Figg on the last Saturday in June, in the garden, with the cats asleep on the warm paving and Mr Tibbles on the wall doing his impression of a sentry who has stopped pretending not to be one.

He had worked out, over the spring, that he had been waiting to ask her for two years and that the waiting had been the old rule in a new coat. Don’t poke the kind thing. It had seemed wise at six. At eight and three-quarters, with a blue notebook of a language nobody spoke and a candle he could light from across a room if the room was small, it seemed like a boy standing outside a library with the lights on because he was afraid the door might be locked.

He had also worked out that she wanted him to. She had a way, since the hands, of leaving sentences open at the end, like a gate on the latch.

“Mrs Figg,” he said, “what are you?”

She was deadheading the geraniums with the right hand. She did not stop, but she slowed, and he saw the shoulders settle in the way he had seen Petunia’s settle at the sink the night of the letter — the look of a woman putting down something she has been carrying a long way.

“I was wondering,” she said, “when you’d get round to it. I had money on this spring. Tibbles thought Easter.”

“I didn’t want to make you choose,” said Harry. “Between me and whoever you write to.”

Mrs Figg turned round then. She looked at him over the geraniums for a long moment, with the face from the kitchen — the surprise, the arithmetic, the gladness — and then she put the secateurs down on the wall beside the cat, and sat on the garden bench, and patted the slats beside her, and he sat.

“I was told,” she said, “by the man I write to, that if you ever asked me what I was, I was to tell you the truth. He said he suspected you already knew. He said he suspected that was the least of what you knew.” She looked at her right hand, resting on her knee, the straight fingers. “He had the right of that, I think.”

“I know there’s a second set of rules,” said Harry. “I’ve known since I was three. I know the people in cloaks run on it, and the man who bowed, and the photograph on your mantelpiece that moved. I know my parents were part of it and didn’t die in a car. I know you watch me for someone kind. I know what I can do isn’t supposed to be possible, for anyone, and I know you knew what it was the first time the cat got up.” He stopped. “I don’t know the word. I’ve never had the word.”

Mrs Figg was quiet for a moment, in the sun, with the bees in the lavender.

“The word is magic, dear,” she said. “Just that. You’ll think it’s a silly word. Everybody does, the first time. It isn’t.”

He had expected to feel something when he heard it, and he did, but not what he’d planned for. It wasn’t revelation. He had known the thing for years; a word was only a label on a drawer. What he felt, sitting on a garden bench in Little Whinging with the bees going, was something much smaller and much older than revelation, and it took him a second to place it, and the second was a long time.

It was company. Someone had said the word to him. Out loud, in a garden, as a plain fact, the way you’d tell a child the name of a bird. For seven years he had been the only person in the world who knew there was a word to say, and now he was one of two.

“Tell me,” he said. “Please. Everything you’re allowed.”

She told him. Not everything — he could feel the gates she kept shut, and respected them, because she kept them with the face of a woman under orders she thought were right. But a great deal. That there were people who could do what he did — thousands of them, a whole hidden world of them, with their own streets and shops and newspapers, living in the same country behind a kind of curtain, which was why nobody at Number Four had ever met one except by accident. That they were called witches and wizards, and that the word was not an insult where they came from. That she was what they called a squib: born to a family of them, two brothers who could do it and she who couldn’t, and so she lived on this side of the curtain and kept cats and did what she could, and what she could, for seven years, had been to watch over one small boy for a man who had asked her to.

“A good man,” said Harry.

“The best I know. Which isn’t the same as always right, mind, and I’ve told him so.” She didn’t give the name. He didn’t ask.

That there was a school, in Scotland, where children like him were sent at eleven to learn to do properly what he had been doing on her kitchen floor. That a letter would come. That his mother and father had both gone to that school, and had been very good — “your mum especially, dear; everyone said” — and that what had happened to them had happened in that world and not this one, and that it was not hers to tell him, and that he would learn it, she promised, and soon enough.

“Eleven,” said Harry.

“Your eleventh birthday. It always comes on the birthday.”

Three years. A hair under, by the calendar. Fifteen hundred and some, by his own.

He sat with that in the sun. Three years to be ready for a world that had schools and streets and newspapers, that had sent a man in a violet hat to bow to him and a kind squib to watch him, that had taken his parents in some way that was not his to know yet, and that was going to send for him on a date he could now write at the top of the plan. Three years in which nobody on the far side of the curtain, by the sound of it, had the slightest idea that a boy on this side was reading their sentences off the roses in a blue notebook in a cupboard under the stairs.

He found he was not frightened. He examined the not-being-frightened carefully, the way he examined everything, and found it was made of two things. One was that he had been getting ready for this since a doorstep, without knowing what it was, and the getting ready had gone rather well. The other was the woman beside him on the bench, with her straight right hand on her knee, who had kept a gate on the latch for two years and never once pushed him through it.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “Thank you. For all of it. The seven years.”

“Oh, get on,” said Arabella Figg, and picked up the secateurs, and her voice had gone odd, and she turned back to the geraniums so that he wouldn’t see her face, which he had already seen, and would keep, on the shelf where he kept the things that had made him who he was.

Mr Tibbles, on the wall, looked at Harry with the lecture-hall face, and then — slowly, deliberately, like a man signing a form — closed his eyes, and went to sleep in the sun.

That night the plan got a new page, and the page had a date at the top: 31 July 1991.

Under it he wrote what he had, in English, in the round hand, for the reader he hadn’t met.

Can warm. Can mend small things, alive or not, if they are truly wrong. Can move a pencil and light a candle by building the purpose honestly and writing the sentence. Each sentence costs; the cost is real; I sleep more after. Cannot do anything for myself; the door faces out.

Have the beginnings of the language. Seven marks and a bracket. Can read what comes. Cannot yet write much. Nobody else can read it at all, I think, because nobody else can stand still enough.

There is a world. There is a school. They will send for me in three years and they do not know about the notebook.

He looked at that for a while. Then he turned to the back of the newest pad, the one with the five-part plan with its four ticks, and under the fifth line — every day; look — he wrote, small, the first thing he had ever added to it:

Faster.

Then he went down to the lounge in his socks and turned the television on with the sound at two, because it was ten past one, and a woman in a committee blouse was about to explain the structure of the cell, and he had, by his own reckoning, fifteen hundred years to get ready, and intended to waste none of them.
