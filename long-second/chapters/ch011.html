Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 11

On Holding

The second time Mrs Figg took him to Diagon Alley, in the week before Christmas, she had a list, and the list was in his handwriting, and it was two pages long, and she looked at it on the train and said, “Well, he can afford it,” in a voice that made the man opposite look up from his paper.

“Who can?”

“The man I write to. He paid the last bill without a word. Not a word, Harry — I had the galleons back by owl in four days and a note that said thank you for the itemisation and nothing else, and I have known that man forty years and I have never once known him pay for anything without a word.” She folded the list. “I think he wants to know what you’ll buy next. I think he is finding out what you are from my receipts.”

“Then I’ll make them interesting,” said Harry.

He made them interesting. He had read the seven grades and Waffling and Bagshot and the two histories in six weeks, which was most of twenty years, and he had come out the far end knowing exactly where the shelf stopped — the way he had known at five where the branch stopped and at six where the fourth floor stopped — and this time he had a map of the shop in his head from forty minutes of spines and knew which dusty singles were the ones that went past the edge. A Guide to Advanced Transfiguration. Ancient Runes Made Easy, which was not easy, which was the point. Magical Drafts and Potions, and the two fat books that followed it on the shelf. A thing called The Theory of Enchantment that the young woman with the inky fingers said nobody had asked for since she had worked there, and which Harry bought on that recommendation alone. A history of the Ministry. A volume of wizarding law — the actual law, the statutes, dry as the phone book and twice as useful — and a bound year of the Daily Prophet, 1981, which cost more than he had expected and which he bought anyway, with his hand flat on the cover, because it was the year, and because he had learned at five that a newspaper was the fastest way into a place.

Fifty-eight galleons. Mrs Figg wrote it down, itemised, on the train home, in the right hand, and said, “He’ll have a fit,” with every appearance of pleasure, and the fit, when it came, arrived by owl four days later in the form of fifty-eight galleons and a note that said thank you for the itemisation and, underneath, in a smaller hand, as if added after the ink had dried: The enchantment one is very good. Tell him to read chapter nine twice.

Harry read chapter nine twice. It was very good. He put a margin on it that said he knew I would need this, and underlined nothing, and lay awake till the boiler thinking about a man in Scotland who paid for books he was not supposed to know about and told the boy which chapter to read.

That winter he read the far side, and for the first time in his life he wrote it down.

Not all of it; even with two trips and a list he had perhaps forty books, and forty books was two afternoons at eye-speed and he knew it. But forty books chosen with a map, by a mind that had just spent twenty years on the hundred and sixty brackets of the Standard Spells, was not two afternoons’ worth of thinking, and he gave them the winter, under the stairs, after the university closed, with the swing on the wall and the house asleep and the pencil going.

The transfiguration book was the one that frightened him, in the good way, the way the fourth floor had. The Standard Spells had been sentences — this, so. Transfiguration was grammar: a hundred pages on how the bracket for what a thing is could be rewritten, from the inside, if you held the whole of the old thing and the whole of the new thing in your head at once and did not let either slip for the length of the casting. The book said this was very hard and that most students never managed more than a matchstick. Harry read it and thought: they cannot hold both for a quarter of a second. I have been holding three sentences at once since December. He did not try it. He had no telephone, and the book was very clear about what went wrong when the holding slipped, and he had promised Mrs Figg on a bench that he would be careful. But he composed the sentence — the whole of it, the old bracket and the new and the long patient becoming between them — and held it, and knew he could hold it, and kept it with the others he was saving for the day he had a wand.

The runes book he read in a night and understood in a week, and what he understood changed the shape of everything after it. Runes were sentences in matter. You did not hold them; you carved them, and the carving held them for you, for years, for centuries, the way the wall in the yard had held its sentence since whoever had built it died. The warmth on the doorstep had been a sentence. The hand on the back of his head had been a sentence. And here was a book that said, in effect, sentences can be written down somewhere other than a mind, and he sat under the stairs at two in the morning and saw, very clearly, a thing that nobody in the hundred and sixty brackets or Waffling’s two hundred pages had seemed to see: that if a sentence could be carved, a sentence could be made. Manufactured. Put in a thing and handed to someone who could not read it. The way a lecture on BBC2 put a thought in a box and handed it to a boy on a carpet at one in the morning.

He gave it a title, because Carrow had taught him that results should be named, and the title was Sentences in Things, and under the title: This is where the lever is. Not in the hand. In the carving. Find out everything about carving.

And it was the runes book, in the end, that made him write the grammar down, because the runes book was proof that a sentence could outlive the mind that held it, and he had a grammar in his head that nobody else alive could read, and if he fell under a bus it would go with him, and the next person to stand still inside a quarter-second and see a sentence go by would start, as the man in 1911 had, from nothing.

He had never written a word of it. The parts had names, and names were enough for a mind that did not forget. But a reader was not that mind. A reader needed marks, and English had no words for the parts, and he was not going to spend a thousand pages saying the pointing part and the pushing part. So on the ninth of January, 1988, in the cupboard, with the pencil going and the swing on the wall, he made marks: one for every part he had a name for, forty-one of them that first night, with the nearest English he could find beside each, and it was the first time the grammar had existed anywhere but behind his eyes. He called it On the Composition of Charms: A Second Edition, because the thin grey book from 1911 was a first edition, and its author had been a decent man who could not see down the road and had said so, and deserved the credit, and under the title he wrote: Begun from where E.B. stopped, by someone who can see the road, for whoever comes after and can’t. By the end of January it was two hundred pages, in the marks, with the English beside them, in the Edinburgh tin under the camp bed, and he had no idea who would ever read it, and he wrote it anyway, the way Carrow had said.

The law he read because he had read the phone book at three and knew what dry books were for. It told him the Ministry was a government with departments, and that there was a Decree about children and magic — the Trace, it was called, in a footnote; a thing that watched for magic near the underage and sent a letter, and that it had never once sent a letter to Little Whinging in six years, which he filed and thought about and concluded, provisionally, that a sentence asked for on a kitchen floor without a stick did not look, to whatever did the watching, like what it was watching for. He filed that under provisional and decided not to lean on it.

And the Prophet of 1981 he read last, in one night, from January to December, and he did it the way he had read the General’s waiting room at five — for the shape of the fear in the careful prose, the way a newspaper goes quiet around a thing — and found the fear, and found it going out, like a tide, through November, and found, in the issue of the third of November, himself. A paragraph. No photograph. The infant son of Lily and James Potter. And in the issue of the ninth, a letter to the editor from a witch in Kent asking where the boy was, and whether he was safe, and whether someone was looking after him, and an editorial reply, brief and careful, saying that the matter was in good hands.

He sat with that for a while. A woman in Kent had written to a newspaper to ask if he was all right. He had been in a cupboard, being small. He put the bound year on the shelf by the camp bed with the swing above it and the date beside it and thought that he would like, one day, to find the witch in Kent and tell her yes.

The feather held on the second of February, 1988, at a quarter to midnight, in the cupboard, and it was the first time his magic had ever looked like theirs.

He had been working towards it since the second trip. Grade 1, chapter four: the Levitation Charm, swish and flick, the single most-practised charm in the first-year curriculum, according to Waffling, who disapproved of it. He had had the sentence under it since October, cleaner in his own parts than the book described it — but he had always known it was a thing he could not do, because a thing sent without a telephone arrived at a tenth of its strength and a tenth of lift was not lift. The pencil had taken three weeks and an inch. A feather was lighter than a pencil. But the book did not want the feather lifted. It wanted it held.

And holding was the thing he had. He had found it with a pencil in December and he had been living on it since — three sentences at once, a page a minute, a book turning its own pages above his face — and it had never once occurred to him, until the runes, to ask whether he could hold one of theirs.

Because the book was full of the opposite. Waffling had a whole sour paragraph on first-years whose feathers shot into the ceiling or wandered off sideways; the grey book from 1911 had said flatly that imprecision in the casting moment is the universal experience and may be regarded as a law. The sentence went out of them ragged. It had to. They sent it in a quarter of a second they could not watch, with their eyes shut, and so it arrived with the bracket slightly wrong and the feather went where the wrongness pointed. They had a telephone and they shouted down it.

He had no telephone. But he could hold a sentence steady for the whole of a casting moment that nobody else alive could even see. If the sentence was perfect — not his version; theirs, every mark of it, the way they had sanded it — and if he held it, then there would be nothing in it to wander. The feather would go exactly as far as a tenth of a telephone could send it, and stop, and stay.

He spent January learning their sentence. Not to send it; to hold it, in the back of his mind, on the train, in the café, in the fourth-floor carrel, the way he held the pencil, until he could carry it through a conversation with Mrs Figg about Snowy’s ear without dropping a mark. Then he built the purpose, which for a feather took the same honesty it had for a pencil — so that I know whether I can — and on the second of February, with a feather from the pillow on the camp bed and his hand flat beside it and the house asleep, he held Wingardium Leviosa in his head the way he would have held a note, and sent it, and the feather rose.

An inch. Then two. And then — this was the thing, this was what he had been working six months towards without quite knowing it — it stopped. Not fell. Stopped, two inches above the blanket, and hung there, perfectly still, turning very slightly in the air from the cupboard door, and held, while he held the sentence, for four seconds, which for him was the better part of a quarter of an hour of looking at a feather hanging in the dark doing exactly, precisely, to the hair, what he had told it to.

He let it down. He was shaking, and not from the cost, though the cost was real and he slept till the boiler.

Because he understood, lying there, what he had just seen. Not that he could do it — he had known since the pencil that he could do something. That it had held. The book said the feather would rise and the student would struggle to control it. Waffling said they went into the ceiling. The grey book said it was a law. And his feather had gone two inches and stopped and turned in the draught and stayed, because he had held the sentence steady through a moment nobody else could see, and so there was nothing in it to wander.

They could not do that. He was sure of it now. Not the children in the book; not, he suspected, most of the adults; not the man in Scotland who read receipts. They had a telephone and could send a hundred times what he could — but they sent with their eyes shut, and the sentence went out ragged, and the feather shot into the ceiling. When he had a telephone, he would be sending with his eyes open.

He named it, because results were named, and the name was On Holding, and it went into the Edition that night as its third chapter, in the marks, with this in English under it for the reader:

This is what the two hundred is for. Not the maths. Not the fifteen papers. I have been reading sentences nobody else can see since I was six, and in three and a half years I will be handed a telephone, and I do not think anyone on the far side has any idea what happens when you give one of those to someone who can hold.

I intend to find out carefully.

The pot stand was Easter, and it was the first thing he ever made that had a sentence in it, and he made it for Mrs Figg because she was the one who had never once asked.

It had been coming since the runes. If a sentence could be carved and the carving would hold it, then the thing to find out was whether he could carve one — not with a wand, which set runes the way the book described, with a word and a tap; with holding. He had thought about it for most of a week of his own on the train and decided that it ought to work, because a rune was only a sentence that somebody had held steady long enough for matter to take the shape of it, and holding steady was the one thing he could do that nobody else could.

He needed something to cut with, and something to cut. The cutting tool he made on Haines’s lathe on a Saturday in March, from a length of silver steel Haines had in a drawer, while Haines told him about the clock — the pendulum clock was finished by then; it had been finished in February; it stood on the bench in the Stonewall physics lab in a case Haines had made and lost four seconds a week, which Haines said was better than the one in the staff room and Harry said was not yet good enough. Haines watched him grind the point and said, “That’s a graver. What do you want with a graver?”

“To cut letters in wood,” said Harry, which was true.

“What letters?”

“Ones I made up.”

Haines looked at him for a moment and went back to his tea.

The wood was an oak offcut from the same drawer, four inches across and an inch thick, and he took it home in his bag and sat with it on the camp bed for three nights composing the sentence before he cut a mark. It was his warming — the one from the doorstep, the one from the café pot, the one he knew better than any sentence in the world — with a bracket he had never built before: not this pot, now but whatever stands on me, as long as this wood is whole. A sentence with no now in it. A sentence that was meant to go on without him.

He cut it on the fourth night, with the door shut and the graver in his right hand and the sentence held open in his head the way he held the pencil, and he found that the graver was the pencil — that the hand knew already, from the mirror and the lathe and three hundred pages a month, how to be told something two hundred times a second and do it — and the marks went into the oak clean and small, forty of them, round the rim, and as the last one closed he felt the sentence leave him. Not sent. Not held. Set: taken out of his mind and put into a disc of wood, where it sat, running, very quietly, the way the roof ran, and did not need him any more.

He put the cold teapot on it. He waited. The teapot went warm.

He sat and looked at it for a long time by his own clock. A thing in the world was doing magic and he was not doing anything at all. He had made it, and it worked, and it would go on working for anyone who put a pot on it, whether they could read or not, and that — he had known it since the runes, and knowing it was not the same as holding a warm teapot in a cupboard at one in the morning — that was the lever.

He gave it to Mrs Figg on the Saturday, wrapped in brown paper, at the kitchen table, with Tibbles on the sugar bowl.

She unwrapped it. She looked at the marks. She looked at him.

“Put the pot on it,” said Harry.

She put the pot on it. She waited, with her hand on the lid, and the lid went warm under her hand, and she took the hand away and looked at it, and put it back.

“Harry,” she said. “What is this.”

“It’s the warming. The one from the café. In the wood, instead of in me. It’ll go on as long as the wood’s whole. I made the letters up; they’re not anybody’s runes. I thought — you sit in that café with a pot that goes cold by half past, and I warm it when I come down, and I’m not always going to be there to come down.”

Mrs Figg sat with her hand on a warm teapot in a kitchen that smelled of cabbage and looked at a boy of seven across the table and did not say anything for a while.

“My brother Edgar,” she said at last, “was a very good wizard. He could do things with a wand I never saw anyone else do. He could not have done this. I don’t know anyone who could have done this. People buy things with runes in them, dear, from shops, from men who spent twenty years learning the runes and have a wand to set them with, and they cost a great deal, and they do about what this does, and not one of them was made by a child at a kitchen table with a — what is that?”

“A graver. I made it. On a lathe. Mr Haines has a lathe.”

“A graver,” said Mrs Figg. She took her hand off the pot and put it over her mouth and he saw, above it, that her eyes had gone wet, and that she was not going to let them do anything about it. “I shall have to put that in a letter,” she said through her fingers. “I have been putting things in letters for seven years and this is the first time I have not known where to start.”

“Start with the teapot,” said Harry. “That’s what it’s for.”

The pot stand stayed on her kitchen table for the rest of the book. He saw it there every Saturday, with a pot on it, and she never once mentioned it again, and the biscuits were from the good tin every time.

The paper appeared in April, and it was eleven pages long, and it had been in print since 1865.

Not the paper. The journal. Carrow handed him the offprint in her office on a Thursday with the rain on the windows and the lake gone pewter, and he held it and read his own name in the font of a conversation that had been going on for a hundred and twenty-three years — H. Carrow and H. Potter, Royal Holloway and Bedford New College, University of London — and thought about Mr Prentice’s margins, and the reader in fifty years, and found that for once he was not thinking about the far side at all.

“The referees,” said Carrow, “did not believe it.”

“You said they wouldn’t.”

“They said so in writing. Politely. The first report said that while the proof appeared sound they could not help noticing that the second author was reported in the national press to be seven years old, and would the first author care to comment.” She took her glasses off. “I commented. I said the second author had found the overlap on the back of my door while I was looking at the front of it, and that if the referee wished to dispute the proof he was welcome to find the hole, and that if he wished to dispute the author he was welcome to Egham on a Thursday and could bring his own chalk.” She put the glasses back on. “He found no hole. He did not come to Egham. The second report was shorter. Ashworth’s is out in June and yours — the one alone — in September, and the referee on yours asked whether the author had a supervisor and I said yes, in the sense that I supervise the weather.”

Harry turned the offprint over. The back was blank. He had not expected it to be anything else and he looked at it anyway.

“It gets easier,” said Carrow, watching him. “The first one. You think it’s the thing. It isn’t the thing. It’s the start of the thing.” She took the offprint back and put it on the stack. “Now. Three things. The woman in London says yes to the book, and she says it in the form of a contract, which Pargeter has, and which says money, which Pargeter says goes where you said. It comes out in September. She wants a title that isn’t For a Reader Who Is Slow and I told her that was your business.” She held up a second finger. “There is a problem I have been stuck on since 1979 that I think you ought to see. And there is a man in the physics department called Lindqvist who has read our paper and the two hundred pages you wrote him in May and has been, I am told, impossible to live with since, and wants to know whether the second author would care to come and tell his third-years why their last problem sheet was wrong, because he had marked it and it was, and he could not for the life of him see where.”

“Where was it wrong?”

“I haven’t the faintest idea. It’s physics.” The knife-face did its small thing at one corner. “Tuesday. Two o’clock. He has a board.”

He went on the Tuesday. The problem sheet was wrong in the fourth question, in a way that was not wrong so much as careless — a limit taken in the wrong order, the kind of thing a man in a brown jacket at one in the morning would have stopped and said in fact let’s do that again more carefully — and he stood at the board in a room of nineteen-year-olds who had been told nothing and turned round in their chairs to look at the child in the school jumper, and he showed them, at the speed of his hand, which was fast now and he let it be only as fast as they could follow, and Dr Lindqvist sat in the back row with his arms folded and his eyebrows up a quarter of an inch, and at the end said, “Yes. Yes, damn it. Thank you,” and then, as the third-years filed out, to Harry, in a lower voice:

“The two hundred pages. I have read them four times. There is a thing on page ninety that I think is wrong and I cannot show it, and there is a thing on page one hundred and forty that I think is right and nobody has it, and I have been a physicist for twenty-two years and I have not had a month like the one I have just had.” He unfolded his arms. “The half of physics that is not in books needs a room. Equipment. Someone standing next to you saying no, not like that. I have a room. It has equipment in it that cost more than this building and a technician called Mrs Osei who has been saying not like that to undergraduates for twenty years and would, I suspect, enjoy saying it to someone who listened. You are seven. I am not supposed to let you anywhere near it.” He looked at the board. “Thursdays, after Carrow, if Carrow can spare you. Mrs Osei will stand next to you. You will touch nothing she has not said you may. Yes?”

“Yes,” said Harry. “And page ninety is wrong. I found it in March. I’ll bring you the page.”

He went on Thursdays. Mrs Osei said not like that to him eleven times in the first month and he kept all eleven and never needed any of them twice, and by June she had stopped saying it and started saying go on, then, and the other half of physics — the half that needed a room — began, at last, to be a thing he could read. He built a spectroscope in May from a prism and a slit and a length of tube, because Mrs Osei said the department’s was out for repair and would be for a year, and she looked at it and at the lines it threw on the wall and said, “Hm,” which from Mrs Osei was a paper in a journal, and it stayed on the bench, and the third-years used it.

Saturdays at the branch were Mrs Prentice’s idea, and he had been waiting for someone to have it since the sports hall.

She said it at the returns desk with the folder of the book in front of her, the week the contract came: that there were children in Little Whinging who were going to be the boy at the back, and that she knew which ones, because she knew which mothers came in on a Saturday morning to get out of the house, and that the children’s corner was empty from ten till twelve, and that if a person were to sit in it with a pencil and explain seven eights nine different ways until one of them went in, nobody at the council would need to be told.

“I can’t teach,” said Harry. “Not properly. I learned it all by myself and I don’t know how it looks from the outside.”

“You taught your cousin.”

He had not told her about the tennis ball. He looked at her, and she looked at the returns desk, and he understood that Mrs Figg and Mrs Prentice talked, on Thursdays, about more than castles.

“Ten o’clock,” said Mrs Prentice. “I shall put a notice up. I shan’t put your name on it.”

Two came, the first Saturday. A girl of nine called Rachel, who was Mrs Okafor’s daughter and had not been told that the boy from the sports hall would be there and went red to the hairline when she saw him, and sat down anyway; and a boy of ten called Lee who could do any sum you gave him in his head and could not read the question. He sat with them in the children’s corner on the small chairs with the pencil going on a pad between them, and he did not teach. He had learned that from Dudley. He found what Rachel already loved, which was horses, and put the fractions in a stable; and he found what Lee could already do, which was the sums, and made the reading a thing you did to get at them; and by twelve Rachel had done a page of fractions without once looking at him and Lee had read a whole question aloud, slowly, with his finger, and got it right, and looked at the page as if it had been hiding from him.

They came the next Saturday, and the one after. By June there were five. He never once said anything clever in that corner, in two hours, in a year, and it was the hardest discipline he had ever kept, and he found he looked forward to it more than Thursdays.

Dudley’s tables he taught in March, sideways, through a tennis ball, and the teaching taught him more than the sum did.

Year Three did tables tests on Friday mornings, out loud, down the rows, and Dudley — who could do things with a football that Harry privately rated above anything in the fifth-floor journals, but for whom seven eights sat in the mind like a wasp in a jar — had come home three Fridays running the colour of putty. There was to be a grand one at the end of term, up to twelves, in front of the class, and Dudley had got it into his head that he would be made to stand up and fail it out loud, and the dread had taken the stuffing out of him so thoroughly that he had gone off his food, which at Number Four was a national emergency.

Harry watched it build for a fortnight and found, to his mild surprise, that he minded.

Not strategy. There was nothing in it for him; a frightened Dudley was a quieter Dudley. He just did not like watching it. Dudley was not clever and never would be, and had spent years making Harry’s life a low grey misery over exactly that gap, and here he was all the same, eight years old, sick with fear over seven eights, and the fear was real, and Harry had two hundred times as long as anyone else to sit and feel how real it was. Knowing a thing is small doesn’t make it light to the one carrying it. He kept that.

So he fixed it, and the fixing was the lesson.

He could not teach Dudley. Teaching Dudley was like the tablet in the sardines — the moment the lesson showed its face, Dudley clamped shut and bolted. But Harry had spent his whole life getting into rooms by other doors, and he knew by now that Dudley did not learn through the front of his head at all. Dudley learned the way he learned goal nets and the batting order and the names of all forty-odd men who had ever played for his team — through the side, through chant and rhythm and not-noticing-you-were-doing-it.

Three weeks of small deniable sideways work. A football rhyme, invented and left lying about, that happened to carry the sixes in its bounce. The eights banged out on the stairs with a tennis ball, two steps to a line, which Harry started doing himself, pointedly, until Dudley took it up to prove he could do it louder. A long car journey to Marge’s turned, by Harry asking Vernon innocent questions about mileage, into forty minutes of Vernon doing sums aloud and getting them wrong and Dudley, delighted, correcting him. None of it looked like teaching. By the end Dudley thought he had simply stopped being frightened of his own accord, the way the weather clears, and that was exactly the shape Harry had built it to have, because a confidence you think you found yourself is worth ten that somebody handed you.

Dudley stood up at the end of term and got to twelve twelves with one wobble, and came home two feet taller than he’d left, and told the whole table that tables were easy, really, you just had to not be thick about it, and Vernon beamed, and Petunia did a steak-and-kidney to mark the occasion, and looked, once, across the table at Harry, with the face with the wall out of it, and said nothing, and he understood that she had seen every tennis ball.

Underneath the table, in the country of one, he turned the whole operation over and found it good, and found also the thing it had taught him, which was bigger than Dudley and bigger than tables and which he would spend the rest of his life proving out:

You almost never move a person by the front door. You find the door they already use. Dudley through football. Petunia through a cup of tea. Vernon through the Rotary. Rachel through horses and Lee through sums. Mrs Prentice through a held-open door, and Mrs Figg through a mended cat, and Carrow through a mistake on page nine. Everyone he had ever managed, he had managed sideways, through the thing they already loved, and it had never once failed, and the one time in his life he had gone through the front door unasked — behind the cooker, Aunt Petunia — he had frightened a woman into five years of cold.

The magic answered caring, and people answered the same way. It was the same law twice, he thought, lying under the stairs that night with the swing on the wall and a good dinner in him for once. You could not make either come by force. You went to where it already lived, and you asked properly, and you were patient, and then — cat’s hips, boy’s tables, warmth in a blanket on a cold step — it came.

He put it in the Edition, in English, under On Holding, because it belonged there. The front-door law. Same as the other. Hold the sentence; ask properly; it comes.

He turned eight on the last day of July, and nobody at Number Four marked it, which was the custom, but when he came back from Egham that evening there was a parcel on his camp bed in brown paper with no name on it, and inside was a blue fountain pen — a good one, old, with a lever — and a card that said only for the pads. E.P., and he sat on the bed and held it for a long time by his own clock, and then he used it, that night, on the thirty-first pad of mathematics for Carrow, because that was what it was for, and it was a good pen, and it did not know that the hand holding it was not doing the work.

Then he took stock, because Carrow had taught him that you took stock before you went on, and he did it in English, in his head, in the words he would one day say to somebody.

Eight. Well over a thousand of mine.

Read: the branch, the fourth and fifth floors, the journals to 1960 in maths and physics, all seven grades and forty of their books. The runes are the lever. The law has a footnote about a Trace and it has never once written to this house.

Written: twenty-three hundred pages since the pencil. Three papers out by September, one alone. Two hundred pages for Lindqvist, who has read them four times. The book, in September, with a title I have not chosen, and money from it going where Vernon cannot reach. Four hundred pages of the Edition, in the marks, in a tin, for a reader I have not met.

Made: a mirror, and Saturn in it. A clock that loses four seconds a week. A graver. A spectroscope the third-years use. A pot stand with a sentence in it, on a kitchen table two streets away, warm.

Can: hold. That is the whole of it and it is enough. A feather that stays. A hundred and sixty of their brackets and forty of my own. Six sentences sent cold, each costing like a hill; three held, costing nothing; one set, in oak, costing nothing ever again. Nothing for myself; the door faces out; I have stopped minding.

Have: a professor with a knife for a face and another with a room. A woman at a desk who says go on, then. A man with a lathe. Five children on Saturdays who do not know what I am and do not need to. A squib who spends scone money and a man in Scotland who pays the bills and tells me which chapter. An aunt who made me laugh. A swing.

Have not: a telephone. Three years.

He looked at that last line for a while. Then he turned to the inside of the cupboard door, where the date was pinned at the height of his eyes and the swing beside it, and under the date, under Faster, in the new blue pen, small, he added to the door — where things were said — what the year had taught him and what he would carry up the drive in three years like a lamp:

They shout. I can hold. Find out what that is worth.

Then he went down to the lounge in his socks, because it was ten past one, and a man in a brown jacket on BBC2 was about to explain thermodynamics to nobody, and Harry Potter, eight years old and the author of three papers in a journal older than his grandparents, sat on the carpet eighteen inches from the screen with the sound at two, and listened, and compared.
