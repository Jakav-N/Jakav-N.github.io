Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 11

On Holding

The second time Mrs Figg took him to Diagon Alley, in the week before Christmas, she had a list, and the list was in his handwriting, and it was two pages long, and she looked at it on the train and said, “Well, he can afford it,” in a voice that made the man opposite look up from his paper.

“Who can?”

“The man I write to. He paid the last bill without a word. Not a word, Harry — I had the galleons back by owl in four days and a note that said thank you for the itemisation and nothing else, and I have known that man forty years and I have never once known him pay for anything without a word.” She folded the list. “I think he wants to know what you’ll buy next. I think he is finding out what you are from my receipts.”

“Then I’ll make them interesting,” said Harry.

He made them interesting. He had read the seven grades and Waffling and Bagshot and the two histories in six weeks, which was most of twenty years, and he had come out the far end knowing exactly where the shelf stopped — the way he had known at five where the branch stopped and at six where the fourth floor stopped — and this time he had a map of the shop in his head from forty minutes of spines and knew which dusty singles were the ones that went past the edge. A Guide to Advanced Transfiguration. Ancient Runes Made Easy, which was not easy, which was the point. Magical Drafts and Potions, and the two fat books that followed it on the shelf. A thing called The Theory of Enchantment that the young woman with the inky fingers said nobody had asked for since she had worked there, and which Harry bought on that recommendation alone. A history of the Ministry. A volume of wizarding law — the actual law, the statutes, dry as the phone book and twice as useful — and a bound year of the Daily Prophet, 1981, which cost more than he had expected and which he bought anyway, with his hand flat on the cover, because it was the year, and because he had learned at five that a newspaper was the fastest way into a place.

Fifty-eight galleons. Mrs Figg wrote it down, itemised, on the train home, in the right hand, and said, “He’ll have a fit,” with every appearance of pleasure, and the fit, when it came, arrived by owl four days later in the form of fifty-eight galleons and a note that said thank you for the itemisation and, underneath, in a smaller hand, as if added after the ink had dried: The enchantment one is very good. Tell him to read chapter nine twice.

Harry read chapter nine twice. It was very good. He put a margin on it that said he knew I would need this, and underlined nothing, and lay awake till the boiler thinking about a man in Scotland who paid for books he was not supposed to know about and told the boy which chapter to read.

That winter he read the far side.

Not all of it; even with two trips and a list he had perhaps forty books, and forty books was two afternoons at eye-speed and he knew it. But forty books chosen with a map, by a mind that had just spent twenty years on the hundred and sixty brackets of the Standard Spells, was not two afternoons’ worth of thinking, and he gave them the winter, under the stairs, after the university closed, with the blue notebook open and the swing on the wall and the house asleep.

The transfiguration book was the one that frightened him, in the good way, the way the fourth floor had. The Standard Spells had been sentences — this, so. Transfiguration was grammar: a hundred pages on how the bracket for what a thing is could be rewritten, from the inside, if you held the whole of the old thing and the whole of the new thing in your head at once and did not let either slip for the length of the casting. The book said this was very hard and that most students never managed more than a matchstick. Harry read it and thought: they cannot hold both for a quarter of a second. I have been holding a bracket for a feather for four minutes. He did not try it. He had no telephone, and the book was very clear about what went wrong when the holding slipped, and he had promised Mrs Figg on a bench that he would be careful. But he wrote the sentence out in his marks — the whole of it, the old bracket and the new and the long patient becoming between them — and looked at it, and knew he could hold it, and put the page in the back of the notebook with the others he was saving for the day he had a wand.

The runes book he read in a night and understood in a week, and what he understood changed the shape of the List before he had written the List. Runes were sentences in matter. You did not hold them; you carved them, and the carving held them for you, for years, for centuries, the way the wall in the yard had held its sentence since whoever had built it died. The warmth on the doorstep had been a sentence. The hand on the back of his head had been a sentence. And here was a book that said, in effect, sentences can be written down somewhere other than a mind, and he sat under the stairs at two in the morning and saw, very clearly, a thing that nobody in the hundred and sixty brackets or Waffling’s two hundred pages had seemed to see: that if a sentence could be carved, a sentence could be made. Manufactured. Put in a thing and handed to someone who could not read it. The way a lecture on BBC2 put a thought in a box and handed it to a boy on a carpet at one in the morning.

He wrote that down in English, because it was for the reader he hadn’t met, and he gave it a title, because Carrow had taught him that results should be named, and the title was Sentences in Things, and under it he wrote: This is where the lever is. Not in the hand. In the carving. Find out everything about carving.

The law he read because he had read the phone book at three and knew what dry books were for. It told him the Ministry was a government with departments, and that there was a Decree about children and magic — the Trace, it was called, in a footnote; a thing that watched for magic near the underage and sent a letter, and that it had never once sent a letter to Little Whinging in six years, which he filed and thought about and concluded, provisionally, that a sentence asked for on a kitchen floor without a stick did not look, to whatever did the watching, like what it was watching for. He wrote that down under provisional and decided not to lean on it.

And the Prophet of 1981 he read last, in one night, from January to December, and he did it the way he had read the General’s waiting room at five — for the shape of the fear in the careful prose, the way a newspaper goes quiet around a thing — and found the fear, and found it going out, like a tide, through November, and found, in the issue of the third of November, himself. A paragraph. No photograph. The infant son of Lily and James Potter. And in the issue of the ninth, a letter to the editor from a witch in Kent asking where the boy was, and whether he was safe, and whether someone was looking after him, and an editorial reply, brief and careful, saying that the matter was in good hands.

He sat with that for a while. A woman in Kent had written to a newspaper to ask if he was all right. He had been in a cupboard, being small. He put the bound year on the shelf by the camp bed with the swing above it and the date beside it and thought that he would like, one day, to find the witch in Kent and tell her yes.

The feather held on the second of February, 1988, at a quarter to midnight, in the cupboard, and it was the first time his magic had ever looked like theirs.

He had been working towards it since the second trip. Grade 1, chapter four: the Levitation Charm, swish and flick, the single most-practised charm in the first-year curriculum, according to Waffling, who disapproved of it. He had had the sentence under it since October, cleaner in his marks than the book described it — but he had always known it was a thing he could not do, because a thing sent without a telephone arrived at a tenth of its strength and a tenth of lift was not lift. The pencil had taken three weeks and an inch. A feather was lighter than a pencil. But the book did not want the feather lifted. It wanted it held.

And holding, he had begun to think, was the thing he had that they didn’t.

Because the book was full of the other thing. Waffling had a whole sour paragraph on first-years whose feathers shot into the ceiling or wandered off sideways; the grey book from 1911 had said flatly that imprecision in the casting moment is the universal experience and may be regarded as a law. The sentence went out of them ragged. It had to. They sent it in a quarter of a second they could not watch, with their eyes shut, and so it arrived with the bracket slightly wrong and the feather went where the wrongness pointed. They had a telephone and they shouted down it.

He had no telephone. But he could hold a sentence steady for the whole of a casting moment that nobody else alive could even see. If the sentence was perfect — not his version; theirs, every mark of it, the way they had sanded it — and if he held it, the way he had learned to hold a bracket at the roses, then there would be nothing in it to wander. The feather would go exactly as far as a tenth of a telephone could send it, and stop, and stay.

He spent January learning to hold their sentence. Not to send it; to hold it, in the back of his mind, on the train, in the café, in the fourth-floor carrel, the way you hold a note in singing, for an hour, for a morning, until he could carry it through a conversation with Mrs Figg about Snowy’s ear without dropping a mark. Then he built the purpose, which for a feather took the same honesty it had for a pencil — so that I know whether I can — and on the second of February, with a feather from the pillow on the camp bed and his hand flat beside it and the house asleep, he held Wingardium Leviosa in his head the way he would have held a note, and sent it, and the feather rose.

An inch. Then two. And then — this was the thing, this was what he had been working six months towards without quite knowing it — it stopped. Not fell. Stopped, two inches above the blanket, and hung there, perfectly still, turning very slightly in the air from the cupboard door, and held, while he held the sentence, for four seconds, which for him was the better part of a quarter of an hour of looking at a feather hanging in the dark doing exactly, precisely, to the hair, what he had told it to.

He let it down. He was shaking, and not from the cost, though the cost was real and he slept till the boiler.

Because he understood, lying there, what he had just seen. Not that he could do it — he had known since the pencil that he could do something. That it had held. The book said the feather would rise and the student would struggle to control it. Waffling said they went into the ceiling. The grey book said it was a law. And his feather had gone two inches and stopped and turned in the draught and stayed, because he had held the sentence steady through a moment nobody else could see, and so there was nothing in it to wander.

They could not do that. He was sure of it now. Not the children in the book; not, he suspected, most of the adults; not the man in Scotland who read receipts. They had a telephone and could send a hundred times what he could — but they sent with their eyes shut, and the sentence went out ragged, and the feather shot into the ceiling. When he had a telephone, he would be sending with his eyes open.

He wrote that down at the end of the chapter he had been writing all winter, the one that began where the grey book stopped, and he gave the chapter a title, and the title was On Holding, and under it, smaller, in the round hand, for the reader in fifty years:

This is what the two hundred is for. Not the maths. Not the fifteen papers. I have been reading sentences nobody else can see since I was six, and in three and a half years I will be handed a telephone, and I do not think anyone on the far side has any idea what happens when you give one of those to someone who can hold.

I intend to find out carefully.

The paper appeared in April, and it was eleven pages long, and it had been in print since 1865.

Not the paper. The journal. Carrow handed him the offprint in her office on a Thursday with the rain on the windows and the lake gone pewter, and he held it and read his own name in the font of a conversation that had been going on for a hundred and twenty-three years — H. Carrow and H. Potter, Royal Holloway and Bedford New College, University of London — and thought about Mr Prentice’s margins, and the reader in fifty years, and found that for once he was not thinking about the far side at all.

“The referees,” said Carrow, “did not believe it.”

“You said they wouldn’t.”

“They said so in writing. Politely. The first report said that while the proof appeared sound they could not help noticing that the second author was reported in the national press to be seven years old, and would the first author care to comment.” She took her glasses off. “I commented. I said the second author had found the overlap on the back of my door while I was looking at the front of it, and that if the referee wished to dispute the proof he was welcome to find the hole, and that if he wished to dispute the author he was welcome to Egham on a Thursday and could bring his own chalk.” She put the glasses back on. “He found no hole. He did not come to Egham. The second report was shorter.”

Harry turned the offprint over. The back was blank. He had not expected it to be anything else and he looked at it anyway.

“It gets easier,” said Carrow, watching him. “The first one. You think it’s the thing. It isn’t the thing. It’s the start of the thing.” She took the offprint back and put it on the stack. “Now. There is a problem I have been stuck on since 1979 that I think you ought to see, and there is a man in the physics department called Lindqvist who has read our paper and wants to know whether the second author would care to sit in on his third-year lectures, and I told him the second author sat in on nobody, the second author read, and he said in that case would the second author care to come and tell his third-years why their last problem sheet was wrong, because he had marked it and it was, and he could not for the life of him see where.”

“Where was it wrong?”

“I haven’t the faintest idea. It’s physics.” The knife-face did its small thing at one corner. “Tuesday. Two o’clock. He has a board.”

He went on the Tuesday. The problem sheet was wrong in the fourth question, in a way that was not wrong so much as careless — a limit taken in the wrong order, the kind of thing a man in a brown jacket at one in the morning would have stopped and said in fact let’s do that again more carefully — and he stood at the board in a room of nineteen-year-olds who had been told nothing and turned round in their chairs to look at the child in the school jumper, and he showed them, at the speed of his hand, which was slow enough that they could follow and fast enough that they stopped looking at the jumper, and Dr Lindqvist sat in the back row with his arms folded and his eyebrows up a quarter of an inch, and at the end said, “Yes. Yes, damn it. Thank you,” and then, as the third-years filed out, to Harry, in a lower voice:

“Carrow says you read. What have you read, in physics.”

“The fourth floor. Most of the fifth. The journals back to 1960, in the parts I could follow, which was about half.”

Dr Lindqvist looked at him for a long moment.

“The other half,” he said. “That’s the half that needs a room. Not a book. Equipment. Someone standing next to you saying no, not like that.” He unfolded his arms. “I have a room. It has equipment in it that cost more than this building and a technician called Mrs Osei who has been saying not like that to undergraduates for twenty years and would, I suspect, enjoy saying it to someone who listened. You are seven. I am not supposed to let you anywhere near it.” He looked at the board, where the fourth question stood corrected in a seven-year-old’s round hand. “Thursdays, after Carrow, if Carrow can spare you. Mrs Osei will stand next to you. You will touch nothing she has not said you may. Yes?”

“Yes,” said Harry.

He went on Thursdays. Mrs Osei said not like that to him eleven times in the first month and he wrote all eleven down and never needed any of them twice, and by June she had stopped saying it and started saying go on, then, and the other half of physics — the half that needed a room — began, at last, to be a thing he could read.

Dudley’s tables he taught in March, sideways, through a tennis ball, and the teaching taught him more than the sum did, and he was aware, writing it up, that he had said that about several things that year.

Year Three did tables tests on Friday mornings, out loud, down the rows, and Dudley — who could do things with a football that Harry privately rated above anything in the fifth-floor journals, but for whom seven eights sat in the mind like a wasp in a jar — had come home three Fridays running the colour of putty. There was to be a grand one at the end of term, up to twelves, in front of the class, and Dudley had got it into his head that he would be made to stand up and fail it out loud, and the dread had taken the stuffing out of him so thoroughly that he had gone off his food, which at Number Four was a national emergency.

Harry watched it build for a fortnight and found, to his mild surprise, that he minded.

Not strategy. There was nothing in it for him; a frightened Dudley was a quieter Dudley. He just did not like watching it. Dudley was not clever and never would be, and had spent years making Harry’s life a low grey misery over exactly that gap, and here he was all the same, eight years old, sick with fear over seven eights, and the fear was real, and Harry had two hundred times as long as anyone else to sit and feel how real it was. Knowing a thing is small doesn’t make it light to the one carrying it. That went in the notebook.

So he fixed it, and the fixing was the lesson.

He could not teach Dudley. Teaching Dudley was like the tablet in the sardines — the moment the lesson showed its face, Dudley clamped shut and bolted. But Harry had spent his whole life getting into rooms by other doors, and he knew by now that Dudley did not learn through the front of his head at all. Dudley learned the way he learned goal nets and the batting order and the names of all forty-odd men who had ever played for his team — through the side, through chant and rhythm and not-noticing-you-were-doing-it.

Three weeks of small deniable sideways work. A football rhyme, invented and left lying about, that happened to carry the sixes in its bounce. The eights banged out on the stairs with a tennis ball, two steps to a line, which Harry started doing himself, pointedly, until Dudley took it up to prove he could do it louder. A long car journey to Marge’s turned, by Harry asking Vernon innocent questions about mileage, into forty minutes of Vernon doing sums aloud and getting them wrong and Dudley, delighted, correcting him. None of it looked like teaching. By the end Dudley thought he had simply stopped being frightened of his own accord, the way the weather clears, and that was exactly the shape Harry had built it to have, because a confidence you think you found yourself is worth ten that somebody handed you.

Dudley stood up at the end of term and got to twelve twelves with one wobble, and came home two feet taller than he’d left, and told the whole table that tables were easy, really, you just had to not be thick about it, and Vernon beamed, and Petunia did a steak-and-kidney to mark the occasion, and looked, once, across the table at Harry, with the face with the wall out of it, and said nothing, and he understood that she had seen every tennis ball.

Underneath the table, in the country of one, he turned the whole operation over and found it good, and found also the thing it had taught him, which was bigger than Dudley and bigger than tables and which he would spend the rest of his life proving out:

You almost never move a person by the front door. You find the door they already use. Dudley through football. Petunia through a cup of tea. Vernon through the Rotary. Mrs Prentice through a held-open door, and Mrs Figg through a mended cat, and Carrow through a mistake on page nine. Everyone he had ever managed, he had managed sideways, through the thing they already loved, and it had never once failed, and the one time in his life he had gone through the front door unasked — behind the cooker, Aunt Petunia — he had frightened a woman into five years of cold.

The thing in him answered caring, and people answered the same way. It was the same law twice, he thought, lying under the stairs that night with the swing on the wall and a good dinner in him for once. You could not make either come by force. You went to where it already lived, and you asked properly, and you were patient, and then — cat’s hips, boy’s tables, warmth in a blanket on a cold step — it came.

He wrote it under On Holding, because it belonged there. The front-door law. Same as the other. Hold the sentence; ask properly; the thing comes.

He turned eight on the last day of July, and nobody at Number Four marked it, which was the custom, but when he came back from Egham that evening there was a parcel on his camp bed in brown paper with no name on it, and inside was a blue fountain pen — a good one, old, with a lever — and a card that said only for the pads. E.P., and he sat on the bed and held it for a long time by his own clock, and then he wrote the date on the next clean page of the twenty-ninth pad with it, and under the date, three years.

Then he took stock, because Carrow had taught him that you took stock before you went on, and he did it in English, in the new pen, for the reader he hadn’t met.

Eight. Well over a thousand of mine.

Read: the branch, the fourth and fifth floors, the journals to 1960 in maths and physics, all seven grades and forty of their books. The runes are the lever. The law has a footnote about a Trace and it has never once written to this house.

Can: hold. That is the whole of it and it is enough. A feather that stays. A hundred and sixty brackets and forty of my own. Six sentences written cold, each costing like a hill. Nothing for myself; the door faces out; I have stopped minding.

Have: a paper. A professor with a knife for a face and another with a room. A woman at a desk who says not like that. A squib who spends scone money and a man in Scotland who pays the bills and tells me which chapter. An aunt who made me laugh. A swing.

Have not: a telephone. Three years.

He looked at that last line for a while. Then he turned to the inside of the cupboard door, where the date was pinned at the height of his eyes and the swing beside it, and under the date, under Faster, in the new blue pen, small, he wrote the thing that the year had taught him and that he would carry up the drive in three years like a lamp:

They shout. I can hold. Find out what that is worth.

Then he went down to the lounge in his socks, because it was ten past one, and a man in a brown jacket on BBC2 was about to explain thermodynamics to nobody, and Harry Potter, eight years old and the second author of a paper in a journal older than his grandparents, sat on the carpet eighteen inches from the screen with the sound at two and the blue notebook on his knee, and listened, and compared.
