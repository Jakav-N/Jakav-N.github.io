Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 12

What Is Owed

The two newspapers lay side by side on the camp bed on a Tuesday night in October, and he had put them there on purpose, and he looked at them for a long time before he let himself understand why.

The one on the left was the Daily Prophet, Monday’s, bought by Mrs Figg at the pub on the way to the third trip and read by him on the train home in eleven minutes. Page four, lower half, a piece about St Mungo’s Hospital for Magical Maladies and Injuries — he had known the name since the law book; he had not known the place, the wards, the Healers in lime-green — and a woman who had been brought in on the Friday with her spine broken in three places from a fall off a broom, and had walked out on the Sunday. Walked. The piece was not about her. It was about a dispute over the hospital’s funding, and she was the example, mentioned in passing, the way the time of the last bus is mentioned, of course.

The one on the right was the Telegraph, Vernon’s, Saturday’s, rescued from the bin. Page seven. An appeal, with a photograph, for a hospital in London that mended children, and a girl of six in the photograph with no hair, and three paragraphs about what the money would be for, and the word spine in the second paragraph, and the word months.

He had put them side by side because he had been not putting them side by side for a year, and he had caught himself at it, and the catching had the taste of the cupboard week.

Two hospitals. Eleven miles apart, roughly — he had worked out where St Mungo’s must be from a hint in the law book and a shop that had always seemed to be closed. One of them mended a spine in three places in a weekend and mentioned it in passing. The other put a girl’s photograph in the paper and asked the public for money and said months.

He had known about the curtain since he was three. He had read the law that kept it up — the Statute, the whole dry hundred pages of it, with its reasons given in the preamble in the careful prose of people who had been frightened once and had written it down so as not to forget. He had understood the reasons. Some of them, reading them under the stairs at seven, he had even agreed with. He had not, until a Tuesday night in October with two newspapers on a blanket, let himself put a face on the far side of them.

The girl in the photograph had a face. She had no hair and a brave smile that somebody had asked her for, and she was six, and eleven miles away there were people in lime-green who did spines in a weekend.

He lay down with the two papers still on the bed and did not sleep, and that was not unusual; but he did not go down to the television either, and that was.

He found the essay on the Thursday, on the fourth floor, in the philosophy, which he had been working through in order since the summer because he had come to the end of the mathematics journals he could follow without Carrow, and had decided that a person who was going to spend his life deciding what to mend ought to know what the people who had thought about that for a living had concluded.

It was in a collection bound in green, a thin thing by an Australian, written before Harry was born, and it began with a famine, and then it put a child in a pond.

He read it on the train. He read everything on the train. But a third of the way down the second page he did a thing he had never done with any piece of writing in his life: he stopped. Not because he didn’t understand it — the argument was short and clean and he had it whole in the time it took to read — but because it was not the kind of page you understood. It was the kind you answered. And he sat with the green book open on his knee and Egham going by in the dark and Mrs Figg beside him with a castle, and found that he did not have an answer, and that he had been living for seven years as if he did.

The argument was this, and he would never afterwards be able to make it sound as plain as it had been on the page. If you walked past a shallow pond and saw a child drowning in it, you would wade in and pull the child out, and you would do it even if it ruined your shoes, and anyone who did not would be a monster, and nobody disagreed about that. And the man then said, very quietly, as if sorry to have to mention it: distance makes no difference to the drowning. Nor does the number of other people walking past. Nor does the fact that the child is in a pond in a country you have never been to and the ruining of your shoes takes the form of a cheque. If you can save a life at a cost that is nothing like a life, and you don’t, the pond does not care how far away it was.

That was all. Four pages. Harry had read something over ten thousand books and none of them had ever made him feel as if he had been caught doing something.

He got off at Little Whinging and walked home with Mrs Figg beside him not asking, and the stone in his shoe from the two newspapers, and his whole ledger — seven years of it, every careful entry, every honest cost — open in front of him in the long quiet, and he went back through it looking for the place where he had asked the Australian’s question of himself, and could not find one.

He had asked how do I get out. He had asked how do I get more. He had asked, every night for seven years, what can I learn, what can I build, how do I get to the shelves — and all of it had been true and good and he did not regret a line. But there was a thing he had never asked, and it had taken a girl in a photograph and a thin green book to put its hand up, and the thing was:

You have two hundred years for every one. What do you owe for that?

The university closed for Christmas on the sixteenth, and Mrs Figg went to her sister-in-law’s in Deal for the week, and Vernon took the family to Marge’s, and Harry — by an arrangement that had taken Petunia one sentence to make and Vernon four seconds to accept, he’ll be better at home with his books than underfoot at Marge’s, and Mrs Next Door will look in — had the house.

Seven days. The door of the cupboard open, this time, and the whole house ticking round him, and nothing to do but the thing he had been not doing since October. Seven days, which by his own clock was most of four years, and he spent them at the kitchen table, where the light was best, with the green book and the two newspapers and a fresh pad, in English, because he had the strong feeling going in that this one was going to be read.

The first day he spent on the obligation, and found he could not get out from under it, and stopped trying.

He had the thing. Whatever it was, wherever it had come from — the green light, the scar, a wheel spun on a doorstep — he had it, and nobody else did, and he had not earned it any more than Dudley had earned his appetite. Two hundred to one. More than a thousand years so far of thought that nobody else on earth had had time for. If that was not a man standing beside a pond with the best pair of shoes in the world, he did not know what the phrase could mean. The Australian had asked people to give up a cinema ticket. Harry had been given time itself, more of it than anyone in history, and had spent the great bulk of it on himself, and he had been right to, in the sense that a child must grow before he can carry, and he had been wrong to, in the sense that he had never once looked up from the growing to ask who was in the pond.

He wrote that down. He let it stand.

The second day he spent on the objection, because an argument that did not stand up to its own best objection was just a feeling in a coat, and the objection was good: you are eight. Eight, with a trust he couldn’t touch and a season ticket and a feather that held. What, exactly, was he to do? Wade into which pond? He could straighten an old woman’s fingers. He could not straighten a famine. He could give the chess money to the people who put the children on the posters, and he would — he decided that on the second day and never went back on it — but the chess money was two hundred pounds, and the Australian had been very clear that the drowning did not care how good you felt about the cheque.

So he made himself look at it the way he looked at a problem Carrow set: not what can I do today, which was the first-year’s question, but where is the floor. Where, in the whole of what he could see from a kitchen table in Surrey, was the one place a boy with two hundred years for every one could put his weight and have the world move?

And he found, on the third day, with the rain going and the boiler whooshing and the two newspapers face-up on the table, that he already knew. He had known since a kitchen floor at Mrs Figg’s. He had known since two newspapers on a blanket. He had simply never put it in the same sentence as the pond.

An old cat had got up and gone upstairs. An old woman’s fingers had come straight. A rose had gone green from the bottom up. He had done that with no teacher, no wand, no word, by minding, at six, on his knees — and eleven miles from a girl with no hair there was a building full of people who did spines in a weekend. Trained men and women. In lime-green. With a telephone each.

He sat with that for a long time by any clock.

He did not know why. He wrote that down first, in capitals, because the thing in him that Carrow had been training for two years insisted on it: I DO NOT KNOW WHY. He had read the Statute. He knew the reasons in the preamble, the burnings and the fear, and some of them were good reasons, and he had agreed with them under the stairs at seven. He did not know what St Mungo’s could and couldn’t do; a spine was not a famine; perhaps there was a floor under their mending the way there was a floor under his, and the floor was lower than two newspapers made it look. He would find out. He would find out before he judged anyone, because he had been judged from a doorstep by people who thought they knew what he was and he had not enjoyed it.

But he knew what he owed, and it was not a cheque.

It was this. Behind a curtain there was a whole science of mending, and a school that taught it, and a hospital that practised it, and he had been born into it with a gift for reading its sentences that nobody else alive seemed to have — Mrs Figg had said so, in so many words, in a café: I have never seen a wizard do magic that way — and the letter was coming in two years and seven months. He was going to walk into that school. He was going to learn everything in it. He was going to find out what the thing could mend and what it couldn’t, and why, and if the why turned out to be a bad reason — if the curtain was there for comfort, or habit, or a fear three hundred years stale, and a girl of six had been put in a newspaper for want of someone in lime-green minding about her properly — then he was going to spend the rest of two hundred years for every one pulling the curtain down.

That was the floor. He had found it. He wrote it on the fourth day, in the round hand, for whoever would read it:

I owe the pond everything I have. What I have is time, and a gift for reading the sentences, and two years and seven months. The biggest lever I can reach is not maths, or money, or chess. It is the thing in me, and the world that knows how to use it, and the question of why that world has St Mungo’s and mine has a photograph on page seven. I am going to go and learn the answer. Then I am going to act on it.

Everything else is shoes.

He asked Mrs Figg on the first Saturday in January, in the garden, with the cats under blankets on the bench, because she had been to St Mungo’s and he had not, and because he had resolved on the third day not to judge before he knew.

“Mrs Figg. The hospital. Yours. What can they do?”

She did not ask which hospital. She had stopped asking which anything.

“A great deal, dear.” She snipped a dead stem off something that had given up in November. “Bones in an afternoon. Burns. Most poisons. Things that would kill a Muggle in a week, they send you home with a potion and a leaflet.” Snip. “My brother Edgar broke his back off a roof in 1963 and was walking by the Wednesday.”

“And the other brother?”

The secateurs paused.

“Amos,” said Mrs Figg. “He had a thing in his blood. They didn’t have a name for it; your side does, I found out after, it was in the paper that very year, some professor in America. They tried. They tried for a year, and they were kind, and he died in a bed in St Mungo’s in 1972 and the Healer said to me, we can mend what is broken, Arabella; this was not broken; this was wrong.” She looked at the dead stem in her hand. “Your side had a word for it. Nobody on mine had ever heard the word.”

Harry stood very still in the January garden.

“They can’t do everything,” he said.

“Nobody can do everything. They can do things yours can’t and yours can do things they can’t, and the two of them have never once sat down at a table, as far as I know, and I have wondered why for sixteen years and never found anyone to ask.” She dropped the stem in the trug and looked at him sideways, with the gladness in it and something else, something that had been getting steadier all year. “I expect you’ll ask.”

“Yes,” said Harry. “I expect I will.”

He walked home the long way, past the recreation ground, in the cold, and the thing he had found on the third day had a shape now that it had not had at the kitchen table. It was not that one side had the mending and the other had the photograph. It was that each side had half. A thing in the blood with a name on one side and no name on the other; a spine in a weekend on one side and months on the other; and a curtain between them three hundred years thick, and nobody — Mrs Figg, who had buried a brother, had wondered for sixteen years and found nobody to ask — nobody had ever sat the two halves down at a table.

He had read both sides. He was, as far as he could tell, the only person alive who had. Fifteen papers and a university on one; seven grades and forty books and a feather that held on the other. He was eight years old and he was, by an accident of a doorstep and a cupboard and a scone jar, the table.

He wrote that down that night, and underlined it, which he almost never did.

Each side has half. Nobody has sat them down. I have read both. I am the table.

He gave the money on the Monday, with Mrs Prentice, in her good coat, who had insisted on coming.

Not all of it. He had thought about all of it for most of a morning and decided against, and written down why, because the green book had taught him that the deciding was where people lied to themselves and he was not going to. There was two hundred and thirty pounds in the toffee tin — the crossword had kept paying, and the chess had started to, four tournaments that autumn with envelopes at the end, the last of them against adults in Guildford who had not enjoyed it — and he was going to need some of it. Not for himself. For the far side; there were books still, and things that were not books, and Mrs Figg’s scone money was Mrs Figg’s and he had decided in October that he would not spend it again. That was not shoes. That was the lever. He kept half.

The other half he took to the Oxfam shop on the high street in an envelope, and a woman with a badge opened it and counted a hundred and fifteen pounds in notes and coins and looked at the eight-year-old in the school jumper and said, “Is this — are you sure, love?”

“Yes,” said Harry. “It’s for the hospital. The one in the paper, for children. Could you put it towards that, please?”

The woman looked at Mrs Prentice.

“He’s sure,” said Mrs Prentice.

They walked back to the library in the thin January sun with nothing said for most of the way, which was how the two of them did their best talking, and at the door she stopped and looked down at him over the glasses.

“Half,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Not all.”

“No. I need the rest for something. I’ve written down what.” He hesitated, and then, because it was her: “I read a book that said you should give until it costs you something that matters. I think it was right. I think it was also written for people who’ve already got their shoes. I haven’t got mine yet. I need them to get to the pond at all.”

Mrs Prentice considered him for a long moment in the doorway, with the smell of polish coming out past her.

“My husband,” she said, “used to say that the trouble with saints was they were no use to anyone till they’d finished being saints.” She pushed the door open. “Half is right, dear. For now. Come and do the crossword. There’s a nine in the bottom corner that wants to be obligation and isn’t.”

He asked Carrow on the Thursday, because he had been wanting to ask someone who wasn’t eight and wasn’t kind, and she was the only person he knew who qualified on both counts.

“Professor. What’s mathematics for?”

She did not look up from pad thirty-one, which she was reading with the pencil, and he had learned to wait.

“For itself, mostly,” she said at last. “Which is a perfectly respectable thing for a thing to be for; I shan’t have it apologised for. Occasionally it turns out to be for bridges, two hundred years later, by accident, and everyone acts very surprised.” She turned a page. “Why?”

“I read a book. About a child in a pond.”

“Ah.” She did look up then. “The Australian. Yes. I read it at your — well. At twenty. It gave me a bad month.” She took the glasses off. “And now you want to know whether a person is allowed to sit in a room doing group theory while the child drowns.”

“Yes.”

“Then I shall tell you what I decided, which you may take or leave. I decided that I was no earthly use to the child. I can’t swim, I faint at blood, and I was already far better at this than at anything else I was ever going to be good at. So I resolved to be very good at this, and to give what I could spare, and to make sure the people I taught understood what they were for, which is more than my own teachers managed.” She put the glasses back on. “And I resolved not to pretend that was the same as wading in. It isn’t. It’s what I had. You put your weight where you’ve got weight, Potter. The man in the pond doesn’t need a geometer. He needs the person who happens to be standing there and happens to be able to swim.” A pause, and the knife-face did its small thing at one corner. “If you ever find you’re standing there and you can swim, you won’t need a book to tell you what to do. You’ll already be wet.”

He thought about a kitchen floor, and a pair of hands, and a hospital eleven miles from a photograph, and a woman in a garden who had buried a brother and found nobody to ask.

“Yes,” he said. “I think I might already be.”

Carrow looked at him for a moment longer than the remark warranted, and then decided, as she always did, to stand a little to one side of the door.

“Pad thirty-one,” she said. “Page sixty. You’ve dropped a sign.”

He drafted the List on the last night of the Christmas week, before the family came back from Marge’s and the house filled up again, at the kitchen table, in English, in the blue pen E.P. had given him, on a clean pad of its own, and he gave it a plain title because the plain ones lasted, and the title was Things Worth a Life.

He did not write it fast. He wrote each line, and sat with it, and some of them he crossed out and wrote again, because he was writing it for the reader he hadn’t met and for himself at fifty and he wanted both of them to be able to see what he had meant at eight.

1. Learn what the thing is. Properly, all the way down. Not what it does — what it IS. Nobody on the far side has done this, or Mrs F would not say what she says and the grey book would not be thin. I can read the sentences. Read all of them. Then carve them.

2. Find out what each side can mend and what it can’t, and why, and sit them down at a table. Amos Figg had a thing in his blood that my side had a name for and his side had never heard of. A girl on page seven has a spine that his side does in a weekend. Nobody has ever put the two in one room. Be the room.

3. Death. (He wrote the word and looked at it for a very long time and did not cross it out.) Not for me. For Amos. For the girl. If the thing can mend a hip it can mend more than a hip, and if there is a floor I want to see it with my own eyes before I believe in it.

4. Pain. Mrs F’s hands for forty years. Edgar’s back off a roof. How much of that is there in the world, on both sides, that nobody is minding about?

5. Waste. People in cupboards. Not me — I was lucky; I had the cupboard and the time. The ones who have the cupboard and don’t have the time. Somewhere there is a child at a kitchen table that nobody can see. Be the one who sees.

6. The curtain. Find out why it is there. Read the preamble again. Then decide.

7. 141. (He had kept the page from the blue book about the even numbers and two primes since he was six; he wrote the number and left it.) Not a duty. Not everything on a list of what a life is worth has to be something you owe. Some of it is why you’d want the life.

He read it over twice. Then at the bottom, smaller, because it was not a thing worth a life but a thing he wanted to remember having understood at eight, he wrote the last line.

The door faces outward. I could not mend my own eyes. I used to think that was a limit. I think now it’s the design. Whatever this is, it was never meant for the one holding it. That’s the right way round. Keep it that way.

He put the pen down. The house ticked. Somewhere past the curtain there were people in lime-green who did spines in a weekend, and a bed where a man had died of a thing with a name his Healer had never heard; and on this side there was a photograph on page seven, and a woman in Kent who had written to a newspaper to ask whether a baby was safe; and a letter coming in two years and seven months; and a telephone at the end of it.

He turned the light off. He did not go down to the television. He lay under the stairs, eight and a half, well over a thousand, with the List face-up on the blanket and the swing on the wall and the date beside it, and for the first time since the doorstep he was not thinking about what he could learn.

He was thinking about who was in the pond, and how many, and how fast.
