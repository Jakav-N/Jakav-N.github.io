Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 13

What Is Owed

Mrs Oduya stopped coming to the bus stop in the second week of January, and it was three weeks before he found out why, and the finding out changed the shape of his life.

He noticed at once, of course. He noticed everything; it was the whole trouble with him. The hat with the cherry was not under the shelter on the Tuesday, nor the Thursday, nor the Tuesday after, and he stood in the frost with his hand in his pocket where it had got into the habit of resting against a flask that wasn’t there, and told himself she had a cold, and knew he was telling himself.

It was Mrs Figg who told him, in the end, because Mrs Figg knew everyone on that side of Little Whinging who was old, and because he asked.

“Oduya,” she said. “Cherry hat. Yes. Her husband, dear. He’d been in the General since the summer before last. Stroke, and then the other things that come after a stroke. She sat with him every day they’d let her.” She looked at Harry over the teapot. “He went just after New Year. She’s gone to her daughter’s in Leicester. I shouldn’t think she’ll be back.”

Harry sat on the good end of the settee with a chocolate biscuit he had stopped eating and looked at the cat.

A year. He had stood beside her for a year of Tuesdays and Thursdays, warming a flask so that a woman could sit a little longer beside a man he had never seen, and the man had been eleven miles away in a bed in the General, and now he was gone, and the hat was in Leicester, and Harry had never once in that year asked himself what the sitting was for. He had built the purpose honestly — he had, he had done it properly, so that whoever she sits with gets a little more of her — and he had never followed the sentence to its end. He had warmed the tea. He had not gone to the hospital.

Could he have done anything? He did not know. That was the part that sat in him like a stone in a shoe all the way home. He did not know what a stroke was, in the sense that mattered — not the word, he had the word, he had every word — but whether it was the kind of wrong that the thing in him would go looking for and find. He had never tried anything larger than a pair of hands. He had never been in a room with a person who was dying. He had spent a year standing at a bus stop next to the one door in Little Whinging that would have let him into that room, and he had warmed a flask.

That was the stone. Not guilt; he was careful about guilt, he had learned early that it was a feeling that liked to be looked at and did no work. Something more useful than guilt. A question with its hand up.

What are you for?

He found the essay on the Thursday, in the copper-roofed library, on the third floor, in the philosophy, which he had been working through in order since Christmas because he had come to the end of the mathematics journals he could follow without Carrow and had decided that a person who was going to spend his life deciding what to mend ought to know what the people who thought about that for a living had concluded.

It was in a collection, bound in green, a thin thing by a man he had never heard of, written before Harry was born, and it began with a famine, and then it put a child in a pond.

He read it on the train. He read everything on the train. But a third of the way down the second page he did a thing he had never done with any piece of writing in his life: he stopped. Not because he didn’t understand it — the argument was short and clean and he had it whole in the time it took to read, the way he had everything — but because it was not the kind of page you understood. It was the kind you answered. And he sat with the green book open on his knee and Egham going by in the dark and found that he did not have an answer, and that he had been living for nine years as if he did.

The argument was this, and he would never afterwards be able to make it sound as plain as it had been on the page. If you walked past a shallow pond and saw a child drowning in it, you would wade in and pull the child out, and you would do it even if it ruined your shoes, and anyone who did not would be a monster, and nobody disagreed about that. And the man then said, very quietly, as if he were sorry to have to mention it: distance makes no difference to the drowning. Nor does the number of other people walking past. Nor does the fact that the child is in a pond in a country you have never been to and the ruining of your shoes takes the form of a cheque. If you can save a life at a cost that is nothing like a life, and you don’t, the pond does not care how far away it was.

That was all. It took four pages. Harry had read ten thousand books and none of them had ever made him feel as if he had been caught doing something.

He got off at Little Whinging and walked home through the dark with the book in his bag and the stone in his shoe and his whole ledger — nine years of it, every careful entry, every honest cost — open in front of him in the long quiet, and he went back through it looking for the place where he had asked the man’s question of himself, and he could not find one.

He had asked how do I get out. He had asked how do I get more. He had asked, every night for nine years, what can I learn, what can I build, how do I get to the shelves — and all of it had been true and good and he did not regret a line. But there was a thing he had never asked, and it had taken a dead man in a hospital bed and a thin green book to put its hand up, and the thing was:

You have two hundred years for every one. What do you owe for that?

It rained the whole of the February half-term, which was exactly what he needed, and he spent it in the cupboard with the notebooks and the green book and the door shut, and he argued with himself for a week, which by his own clock was most of four years, and he kept the argument in English in a pad of its own because he had the strong feeling, going in, that this one was going to be read.

The first day he spent on the obligation, and found he could not get out from under it, and stopped trying.

He had the thing. Whatever it was, wherever it had come from — the green light, the scar, whatever had spun the wheel — he had it, and nobody else did, and it was not a thing he had earned any more than Dudley had earned his appetite. Two hundred to one. Twelve hundred years, so far, of thought that nobody else on earth had had time for. If that was not a man standing beside a pond with the best pair of shoes in the world, he did not know what the phrase could mean. The man in the green book had asked people to give up a cinema ticket. Harry had been given time itself, more of it than anyone in history, and had spent the great bulk of it on himself, and he had been right to, in the sense that a child must grow before he can carry, and he had been wrong to, in the sense that he had never once looked up from the growing to ask who was in the pond.

He wrote that down. He let it stand.

The second day he spent on the objection, because an argument that did not stand up to its own best objection was just a feeling in a coat, and the objection was good: you are nine. Nine, with a trust he couldn’t touch and a season ticket and a pencil he could move an inch. What, exactly, was he to do? Wade into which pond? He could straighten an old woman’s fingers. He could not straighten a famine. He could give the chess money to the people who put the children on the posters, and he would — he decided that on the second day and never went back on it — but the chess money was two hundred pounds, and the man in the green book had been very clear that the drowning did not care how good you felt about the cheque.

So he made himself look at it the way he looked at a problem Carrow set: not what can I do today, which was the first-year’s question, the wish-and-a-wand question, but where is the floor. Where, in the whole of what he could see from a cupboard in Surrey, was the one place a boy with two hundred years for every one could put his weight and have the world move?

And he found, on the third day, with the rain going and the boiler whooshing and the green book face-down on the camp bed, that he already knew. He had known since a kitchen floor. He had simply never put it in the same sentence as the pond.

An old cat had got up and gone upstairs. An old woman’s fingers had come straight. A rose had gone green from the bottom up. He had done that with no teacher, no wand, no word for it, by minding, at seven, on his knees — and there were, Mrs Figg said, thousands of them. Thousands of people who could do what he did, with schools, with a library whose books bit, with — she had let it slip in the garden, in October, between the geraniums — a hospital. A hospital. Trained men and women who could do what he did to a cat’s hips, but properly, for years, to people, in a building built for it.

And on this side of the curtain, eleven miles away, a man had lain in the General for eighteen months after a stroke while his wife drank cold tea beside him, and died.

He sat with that for a long time by any clock.

He did not know why. He wrote that down first, in capitals, because the thing in him that had been trained by Carrow for a term insisted on it: I DO NOT KNOW WHY. Perhaps they could not. Perhaps strokes were beyond them as the boy at the back of the sports hall had been beyond him; perhaps the thing had a floor, and the floor was lower than he hoped. Perhaps there were reasons for the curtain that were good, that a nine-year-old could not see from a cupboard — he could think of some; he could think of a dozen, and some of them frightened him. He would find out. He would find out before he judged anyone, because he had been judged from a doorstep by people who thought they knew what he was and he had not enjoyed it.

But he knew what he owed, and it was not a cheque.

It was this. Somewhere behind a curtain there was a whole science of mending, and a school that taught it, and a hospital that practised it, and he had been born into it with a gift for reading its sentences that nobody else alive seemed to have — Mrs Figg had said so, in so many words: I have never seen a wizard do magic that way — and the letter was coming in eighteen months. He was going to walk into that school. He was going to learn everything in it. He was going to find out what the thing could mend and what it couldn’t, and why, and if the why turned out to be a bad reason — if it turned out that the curtain was there for comfort, or habit, or fear, and a man had died in the General for want of someone minding about him properly — then he was going to spend the rest of two hundred years for every one pulling the curtain down.

That was the floor. He had found it. He wrote it on the fourth day, in the round hand, for whoever would read it:

I owe the pond everything I have. What I have is time, and a gift for reading the sentences, and eighteen months. The biggest lever I can reach is not maths, or money, or chess. It is the thing in me, and the world that knows how to use it, and the question of why that world has a hospital and mine has the General. I am going to go and learn the answer. Then I am going to act on it.

Everything else is shoes.

He gave the money on the Monday, when the rain stopped.

Not all of it. He thought about all of it, for most of a morning, and decided against it, and wrote down why, because the green book had taught him that the deciding was where people lied to themselves and he was not going to. There was two hundred and twelve pounds in Mrs Prentice’s toffee tin — the crossword had kept paying, and the chess had started to, three tournaments that autumn with envelopes at the end — and he was going to need some of it. Not for himself. For the far side. There was a bank there, Mrs Figg had said; there were shops; and he had a strong and growing intention, which he had not yet written down anywhere, of walking into one of those shops before anyone sent for him, with money in his pocket, and finding out what the books cost. That was not shoes. That was the lever. He kept half.

The other half he took to the Oxfam shop on the high street in an envelope, with Mrs Prentice, who had insisted on coming, in her good coat, and who stood a little behind him at the counter while a woman with a badge opened the envelope and counted a hundred and six pounds in notes and coins and looked at the nine-year-old in the school jumper and said, “Is this — are you sure, love?”

“Yes,” said Harry. “It’s for the famine. The one in the posters. Could you put it towards that, please?”

The woman looked at Mrs Prentice.

“He’s sure,” said Mrs Prentice.

They walked back to the library in the thin February sun with nothing said for most of the way, which was how the two of them did their best talking, and at the door she stopped and looked down at him over the glasses.

“Half,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Not all.”

“No. I need the rest for something. I’ve written down what.” He hesitated, and then, because it was her: “I read a book that said you should give until it costs you something that matters. I think it was right. I think it was also written for people who’ve already got their shoes. I haven’t got mine yet. I’m going to need them to get to the pond at all.”

Mrs Prentice considered him for a long moment in the doorway, with the smell of polish coming out past her.

“My husband,” she said, “used to say that the trouble with saints was they were no use to anyone till they’d finished being saints.” She pushed the door open. “Half is right, dear. For now. Come and do the crossword. There’s a nine letters in the bottom corner that wants to be obligation and isn’t.”

He asked Carrow on the Thursday, because he had been wanting to ask someone who wasn’t nine and wasn’t kind, and she was the only person he knew who qualified on both counts.

“Professor. What’s mathematics for?”

She did not look up from pad nineteen, which she was reading with the pencil, and he had learned to wait.

“For itself, mostly,” she said at last. “Which is a perfectly respectable thing for a thing to be for; I shan’t have it apologised for. Occasionally it turns out to be for bridges, two hundred years later, by accident, and everyone acts very surprised.” She turned a page. “Why?”

“I read a book. About a child in a pond.”

“Ah.” She did look up then. “The Australian. Yes. I read it at your age — well. At twenty. It gave me a bad month.” She took the glasses off. “And now you want to know whether a person is allowed to sit in a room doing group theory while the child drowns.”

“Yes.”

“Then I shall tell you what I decided, which you may take or leave. I decided that I was no earthly use to the child. I can’t swim, I faint at blood, and I was, at twenty, already far better at this than at anything else I was ever going to be good at. So I resolved to be very good at this, and to give what I could spare, and to make sure the people I taught understood what they were for, which is more than my own teachers managed.” She put the glasses back on. “And I resolved not to pretend that was the same as wading in. It isn’t. It’s what I had. You put your weight where you’ve got weight, Harry. The man in the pond doesn’t need a geometer. He needs the person who happens to be standing there and happens to be able to swim.” A pause, and the knife-face did its small thing at one corner. “If you ever find you’re standing there and you can swim, you won’t need a book to tell you what to do. You’ll already be wet.”

He thought about a kitchen floor, and a pair of hands, and a hospital eleven miles away.

“Yes,” he said. “I think I might already be.”

Carrow looked at him for a moment longer than the remark warranted, and then decided, as she always did, to stand a little to one side of the door.

“Pad nineteen,” she said. “Page sixty. You’ve dropped a sign.”

He drafted the List on the last night of half-term, in English, in the blue pen, on a clean pad of its own, and he gave it a plain title because the plain ones lasted, and the title was Things Worth a Life.

He did not write it fast. He wrote each line, and sat with it, and some of them he crossed out and wrote again, because he was writing it for the reader he hadn’t met and for himself at fifty and he wanted both of them to be able to see what he had meant at nine.

1. Learn what the thing is. Properly, all the way down. Not what it does — what it IS. Nobody on the far side seems to have done this, or Mrs F would not say what she says. I can read the sentences. Read all of them.

2. Find out what it can mend and what it can’t, and why. Then find out why the people who can mend keep it behind a curtain while a man dies in the General. Do not judge them before I know. Then judge.

3. Death. (He wrote the word and looked at it for a very long time and did not cross it out.) Not for me. For the hat in Leicester. For whoever is in the pond. If the thing can mend a hip it can mend more than a hip, and if there is a floor I want to see it with my own eyes before I believe in it.

4. Pain. Mrs F’s hands for forty years. How much of that is there in the world, that nobody is minding about?

5. Waste. People in cupboards. Not me — I was lucky; I had the cupboard and the time. The ones who have the cupboard and don’t have the time. Somewhere there is a child at a kitchen table that nobody can see. Be the one who sees.

6. 141. (Not a duty. I’m keeping it anyway. Not everything on a list of what a life is worth has to be something you owe. Some of it is why you’d want the life.)

He read it over twice. Then at the bottom, smaller, because it was not a thing worth a life but a thing he wanted to remember having understood at nine, he wrote the last line.

The door faces outward. I could not mend my own eyes. I used to think that was a limit. I think now it’s the design. Whatever this is, it was never meant for the one holding it. That’s the right way round. Keep it that way.

He put the pen down. The house ticked. Somewhere past the curtain there were thousands of people who could do what he did, and a school, and a hospital, and a letter coming in seventeen months, and a bank; and on this side there was a woman in Leicester with a cherry on her hat who would never know that a boy had warmed her tea for a year and had only now, too late for one man in the General, worked out what the warming had been for.

He turned the light off. He did not go down to the television. He lay in the dark under the stairs, nine and a half, sixteen hundred and some, with the List face-up on the blanket beside him, and for the first time since the doorstep he was not thinking about what he could learn.

He was thinking about where the shops were.
