Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 14

Interlude: Receipts

The Headmaster’s office, Hogwarts, a Monday evening in late July 1989

“He has a degree, Albus.”

“So I see.”

“He is eight. He was eight on the day. He is nine now, Arabella says, as of Monday, and she says it as if the extra year were a thing to be grateful for.” Minerva McGonagall put the cutting down on the desk, on top of the others, which had been accumulating in a wooden tray since the spring of 1986 and had lately needed a second tray. BOY, 8, TAKES FIRST-CLASS DEGREE. The photograph was a good one; they had got his eyes, and the way he held the scroll, with both hands, as if carrying it for someone else. “A first. Top of the year. There is a second young man of twenty-two who has, I gather, been told. Minerva McGonagall said in February of 1987 that she would look at that child with her own eyes, and she would like it noted that she has now waited two and a half years.”

“Noted,” said Dumbledore.

He had the cuttings, and the receipts. The receipts were in a third tray, and they were the thing in the office he found himself looking at most often, late, when the castle was quiet, and he could not have said why until Minerva had put her finger on it, two trips ago, with the particular accuracy she reserved for his failings: you are learning what he is from Arabella’s shopping lists, Albus, because you have forbidden yourself every other way of learning it.

It was true. Three trips to Diagon Alley now, at seven, at seven and a half, at eight, each one itemised in Arabella’s freed right hand and sent to him with a note that grew drier each time, and he had paid each one by return owl without comment and then sat with the list for an evening and read it the way he had once read the movements of a Dark wizard across Europe: for the shape of the mind behind it.

The first list had been a child’s. All seven Standard Books, which was greed, and Waffling, which was sense, and the two histories, and a Prophet, and then — the one item on the first list that had made him put down his tea — the Blishwick monograph. On the Composition of Charms. 1911. He had not known Flourish and Blotts still had a copy. He had not known anyone alive but himself had read it.

The second list had been a scholar’s. Advanced transfiguration, runes, potions, the Ministry, the law — the actual statutes, which no child had ever voluntarily bought in the history of the shop — and a bound year of the Prophet, and 1981 of all years, and The Theory of Enchantment, which was the best book on its subject written in the century and which perhaps forty people had read, and he had written on the receipt, before he could stop himself, tell him to read chapter nine twice, and had then sat for a long time wondering why he had done it, and had concluded that it was because he had wanted, very badly, to have said one thing to the boy, after eight years, even if the thing was about a chapter.

The third list had frightened him a little, and he had not told Minerva so.

The third list had been specific. Not books about magic; books about particular problems. Two monographs on the structure of wards. A treatise on Healing that was not on any syllabus and that St Mungo’s used to train Healers. A history of the Statute of Secrecy — not Hogwarts: A History’s chapter on it, the full thing, with the preamble and the debates and the dissenting pamphlets of 1692. A thin book about Azkaban, long out of print, that Dumbledore had thought he owned the only copy of. And four back numbers of a journal on magical theory that was read, as far as he knew, by eleven people, of whom he was one.

A child of eight did not buy those books to learn magic. A child of eight bought those books because he had already learned the magic that was in the ordinary ones, and had looked up from them, and had begun to ask what it was for.

“Albus.”

“Yes.”

“You have gone somewhere.”

“I have gone to the third receipt, Minerva. I go there rather often.” He turned from the window. “Sit down. I have asked Filius to come up, and I should like you to hear what he says, because I have been hearing it in my head for two years and I am not sure any more whether I am hearing him or myself.”

Filius Flitwick came up at eight, with a cup of something, and sat in the chair that had been made the right height for him in 1971 and listened, with his head on one side, while Dumbledore read aloud from a letter in a squib’s freed hand.

He does not tell it anything. He sits on the floor and is sorry for a cat until the cat gets up. He told me, in a café, that it answers caring, and that the broken things are only easier because they tell you what is wrong, and then he warmed my teapot so that I should see.

And then, from a later letter:

He says he can see the sentences under the words. He says it is like being handed a dictionary for a language he has been reading off roses. He says they shout and he can hold.

Flitwick did not say anything for a while. He had put the cup down.

“Albus,” he said eventually, in the small precise voice that had taught four decades of first-years to lift a feather, “that is not a charm.”

“No?”

“No. A charm is a thing you do. You have the form, and you have the focus, and you have the will, and you put the three together in the moment and the thing is done. Every book I have ever taught from says so and every student I have ever taught has learned it so, including you, including me.” He picked the cup up again and did not drink from it. “What that woman is describing is a thing you ask. It has the shape of a prayer. It has — forgive me — the shape of the thing we tell first-years they are not doing when they first get it right, because if they thought they were asking they would start to wonder what they were asking of, and that way lies a century of theology and no feathers.” He looked at the letter. “And he says he can see it. The form. In the moment. Albus, nobody can see the form in the moment. The moment is too short. That is why there are incantations; the word is a handle because the thing itself is too fast to hold. Blishwick spent his whole life trying to see it and wrote one thin book saying he couldn’t.”

“The boy has bought Blishwick,” said Dumbledore. “It was on the first receipt.”

Flitwick’s eyebrows, which were already high, went higher.

“Then he has read a man say it cannot be done,” he said slowly, “and gone on doing it. On a kitchen floor. At six.” He put the cup down again, carefully, as if it had become heavier.

“There is a third letter,” said Dumbledore, and read it. He has made a thing. A stand for my teapot, oak, with forty marks round the rim that are not anybody’s runes, and the pot on it is warm, and it has been warm since Easter. He cut them with a tool he made himself on a Muggle lathe. He had no wand. He says he held the sentence while he cut.

Flitwick did not pick the cup up this time.

“He set it,” he said. “Without a wand. Albus, one cannot set a rune without a wand. The setting is the wand’s work; that is what the wand is for in runecraft, the carving is only the shape and the wand puts the — one cannot hold a sentence in matter with nothing but—” He stopped. “What held it?”

“He did, Filius. Arabella says he held it.”

Flitwick sat for a moment with his small hands flat on his knees.

“I should like to meet him,” he said.

“In two years.”

“Two years—”

“Two years, Filius. On the thirty-first of July, 1991, Minerva will knock on a door in Surrey, and in September you will have him in your classroom, and I rather think you will have the most interesting first-year of your career and quite possibly the most interesting Charms student in the history of the school, and you will have him because I did not go up a garden path in 1986 and spoil whatever it is he is building in there.” He said it gently. “I have been wrong about a great many things in my life and I may be wrong about this. But I made a decision on a doorstep, and I have held to it through two trays of cuttings and three receipts, and I am going to hold to it for two more years, and you may both tell me afterwards exactly what it cost.”

Flitwick looked at him for a long moment, with his head on one side.

“Does he know what he’s doing?” he said. “The boy. Does he know that it isn’t — that nobody—”

“He knows,” said McGonagall, from her chair, before Dumbledore could answer. “Read the receipts. He bought the Statute and the Azkaban book and a Healing text and four numbers of your journal, Filius. He knows exactly what he’s doing. He is finding out what it is for.” She stood. “And he wrote in the margin of Blishwick. Arabella saw it. Two words.”

“What two words?”

“Not quite,” said Minerva McGonagall, and went to find her supper.

He sat late, after they had gone, with the three trays and the quiet castle, and did a thing he had not done in some years, which was to go down to the Restricted Section himself, with a lamp, and find a book.

He had read it in 1909, at eighteen, when it was two years old and he had been the sort of young man who read everything and agreed with nothing. On the Composition of Charms: Notes Towards a Grammar. Edwin Blishwick had been a Hufflepuff, a decade ahead of him, a quiet man with ink on his cuffs who had worked in the Department of Mysteries for six years and then resigned, for reasons he never gave, and had spent the rest of his short life trying to see the thing nobody could see. The book was his only publication. It had sold, Dumbledore recalled, some ninety copies, of which he had bought one and Flourish and Blotts had evidently kept another on a high shelf for seventy-eight years until a child of seven in a football cap had taken it down because his eyes had slid past it, which was, Dumbledore thought, standing in the Restricted Section at midnight with the lamp, exactly the sort of thing Edwin would have found funny.

He had gone to France in 1915. Not with the wizards; there had been no wizards’ war to go to. With the Muggles, as a Muggle, in a Muggle regiment, because — he had written to Dumbledore once, a single letter, in 1916, from somewhere he was not allowed to name — the two sides are one country whatever the Statute says, and I find I cannot sit in Wiltshire reading about charms while the other half of it is being killed. He had died the following year, in the mud, in a place with a Flemish name. There had been nobody to send the notice to but Dumbledore. He still had it.

He stood in the stacks with Blishwick’s thin grey book in his hand and read the footnote, which he had not needed to read, because he knew it: the present writer regrets that observation of the casting moment is beyond any human faculty and must be inferred.

And he thought about a child of seven writing not quite in the margin of it, in pencil, in a round hand, and about a child of eight buying the Statute and the preamble and the dissenting pamphlets of 1692, and a book about Azkaban, and a Healing text, and about a squib writing each side has half in a letter, which the boy had said to her in a garden, at eight, having buried nobody, having read everything.

The two sides are one country whatever the Statute says.

“Edwin,” said Albus Dumbledore, quietly, to the Restricted Section, “I think you may have a reader.”

He did one more thing that night, and he did it at his desk, with the lamp, and the three trays pushed to one side, and he did it on good paper, in the hand he used for letters that mattered, and when he had finished he read it through once and did not send it.

Dear Harry, You will ask me, in two years, why I left you on that step. Arabella has told me that you have asked her, and your aunt, and that you have written the question down, in order, on a list, and that you intend to ask it of me to my face. You are right to. I have had eight years to prepare the answer and I find that it has changed every year, and that the version I would have given you at six is not the one I would give you now, and that neither of them is the whole of it, and that you will know that, because you have — Arabella says — spent your life reading the sides of faces for the part that is not being said. So I am writing it down now, this once, at midnight, in July of 1989, with your degree in a tray on my desk and a book by a dead friend of mine beside it that you have read and I had thought nobody would read again. I am writing it so that when you ask, I shall have an honest record of what I believed before I met you, and shall not be tempted to improve it. I left you there because your mother died to keep you alive, and her death made a thing that I could use, and the only place it would hold was under her sister's roof. That is the first reason and it is true and it is not sufficient. I left you there because I was afraid of what our world would make of you if it had you at fifteen months — the bowing, the newspapers, the men who would have wanted to raise the Boy Who Lived as a symbol or a weapon or a son — and I thought that a Muggle house, however cold, would at least leave you alone to become something before anyone told you what you were. That is the second reason and it is true and it is the one I am least sure of. And I left you there because I was tired, Harry, and grieving, and I had made a great many decisions that autumn very quickly in the dark, and this was one of them, and I have never been certain it was right, and I did not go back to check, for eight years, because I was afraid that if I went back I would find I had been wrong and would not be able to bear it. That is the third reason. It is the true one. It is the one I shall find hardest to say to your face. I have watched you from two streets away through a kind woman's letters for eight years, and I have learned from her shopping lists that you have read everything, and from her stories that you can do a thing I did not know could be done, and from a newspaper that you took a first-class degree at eight and held the scroll as if it were someone else's. I have paid for your books without a word because a word would have been an intrusion and I had forfeited the right. I told you once to read chapter nine twice and I have regretted the presumption and treasured it, both, ever since. I do not know what you are. I know what you have decided to be, because Arabella wrote it down: a table, at which two halves of one country sit down for the first time. I knew a man once who believed that, and died for it in the mud, and I have his book on my desk tonight and your margin is in it. In two years you will walk into my office and ask your question, and I shall give you an answer that is good, and true, and not the whole of it, because that is what I am like, and Arabella has warned you. This is the whole of it. I shall keep it in the drawer. If, when you have asked, you want it — ask for that too. Albus Dumbledore

He folded it. He put it in the drawer of the desk that held three other letters he had written and not sent, over sixty years, and closed the drawer, and sat for a while with his hands folded on the empty blotter, looking at nothing.

Then he took a fresh sheet and wrote to Arabella, and the letter was short, and it said: Thank you for the cutting. He held the scroll well. Please find enclosed the sum for a new tin — a large one — and tell him, from me, that the Healing text he bought in June has an error in its fourth chapter that has killed two people, and that I should be glad to know whether he finds it.

Wisteria Walk, the following Saturday

“He says,” said Mrs Figg, reading from the letter with the right hand while Harry sat on the good end of the settee with a chocolate biscuit from the new tin, which was enormous and had a picture of the Forth Bridge on it, “that the Healing book you bought in June has an error in the fourth chapter that has killed two people. And that he should be glad to know whether you find it.”

Harry put down the biscuit.

“He read my receipt.”

“He reads all your receipts, dear. I did tell you.” She folded the letter. “Well? Have you?”

Harry had read the Healing text in June, on the train, in forty minutes, and had put six Mr Prentice margins on it, and he went back along the shelf now to the fourth chapter and stood in it for the length of a quarter-second, which was most of a minute, and found the place — a dosage, in a table, where two columns had been transposed by a printer sixty years ago and nobody had checked, because the table looked right, and tables that looked right were where the bodies were buried.

“Page ninety-one,” he said. “The table. The columns are the wrong way round. If you gave the dose in the left column for the weight in the right column you’d—” He stopped.

“Yes,” said Mrs Figg. “You would. Twice, he says.”

Harry sat very still on the settee, with the Forth Bridge beside him and Mr Tibbles on the arm of the chair watching him with the lecture-hall face, and found that his heart was going.

Not because of the table. Because of the question. For three years a man in Scotland had paid his bills without a word, and told him a chapter, and now, for the first time, had asked him something. Had set him a problem. Had looked at a receipt and seen what was on it and thought — Harry could see the thought as clearly as if it were on the shelf — let us find out whether he reads the way Arabella says he reads.

Page ninety-one. He had found it in June and put a margin on it that said surely not and had not known it had killed anyone.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “Will you write back and tell him page ninety-one, the table, the columns transposed, and that I’d put a margin on it in June, and that I’d like to know who the two people were, if that’s allowed, because I think somebody should know their names.” He picked up the biscuit again. “And tell him thank you for the question. Tell him it’s the first one anybody on that side has asked me, and that I’ve got about four hundred for him, in order, and that I’m saving them.”

Mrs Figg looked at him over her glasses for a long moment, with the gladness in it, and the pride, and the thing that had been getting steadier for three years and was, he thought, very nearly steady now.

“I shall put it exactly so,” she said, and picked up the pen with her right hand.
