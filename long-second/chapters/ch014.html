Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 14

Petunia’s Drawer

He had been choosing the evening for a year, and when it came it was a Wednesday in April with the kitchen window open and the smell of cut grass coming in, and Vernon at a conference in Birmingham, and Dudley at Piers Polkiss’s for the night, and his aunt at the sink with her sleeves pushed up, washing a single cup.

He had made the tea. That was new — he had never made her tea before, in nine years; it was not a thing the house permitted — and she had looked at the cup when he put it down beside her, and then at him, and said nothing, and drunk it. The garden was going blue outside. Somewhere down the road a lawnmower stopped.

“Aunt Petunia,” said Harry. “Will you tell me about my mother?”

The cup went still in the water.

He had thought for a year about how to ask and had decided, in the end, on the front door, because he had learned from a tennis ball and a times table that the front door was for the things people were ready for, and he had watched her for nine years and believed — on the evidence of a cutting in a purse, of sensible, of a woman standing at the bottom of some steps in Egham and saying well, go on, then — that she was readier than she knew. If he was wrong, he would lose nothing but an evening. He had plenty of those.

She didn’t turn round. She stood with her hands in the water and looked out at the garden, where there was nothing, and he watched the side of her face in the window’s reflection and saw it do what it had done at the cooker when he was two, the white coming up — and then, for the first time in his life, saw it stop. Hold. And slowly, like a tide going out that had been coming in for twenty years, go back down.

“I wondered,” said Petunia, “when you’d ask.”

“I didn’t want to frighten you.”

“No.” She took her hands out of the water and dried them, slowly, on the tea towel, one finger at a time, the way she did when she was deciding something. “No. You never have. You’ve been very—” She stopped. She put the tea towel down. “You’ve been very careful of me, haven’t you. All this time. I did notice.”

He said nothing. It was the most she had ever said to him that was not an instruction.

“Sit down,” said Petunia.

She told him at the kitchen table with the window open and the garden going dark, and she did not tell it well, because she had never told it to anyone, and some of it came out in the wrong order and some of it came out as weather and he sat very still and let it all come, in the long quiet, and arranged it afterwards.

There had been two girls in a town up north that she did not name, and the younger one had been able to make a flower open and close in her hand, and the older one had been able to make nothing at all. That was where it started, and in a sense that was all of it, and she said so, flatly, looking at her own hands on the table: “That’s all it ever was. She could and I couldn’t. People think there’s more to it. There isn’t.”

There had been a boy — “an awful boy, from the bad end of town, with hair that wanted washing” — who had known what the younger girl was before she did, and had told her, and the word he’d used was the word Mrs Figg had used in a garden, and the older girl had hated him for it with a completeness she could still feel, thirty years later, in her teeth.

There had been a letter, when the younger girl was eleven, on thick yellowish paper, in green ink, from a school. Petunia said the name as if it were a stone she had carried in her mouth for twenty-five years and was spitting out at last: “Hogwarts.” And then, after a moment, lower: “She went. Of course she went. And I wrote to them. Did you know that? I never told anyone that, not your uncle, nobody. I wrote to the headmaster, eleven years old, and asked if I could come too.”

“What did he say?”

“He said no.” She was looking out at the garden. “He was kind about it. That was the worst part. He wrote back to a child of eleven with his own hand and he was kind. I’ve still got it. Somewhere.”

The headmaster had a name, and she gave it, the way she’d given the school’s: as if it had been waiting a long time to be said in that kitchen. Dumbledore.

Harry held very still. The kind man had a name now.

She told him about the summers, when the younger girl came home with her trunk and her wand and her new friends’ letters and her new words, and the older girl had sat in her room with the door shut and heard them laughing downstairs. About how the younger girl had tried. “She tried. I’ll say that for her. She kept trying till the end and I wouldn’t have it. I wouldn’t have any of it.” About a wedding she had not gone to, and a christening she had not been asked to, and a card with a photograph of a baby with its father’s hair, which she had put in a drawer without looking at it and had looked at, she admitted, to the table, perhaps twice a year since.

And then there had been a night, at the end of October, nine years ago, and a man in a cloak on the doorstep at midnight, and a basket.

“There was a letter,” said Petunia. “In with you. From him. The headmaster.” Her hands had gone flat on the table, pressing down, as if the table might lift. “It said she was dead. Both of them. It said how, more or less — I didn’t understand half of it and I didn’t want to, there was a man, a wizard, a bad one, and he’d — and you’d —” She stopped. “It said you’d lived. It said nobody knew why. And it said—”

She stopped again, for longer, and he waited, and the lawnmower down the road did not start up again, and he understood that he had come to the drawer itself, the one that had been nailed shut his whole life, and that she was standing in front of it deciding, and that nothing he could say would help, so he said nothing.

“It said that as long as you lived under my roof,” said Petunia, very low, “there was a thing he’d done — a protection — that would hold. That you’d be safe here in a way you couldn’t be anywhere else. Because of her. Because she was my sister and I’d taken you in. It didn’t say from what. It didn’t need to. It said as long as he calls this place home.” She looked at Harry for the first time since she had sat down. “That’s why you can’t board. Did you think I didn’t know you wanted to? Egham, and the school, and the solicitor, and the trains — I know what it all is, I’m not a fool, you’ve been walking out of this house an inch at a time since you were seven and I’ve let you, every inch, because God knows there’s little enough here to hold anyone. But you sleep under this roof. You sleep under it until the letter comes. I promised her that. I never promised her anything else in her life and I promised her that, to a piece of paper, in the middle of the night, with you asleep in a basket on my step, and I have kept it.”

The kitchen was very quiet.

Harry sat in it and found that he could not, for a moment, say anything at all — and that the moment, which was long for anyone and for him was a working afternoon, was not the long quiet he lived in. It was something else. A different kind of stillness, one he had no name for and had never been inside, and he sat in it and looked at the woman across the table who had made his life a low grey misery for nine years, and who had been keeping a promise the entire time, badly, grudgingly, in a house that hated it, to a sister she had not spoken to in a decade, with nobody to tell.

He had thought he had read her. He had read the fear. He had read the drawer. He had never once read this, because it had never occurred to him that there was anything in his aunt he could respect, and he sat with that and let it be true and let it be his fault, and then he put it down, because there was a thing to do, and the thing was in front of him.

“Thank you,” he said. “For keeping it.”

Petunia made a small sound that was not a laugh and not the other thing.

“Don’t,” she said. “Don’t you thank me. I have not been—” She shut her mouth on it. Then, after a while, to the table: “I have not been what I should. I know that. I’ve known it the whole time, and I’ve done it anyway, and I couldn’t have told you why, except that every time I looked at you I saw her at eleven with that letter in her hand, going somewhere I couldn’t follow. And then you turned out clever.” It came out almost as an accusation, and then wasn’t. “Clever like her. Cleverer. In the papers. And I thought, there it is, there it goes again, and I couldn’t — I didn’t know how to — ” She stopped. “You made me tea.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody in this house has made me a cup of tea in nineteen years.”

“I know,” said Harry. “I should have done it sooner. I was being careful of you. I think I was being careful of the wrong thing.”

She looked at him, across the kitchen table in the dark, with the garden gone and the window open and the smell of grass, and he watched a thing happen on her face that he had waited nine years to see without ever once letting himself expect it, and it was not love, he was not a fool either, but it was the thing that lives next door to love and lends it sugar.

“You look like him,” she said. “Your father. The hair. But you’ve got her eyes, and you look at people the way she did. As if you were working out how to help and hadn’t decided whether they deserved it.” She stood up. “Wait there.”

She came back with a shoebox.

It was a different shoebox from his — hers was for a pair of court shoes, 1972, from a shop that no longer existed — and she put it on the table between them and took the lid off and did not look inside it, and said, “I kept some things. I don’t know why. Don’t ask me why.”

He didn’t. He looked.

A letter, on thick yellowish paper, in a hand that slanted and was kind, beginning Dear Miss Evans and dated the summer of 1971. Another letter, heavier, newer, folded small, which she took out of the box and put face-down on the table under her hand before he could read more than the first line, and the first line was Petunia — and the hand was the same, and he let it go. A christening card with a photograph of a baby with black hair standing straight up, and a woman holding him, laughing, with red hair down her back, and the baby’s eyes were green and so were hers.

And under that, at the bottom, gone soft at the corners from a quarter-century of being not looked at twice a year, a photograph of two girls on a swing in a park. The older one pushing. The younger one with her head back and her hair flying, in the middle of a shout of laughter you could hear through the paper, and the older one — eight, perhaps, or nine; his age — looking at her with an expression that Harry recognised at once, because he had seen it on his aunt’s face that evening across the table and had only now understood what it was.

She had loved her. Before the flower, before the boy, before the letter in green ink. There it was, in black and white, in a shoebox, on a swing.

“You can have that one,” said Petunia, not looking. “The swing. I’ve got the rest.”

He took it. He held it the way he’d held the chess cup in the photograph, with both hands, as if carrying it for someone else.

“Aunt Petunia,” he said. “The letter. The one from the step. May I—”

“No.” It was quick, and then softer. “No. Not yet. It’s — there are things in it about that night that you don’t need, and things about the protection that I don’t understand, and I think — I think he’d want to tell you himself. The headmaster. When they send for you.” She put the lid back on the box. “Ask him. Ask him why he left you with me. He had his reasons. I’ve had nine years to think about whether they were good ones, and I still don’t know, and I’d like to hear him say.”

“I will,” said Harry.

“And Harry.” She had never used his name as the start of a sentence either. It was a night for it. “When you go. When the letter comes and you go off up north to that place with your trunk and your — all of it. Come back in the summers.” She said it to the shoebox. “Not for me. I shan’t pretend. For the roof. The paper said as long as he calls this place home, and I don’t know how long that’s meant to hold, and I’m not having it fall down for want of you sleeping here six weeks a year.” A pause. “And your uncle will want to tell the Rotary.”

It was so exactly her, and so exactly not, that he laughed — a real one, out loud, in that kitchen, for the first time in his life — and after a second, to the shoebox, so did she.

He lay under the stairs that night with the photograph of the swing propped against the wall where the Radio Times page had been, and he did not turn the television on, and he did not open the blue notebook, and he went back through the evening once, slowly, in the long quiet, and then put it on the shelf where he kept the things that had made him who he was, in the place of honour, next to the straight fingers.

Hogwarts. Dumbledore. A man — a wizard — a bad one. As long as he calls this place home. A protection, left on a doorstep with a baby and a warming charm, that depended on the roof of the one person in the world least willing to hold it up, and who had held it up anyway, for nine years, with her jaw set, for a sister on a swing.

He had been so sure the Dursleys were weather. He had built his whole childhood on it. And weather had turned out, at the kitchen table, in the dark, with the grass coming in, to have kept a promise.

He had the name now. The kind man. He turned it over: Dumbledore. The man who had written back to a girl of eleven in his own hand and been kind, and left a child on a step with a letter, and set a squib to watch over him, and said tell him the truth if he asks. The man who had reasons. Harry found that he was not angry, which surprised him a little, and he examined the not-being-angry and found it was made of the same two things as always: that the man had plainly minded, in his way, and that there would be time. He was going to meet him. He was going to ask him why. He had promised his aunt, and he had found, that evening, that he intended to keep his promises to his aunt, which was a sentence he could not have written the night before.

He looked at the swing for a long time.

Fifteen months.

He was nine and three-quarters, and he knew the name of the school and the name of the man, and he had a photograph of his mother laughing, and an aunt who had made him laugh, and a List, and a hundred and six pounds in a toffee tin for the far side, and a season ticket, and a protection on his roof that he now understood well enough to respect, and he was done — he thought, lying there — he was done finding things out second-hand. From garden benches, from shoeboxes, from the sides of people’s faces.

The far side had shops. Mrs Figg had said so, and his aunt had said so, and he had a hundred and six pounds and travel to and from any place of learning and a London on the other end of a train line where, somewhere, there was a street that ran on the second set of rules.

He was going to go and look at it.

“I know his name now,” he said to Mrs Figg, on the Saturday, in the garden, with the cats.

She didn’t ask which. She snipped a stem and looked at it and dropped it in the trug.

“Then you know more than I’ve told you,” said Arabella Figg, “and that’s exactly as it should be, and I shan’t ask how.” Snip. “She told you herself?”

“Yes.”

“Well.” The secateurs paused. “Well, now. I’ve lived two streets from that woman for nine years and I’d not have put money on it.” She looked at him sideways, with the gladness in it. “You made her tea, I suppose.”

“How did you—”

“Because that’s what you do, dear. You find the door they already use.” She went back to the geraniums. “Your mother was the same. Everyone said.”
