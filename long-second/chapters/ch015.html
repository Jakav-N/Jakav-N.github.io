Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 15

The Edge of the Map

He told his aunt he was going to the Science Museum, and it was true, and he went.

That was the rule now, and he kept it even when it cost him a morning: he did not lie to Petunia. Not since the shoebox. So on the first Saturday in May he stood in the kitchen with his bag and his season ticket and the eight pounds forty for the London fare counted out of the toffee tin, and said, “The Science Museum, Aunt Petunia. There’s a pendulum,” and she looked at him for a moment over the sink with the face she had worn since April, which was the old face with one wall taken out of it, and said, “Be back for tea,” and he was out of the door before Vernon had finished his second rasher.

He had never been to London alone. He had never, in fact, been to London at all, except through it on the way to Marge’s, in the back of the car with Dudley’s elbow in his ribs. He stood on the concourse at Waterloo at twenty past nine on a Saturday morning with the departure boards clattering and ten thousand people going past, and he had to stop, as he had stopped on the library threshold at five, because the thing in front of him was too large to take in at a walk.

Ten thousand faces. He had lived nine years in a town where he knew every one. Here they came past at the rate of a hundred a second, which was to say a face every two seconds of his own time, close enough to read, every one of them doing a dozen things at once and all of it legible, and none of it repeating. A woman late for something that frightened her. A man carrying flowers he was not sure about. Two boys Dudley’s age with the specific, elaborate casualness of boys who have just done something. It was the newsagent’s rack, the day he was three, multiplied by the whole city, and he stood in the middle of it with his bag on his shoulder and let it come, and did not move for a full real minute, and nobody noticed, because nobody at Waterloo on a Saturday morning notices anything.

Then he went and found the pendulum.

It hung in a stairwell at the Science Museum, a brass ball on a wire four storeys long, swinging slowly over a dial, and every few minutes it knocked over a small peg, because the earth had turned underneath it while it swung, and that was the whole of the exhibit, and he stood at the rail and watched it for an hour by his own clock, which was eighteen seconds by the museum’s.

He had made one of these. Washers and string, in a cupboard, at four, to find out how fast he was. The man who had made this one had used it to find out how fast the earth was, and had been right, and had written it down for people who were slower than he was, and here it was a century later, still knocking over pegs for children. Harry watched the brass ball come to the end of its swing and hang there — he could see it hang, a quarter-second of stillness at the top of the arc that nobody else in the stairwell could see at all — and go back, and he thought about pads, and margins, and the reader in fifty years, and found he was perfectly happy.

He did the rest of the museum because it was there and because he had promised. The steam engines, which he had read about and had never seen move, and which moved with a slow enormous courtesy that he liked better than anything he had read. The aeroplanes hung from the ceiling. A capsule that had been to the moon and come back, scorched, small, and he stood under it and thought that people had sat in that, three of them, and gone, and come home, and that this was a species that did such things, sometimes, when it decided to.

At one o’clock he ate the sandwiches Petunia had made — she had made him sandwiches; that was new too, and he had not known what to do with it, so he had said thank you and taken them — on a bench outside, and then he put the Science Museum on the shelf where it belonged and went to do the thing he had come for.

He had worked out where to look the way he worked out everything, which was by refusing to guess.

He knew almost nothing. A street, Mrs Figg had said, with shops, and a bank, and a newspaper. London, his aunt had said, when he’d asked where the shopping had been done for the trunk and the wand, in a voice that said she had not been taken and had not asked. A street in London where thousands of people in cloaks went to do their shopping and nobody on this side of the curtain had ever noticed: that was the whole of his information, and it was, he had decided on the train, enough.

Because a street like that had to have a door. People in cloaks had to come from somewhere and go to somewhere, and the somewhere had to open onto an ordinary pavement, and the pavement had to be one where a cloak did not draw a crowd. Where in London could you walk in a floor-length cloak and a hat and not be looked at twice? He had thought about it for a week, with a street atlas from the Stonewall library open on the camp bed, and arrived at an answer that pleased him with its plainness: the theatres. Costume shops. The streets round Covent Garden and Soho and the Charing Cross Road, where half the people on the pavement were dressed as something, and the other half had seen everything.

So that was where he went, and he did it from the top deck of buses, because the top deck of a bus was the best observation platform ever built for a boy who could read a crowd at a face every two seconds and had an afternoon.

He rode the 24, and the 29, and the 176, and he sat at the front with his forehead against the glass and watched the pavements of the West End go by in a slow river of faces, and he looked for the thing he had seen three times in his life — the cloak in the wrong season, the hat with the wrong brim, the particular wrongness of someone dressed by a different century’s rules and not in on the joke — and for an hour he saw nothing, and then on Charing Cross Road, going north, he saw a woman.

Emerald green. Ankle-length. A pointed hat she was carrying rather than wearing, as if she’d been told to, and resented it. She walked fast, like someone with an errand, and the Saturday crowd parted round her and did not look at her, and that — the not looking — was what caught him, more than the green. The crowd should have looked. Londoners looked at everything for a quarter-second and then moved on; he had watched them do it all morning. They did not look at her at all. Their eyes went over her the way water goes over a stone.

He got off at the next stop and walked back.

She was gone, of course; she had had a full minute. But he had the place now, within a hundred yards, and he had the afternoon, and he began to do the thing he was best at in all the world, which was to stand still and wait.

He stood on the east side of Charing Cross Road for two hours, in a shop doorway and then at a bus stop and then in a shop doorway again, and he watched the far pavement, and in two hours he saw eleven of them.

He kept a tally in his head. A tall man in a cloak like a monk’s. Two women together, arguing cheerfully, one with a basket that moved on its own — he saw it move; nobody else did. A boy his own age, in ordinary clothes, holding the hand of a woman who was not in ordinary clothes at all, and looking round at the street with the specific wide-eyed air of a child being taken to town for something exciting. An old man with a beard who stopped on the pavement, looked both ways like a child crossing, and stepped in — and that was the thing.

They all stepped in. At the same place.

Not through a door that he could see. That was the problem, and he had expected it to be the problem, and had sat on the train composing how he would handle it. Between a large bookshop and a record shop there was a stretch of frontage perhaps thirty feet long, and eleven people in two hours had walked along the pavement towards it and had — he could not put it better — gone. Not vanished. Nothing so vulgar. They had simply, at a point about two-thirds along, stopped being on the pavement, and the crowd had flowed on over the place as if it had always been empty, and every time it happened, Harry found that he had been looking at the record shop.

Every time. Eleven times. He was the best watcher in Little Whinging and probably in England and he had been looking at the record shop.

That was the moment he understood the curtain, and it was not at all what he had imagined. He had pictured it like a wall, or a lock — a thing on the far side, keeping people out. It was nothing of the kind. It was on his side. It was in his head. Something, in that thirty feet of frontage, reached into the place where he decided what to look at, and moved his looking, gently, an inch to the left, and let him think he’d done it himself. He had felt his attention go. He had every one of those eleven moments on the shelf and he went back through them now at leisure and found the same thing in each: a small sideways pressure, like a hand on the back of the head, so soft and so timed to the ordinary jump of the eye that no one on earth could possibly have noticed it happening.

No one, that is, who looked at the world at one-to-one.

He stood in his doorway with his heart going and understood what he had. It was a sentence. It was the roses — a shape, a grammar, working on him, written by somebody long ago and left running, like the warmth in the blankets. And it was written for people whose attention jumped once and landed. His jumped two hundred times in the space where theirs jumped once. There was room in his looking that the sentence had never been built to fill.

So he went and stood in it.

He crossed the road and walked, slowly, along the thirty feet, and felt the hand come — soft, kind, nothing to see here, dear — and let it move him, and watched it move him, the way he had watched the roses. Then he walked back, and let it do it again, and watched again. Then a third time, and this time, where the hand came, he did a thing he had been practising on the roses for a year without knowing what it was for: he stood still inside himself and didn’t go with it. He let the push arrive and simply declined to look at the record shop, the way you decline a biscuit, and held his looking where it was, on the stretch of grimy brick between the two shopfronts, and for a second nothing happened, and the hand pushed harder, almost puzzled —

and there was a pub.

Small. Grubby. A low door and a window of thick greenish glass and a sign he could not read from the angle, hanging crooked, and it had been there the whole time, and it was there now, and a man in a cloak came along the pavement and nodded to no one and went into it, and the door shut, and the Saturday crowd went by on either side of Harry Potter where he stood on the kerb with his mouth slightly open, looking straight at the edge of the map.

He did not go in.

He wanted to. He examined the wanting, standing there, and it was the strongest wanting he had felt since the newsagent’s at three, and he examined it anyway, because a boy who had written no dumb decisions at the top of a plan at seven owed it to the plan. He had no money with him but the fare home and a pound. He had no idea what was inside, who was inside, what the rules were, whether a nine-year-old in a school jumper walking in alone was ordinary or alarming. He had a face, Mrs Figg had let him understand, that people on the far side knew — Mr Potter, an honour — and he did not yet know why, and did not intend to find out by walking into a room full of them unprepared. Information first. That was the whole of his method. It was how he had got everything he had, and he was not going to abandon it on a kerb in the Charing Cross Road because the door was right there.

So he did the next best thing, which was to buy a cup of tea in the sandwich bar directly opposite, and sit in the window for an hour and a half, and watch, and listen.

He could see it now. That was the strange part, and he turned it over while the tea went cold: having once declined the hand, he found he could see the pub whenever he chose, the way you can see the hidden shape in a picture once someone has pointed. The push was still there — he could feel it, every time his eye went that way, the little nothing to see — but it had become a thing he noticed rather than a thing he obeyed, and he sat in the window making a note in the blue notebook of exactly where in the sentence the push lived, and what it was made of, and how it had been meant to fail.

And people came and went, and some of them stopped to talk on the pavement outside, and the window of the sandwich bar was open a crack for the steam.

He heard a great deal. Most of it meant nothing yet; he filed it anyway, because he filed everything. A man complaining about the price of something called floo powder. Two women on the subject of a Minister he had never heard of, who was apparently a fool. A boy his own age, the one with the wide eyes from earlier, coming out now with his mother and a long thin parcel he was carrying as if it might go off, saying “—can I try it, can I, just once, just outside—” and the mother saying “Not in the Charing Cross Road,” in a voice Harry recognised from every mother in every high street in England.

And then, late, with the light going, an older man in a tweed cloak came out of the pub with a younger one, and stopped on the step to do up a button, and said, in the carrying voice of a man who has had a drink and is pleased with an afternoon:

“—told him, take it to Gringotts, they’ll change Muggle money for anyone, always have, you just have to put up with the goblins looking at you as if you’d brought it in on your shoe—”

and the younger man laughed, and they went off down the road towards the station, and Harry sat in the window of the sandwich bar with his cold tea and did not move for some time.

Gringotts. The bank. They’ll change Muggle money for anyone.

He wrote it down. He wrote down floo powder and Minister and goblins, and the name on the pub sign, which he had finally read when a bus went past and lit it — THE LEAKY CAULDRON — and three others he had caught in the afternoon’s traffic of talk: Flourish and Blotts, said twice, both times about books; the Prophet, said with the weary affection people used for a newspaper they didn’t trust and read anyway; and a name said by the two arguing women that he had almost missed, because it had been said the way you’d say the high street, as if everyone knew it.

Diagon Alley.

He put the notebook away. He paid for the tea. He stood on the pavement opposite the pub as the lamps came on, and let the hand push him towards the record shop one last time, and declined it, politely, and looked straight at the low door with the greenish window and the crooked sign for a long moment, with the whole of London going past him on either side and not one person in it able to see what he was looking at.

Not yet, he told it. Soon.

Then he went and caught his train.

He planned it on the way home, in the dark, with his forehead on the glass, and the planning took him the whole of the line and was finished before Little Whinging, which by his own clock was about three days, and he felt, when it was done, the particular calm of a person who has turned a thing he wanted into a thing he was going to do.

He would go in the summer. July, before the birthday; the birthday was the letter, and he wanted to walk through that door before anyone sent for him, once, on his own account, so that whatever he was to them when they came, he would already have been something to himself. He would bring the money — all of the far-side half, a hundred and six pounds and whatever the crossword and the chess had added by then — and change it at the bank where the goblins looked at you as if you had brought it in on your shoe, and he would find out what a goblin was when he got there. He would wear a cap. He would go to the shop called Flourish and Blotts, which was about books, and he would spend every penny on books, because books were the one thing he knew how to turn into everything else.

He would not stay long. He would not talk to anyone he didn’t have to. He would walk in, and buy, and walk out, like any other child in a cap on a Saturday, and he would be home for tea.

And he would read, that night, under the stairs, in a language nobody on this side spoke, about the world that had sent a man to bow to him and a squib to watch him and a letter to his aunt in the middle of the night, and he would find out — at last, first-hand, off a page, with nobody’s face to read it from — who he was.

“How was the museum?” said Petunia, at the door, in the hall light.

“There was a pendulum,” said Harry. “It knocks over pegs. The earth turns under it.” He hung his bag on the hook. “Thank you for the sandwiches.”

She looked at him a moment longer than the sentence needed, with the face with the wall out of it, and he had the odd, strong sense that she knew precisely where he had been all afternoon, and had decided — the way the good ones did — to stand a little to one side of the door.

“Tea’s at six,” she said.
