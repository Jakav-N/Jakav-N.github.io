Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 16

Doctor

The thing from 1979 gave up the rest of itself in November, on a Tuesday, at the board, and this time neither of them said anything at all for most of a minute, which for him was the better part of a morning and for her was a minute, and he thought afterwards that they had both needed exactly the time they took.

Lemma two had taken the autumn. Lemma three had taken a fortnight, because lemma three turned out to be lemma two seen from the other side, and once he had said so — standing at the board with the chalk, on a Thursday, to a woman who had been looking at it from the first side for eleven years — Carrow had sat down very suddenly in the chair with the paper on it and said, “Oh, for God’s sake,” in a voice that was not angry at all. And then there had been the thing itself, the theorem the lemmas had been for, which was not hard once the three of them were standing, the way a roof is not hard once the walls are up, and he had built it over three Tuesdays while she watched with her feet on the paper and her glasses off, and on the fourth Tuesday he had written the last line and put the chalk down and turned round.

“Say it,” said Carrow, very quietly.

“It holds.”

“Yes.” She did not move. “Yes. It does.” And then, after the minute, in the ordinary voice, as if nothing had happened: “Eleven years. I started that the year you were born, Potter. I was thirty-eight. I had hair.” She put her glasses on. “A thesis. Write it. In English, for the examiners, who are slow, and one of whom is going to be from Cambridge and will arrive having decided not to believe a word, and I want him to leave having read every one. You have till Christmas.”

“Yes, Professor.”

“And Potter.” He had turned to the door and turned back. “It is a good theorem. Not because it is hard. Because it is true, and it was not true before, and now it is, and in fifty years somebody will build a bridge on it by accident and act very surprised.” The knife-face did its small thing. “Go home. Your aunt will have my hide.”

He wrote it by Christmas. A hundred and sixty pages, in the round hand that had learned at six to go at a reader’s pace, with every step laid down like a stepping stone and no more than one stone at a time, and he gave it to Carrow on the twentieth of December in a box file, and she took it home to Yorkshire for the holidays, and when she came back in January she put it on the desk between them and said:

“There is nothing wrong with it. I looked for three weeks. I had my brother look, who is a better mathematician than I am and has never admitted it. There is a comma on page ninety that I would have placed differently.” She pushed the box across. “Submit it. The viva will be in April. I shall ask Aldous from Cambridge to be external, because he is the rudest man in the field and if he passes it nobody will dare say a word.”

The book came out in February, and it was the only thing he had ever written that was for people who were slow on purpose, and Mrs Prentice read it first, which was the point.

He had written it over the autumn, in the evenings, after the thesis and before the broadcasts, in a hand that was not the round one for examiners but something looser, because he had decided at the first page that it was for a child of six at a kitchen table that nobody could see, and children of six did not want stepping stones; they wanted someone to walk beside them and point. It was eighty pages. It was called Three and Thirteen, because that was what sixteen was, and because he had sat under a staircase at six with a blue book about even numbers and had nobody to tell. It began with conkers and ended with a mountain nobody had climbed, and in between it did the thing the men in brown jackets had done for him at one in the morning for four years: it went a different way, and said so, and said in fact let’s do that again more carefully, and hesitated before the hard steps so that you knew where they were.

Carrow had read the first draft and said, “This is not for me,” and then, after a page, “No. This is not for me at all,” and then read the whole of it without stopping and put it down and said, “Give it to Edith first. If Edith says it’s right, it’s right. Then I know a woman at a press who will want it, and will be insufferable about having wanted it.”

Mrs Prentice read it on a Thursday, after four, at the returns desk, with the stamp beside her and the new aluminium ladder that did not stick standing in the adult section like a stranger at a funeral, and she read it the way she read everything, which was all the way through without looking up, and when she had finished she sat for a while with her hands on the cover.

“My husband,” she said, “would have liked this.”

“I wrote it for him. Partly. For the margins.”

“I know you did, dear. It’s got his enough for one night in it, on page forty, you’ve put it in a boy’s mouth.” She took her glasses off and polished them on her cardigan and put them back on, which was how she signed things. “It’s right. Every word. It’s the first book I have read since 1979 that I should like to put in a child’s hand and not watch them read it, because I shouldn’t need to.” She pushed it back across the desk. “Tell Helen’s woman at the press to print a great many. And Harry — the money.”

“The hospital. The children’s one. All of it. Mr Pargeter’s drawing it up so it goes straight there and never touches the trust.” He paused. “I kept half of the chess money. I’m not keeping any of this. This one isn’t shoes.”

“No,” said Mrs Prentice. “No, I shouldn’t think it is.” And then, in the filing voice, going back to the stamp: “There’s a nine in the bottom corner. It wants to be generosity and it isn’t.”

The press printed a great many. Three and Thirteen, by Harry Potter, aged ten, with a two-line foreword by Professor H. Carrow that said I did not help with this book and I should like that known, went into the shops in the second week of February and was in its third printing by Easter, and the first cheque to the children’s hospital was for a sum that made Mr Pargeter’s bowling-green voice go, for one syllable, slightly uneven. There was a photograph in the Telegraph of a girl of seven reading it on a bench, and she was frowning at page forty, and he cut that one out and kept it, because it was the second time a newspaper had got the point.

He designed the press in March — in his head in a night, and on paper for the back of the Edition in a week — and he could not build it, and he knew exactly how, and that was the strangest feeling of his life.

It had been coming since the runes. Sentences in things. He had named it at seven and gone on reading, and the enchantment book had told him how a sentence was put into a thing — slowly, by hand, by someone who held the whole of it in their head and pressed it into the matter the way you press a seal into wax, and it took a master a day for a simple one and a week for a good one and that was why enchanted things were rare and expensive and most people had never owned one. And the runes book had told him how a sentence was carved, which was different — the carving held it; you did not have to; but you could only carve what the runes had marks for, and the runes had marks for perhaps forty things, and his notation had two hundred and twenty.

And chapter nine of the enchantment book — tell him to read chapter nine twice, and he had, and then eleven more times — had said a thing in passing, the way the time of the last bus is mentioned, which was that the pressing and the carving were not two things. The carving was a pressing that had been made permanent. The master who pressed a sentence into a cup for a day was doing by hand, slowly, with his whole mind, what a carved rune did once and for ever. And the author of chapter nine had wondered, in a footnote, whether a thing could be made that did the pressing — not a wizard; a thing, enchanted itself, that held a sentence and pressed it into whatever was put in front of it, over and over, the way a seal does — and had concluded that it could not, because no wizard could hold a sentence precisely enough to press it into the pressing-thing without the sentence going ragged, and a ragged seal made ragged copies, and ragged copies of a sentence were worse than none.

Harry had read that footnote at eight and put a margin on it that said they shout and gone on.

He read it again in March, at ten, with four hundred and twelve pages of grammar in a biscuit tin and a feather that held and a thesis in a box file on its way to Cambridge, and he sat under the stairs with the book open and understood, very clearly, in the long quiet, that the footnote was describing the thing he had been for since a doorstep.

Nobody could hold a sentence precisely enough. He could. He had been holding sentences precisely for four years; it was the one thing he had that they didn’t; there was a chapter in the Edition about it called On Holding. If he had a telephone — if he had a telephone and a year — he could press a sentence into a thing so cleanly that the thing could press it into other things, and the copies would not be ragged, because the original would not be, because he could see it go in.

A seal. A press. A thing that made enchanted things the way a printing press made books, and he knew what the printing press had done to the world because he had read the whole of the fifth floor’s history shelf at six, and he sat under the stairs with his heart going and designed it.

Not the whole thing; he did not know enough about matter yet, about what would hold a sentence and what would spit it out, and the enchantment book was vague about that in the way books were vague about things their authors had never had to do. But the sentence — the one the press itself would hold, the one that said take what is held in you and put it into what is placed before you, exactly, and again — that he could write, and he wrote it, over a week of nights, in his marks, two hundred lines, with the asking built into it so that the press would check each copy before it let it go, the way the doorstep sentence checked a child’s temperature before it gave warmth. He could not cast it. He could not build it. He could look at it on the page and see, the way Carrow saw a proof, that it would hold.

And then, because he was ten and had read the fifth floor, he sat with the design and did the thing the fifth floor had taught him to do with any machine, which was to ask what it would be for, and the answer came up through him like the tide at Mrs Figg’s hands.

The doorstep sentence. The one that checked for cold and gave exactly the difference. Pressed into a blanket. A thousand blankets. In a children’s hospital eleven miles from a photograph on page seven, where a girl of six had no hair and the ward was cold at night because the heating was a line in a budget. He did not know whether a sentence would hold in a Muggle blanket. He did not know whether a Muggle child could be warmed by a thing that answered caring. He did not know whether the Statute would let him within a mile of the idea, and he had read the Statute and suspected it would not, and he wrote that down too, in capitals, under the design, because the thing Carrow had trained in him insisted on it: I DO NOT KNOW IF THIS IS ALLOWED. FIND OUT BEFORE, NOT AFTER.

But he knew what it was for. He had known since two newspapers on a blanket.

He called the design the press and the sentence in it the seal, and under them, in English, for the reader who was slow, he wrote the plainest thing he had written in four hundred pages: This is the lever. Not a wand in a hand. A sentence in a thing, made by someone who can hold, copied by a thing that can check. One good sentence, pressed a thousand times, is a thousand people warm. Nobody has built this because nobody could hold the first copy. I can. Give me a telephone and a year.

Then he closed the biscuit tin and put it under the bed and lay in the dark with the swing on the wall and the date beside it — four months now — and found, for the first time since the cupboard week at five, that he was not waiting for the far side to send for him.

He was waiting for them to get out of the way.

The viva was on the ninth of April, in a room at Royal Holloway with a long table and a view of trees, and it lasted four hours and eleven minutes, and the rudest man in the field came out of it with his tie loosened and said, to Carrow, in the corridor, in a voice meant to be overheard, “Helen, I came here to fail a child and I have been taught.”

Professor Aldous had arrived from Cambridge having decided not to believe a word. Harry had seen it in the first second — the way the man sat, the way he put the box file on the table without opening it, the way he looked at the small boy in the school jumper at the far end with an expression Harry had last seen on Mr Bellamy of four examination boards, before Mr Bellamy had been shown the regulations upside down. The internal examiner was Dr Lindqvist, who had read the thesis twice and understood two-thirds of it and said so, which was honest, and which Aldous had plainly taken as a sign that the whole thing was a Royal Holloway conspiracy to put a child in a gown.

So Aldous had gone for the throat, and Harry had let him.

It was, he thought afterwards, the best four hours of his life to date, and he had said that about several things and this time he would have defended it to anyone. Because Aldous was good. Not kind — not remotely; Carrow had chosen him for exactly that — but good, the way the men in brown jackets were good, the way Mr Haines was good about diagrams: a man who had spent forty years knowing where the bodies were buried in this one field and had come to dig them up. He went for the lemma first, the one from 1979, and he went for it from the Hungarian side, and Harry said, “Yes — that trap; Professor Carrow found it in February of last year; it’s the second footnote on page forty-one, and here is why it doesn’t bite,” and showed him on the board, and Aldous was quiet for a moment and then went for the second lemma from a side Harry had not seen anyone come from, and Harry stood at the board for eleven minutes — most of a day and a half — and thought, with the room watching, and found the answer, and it was in the thesis, on page ninety, next to the comma Carrow would have placed differently, and he said so, and Aldous turned to page ninety and read it and said, “Hm.”

By the second hour Aldous had stopped going for the throat and started going for the mathematics, which was a different thing and a better one, and by the third hour the two of them were at the board together, Aldous with the chalk and Harry at his elbow, working out whether a thing Aldous had suggested — a thing that was not in the thesis at all, that Aldous had thought of in the car — would extend the result to a case Harry had marked as open, and it did, nearly, and the nearly was interesting, and Lindqvist had stopped taking notes and was simply watching, and Carrow, who as supervisor was supposed to say nothing, said nothing with such force that Harry could feel it from the board.

In the fourth hour Aldous sat down and loosened his tie and said, “All right. All right. Last question, and it’s not a mathematical one, and you may decline it.” He looked at Harry down the long table. “You are ten years old. You have written a thesis that I would have been proud to write at forty and that I could not have written at any age, because I did not see the overlap and I have looked at that problem for a decade. I want to know — not how. Helen has warned me that you will not say how and that I am not to ask. I want to know why. Why mathematics. A mind like yours could have gone anywhere. Why this.”

Harry thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how.

“Because you can always check whether you’re right,” he said. “I told a newspaper that when I was seven and it was the one true thing I told them. Everywhere else I’ve ever been, people decide whether a thing is right by who said it. Here it doesn’t matter who said it. It matters whether it holds. A child of ten can stand at a board in front of a man from Cambridge who came to fail him, and if the thing on the board holds, it holds, and the man from Cambridge has to say so.” He paused. “It’s the only room I’ve ever been in where that was true. I wanted to find out what it felt like to be judged by the thing instead of the face. It feels—” he searched “—it feels like being seen. Properly. For the first time. I’d like everyone to have that. I think it’s what the maths is for.”

The room was quiet for a moment.

“Hm,” said Professor Aldous. He looked at Carrow. “Helen. I shall want it noted in my report that the candidate answered a question that was not on the examination, at length, in a way that I found I agreed with, and that I am sixty-three and did not expect to agree with anything this afternoon.” He stood up. “Pass. No corrections. The comma on page ninety is in the wrong place and I shan’t make him move it.”

The ceremony was on the twelfth of July, in the hall with the long windows, on a hot afternoon, and the gown fitted this time, because Carrow had had it made, and he did not care at all, because of who was in the third row.

They had all come. Petunia, in the hat from two years ago and gloves that were new, with Vernon in the funeral suit beside her and Dudley — twelve, enormous, in a Smeltings blazer that he hated and had worn anyway — on the end, eating nothing for once, watching the stage with an expression Harry had never seen on him and filed, carefully, as interest. Mr Haines, in the tie, with his hands on his knees and no chalk on his elbows. Mrs Prentice in the good coat and Mrs Figg in the cherry hat, side by side again, handbags on knees, not speaking, and this time — he saw it from the stage — holding hands, which they would both have denied to the grave. Dr Lindqvist, who had come to sit in the audience rather than on the stage because, he said, he wanted to see it properly. Mrs Osei, in a coat Harry had never seen her without the lab under. Mr Pole from the chess club, who had lost to him every Wednesday for four years and had come to lose to him, he said, in a gown. And at the very end of the row, in a suit that did not fit him either, with his tie loosened in a hall where every other tie was tight, Professor Aldous of Cambridge, who had not been invited and had come anyway, and who caught Harry’s eye from the stage and did not smile and inclined his head one inch, which Harry understood was a thing he had probably not done for anyone since 1962.

Carrow read the citation herself. She had told Lindqvist he could not be trusted to keep it under a minute, and she kept it under forty seconds, and the forty seconds said what the thesis was and what it had cost and who had been stuck on it since 1979, and ended: The candidate is ten years old. I have been asked by a great many people what that means. I do not know what it means. I know that he found the overlap on the back of my door while I was looking at the front of it, and that I have never once in five years had to tell him a thing twice, and that he has never once in five years failed to tell me when I was wrong. I have supervised nineteen doctoral students. I should like it on record that he was the easiest, and that this is the hardest thing I have ever had to say in public.

And then the Vice-Chancellor, who had been told what to say and said it anyway as if he had thought of it: “Doctor Potter.”

He walked across the stage in a gown that fitted and took the scroll with both hands, carefully, the way he had taken everything in his life that had been handed to him on a stage, as if carrying it for someone else, and he looked at the third row, and Petunia was looking at her gloves, and then she looked up, and her face did the thing, and he thought: for the roof. And for the tea.

Outside, in the sun, there were photographers — a great many; this one had gone round the world, and there had been a man from an American network on the telephone to Mrs Bell every day for a fortnight, and Mrs Bell had taken to answering it with no before he had finished the question — and he gave them one picture, and said no to everything else, and the picture was a small boy in a gown holding a scroll, and the headline, in nine countries, was some version of DOCTOR, 10, and he did not read any of them, because he had an appointment in nineteen days and nothing in nine countries was going to be as interesting.

Vernon bought every copy the newsagent had. The lounge ran out of wall.

“Doctor,” said Petunia, that evening, at the sink, to the window, in the fence voice. And then, drying a cup, in the other one: “She’d have — your mother. She’d have—” She stopped. “Well. She’d have known what to say. I don’t.”

“You came,” said Harry. “That was what to say.”

Petunia dried the cup for a long time.

“Nineteen days,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then I shall want the garden done before you go. And the gutters.” She put the cup away. “Doctor or no doctor.”

He took stock on the twenty-ninth of July, two days before, at the kitchen table, where the light was best, in English, in his head, because the cupboard was full of a trunk that Mrs Figg had brought back from the fourth trip with a face like a woman carrying a coffin she approved of, and because he wanted to do it somewhere he could see the garden.

Ten. Eleven on Wednesday. I have stopped counting the other way. Call it the better part of two thousand, and call it not the point.

Have: a doctorate, in a world that has never heard of one. Four papers, a book in its fourth printing whose money goes to a ward eleven miles from here. Seven languages. A county championship I gave up to keep the game. A library with a stepladder that does not stick and a woman in it who held the door. A professor with a knife for a face who said I was the easiest. A man from Cambridge who came to fail me and inclined his head. An aunt who came in, both times.

Have, on the other side: everything the shop will sell, read, eleven times. Four hundred and twelve pages of grammar in a biscuit tin that nobody alive can read. Forty sentences of my own, six good. The asking. The seal. The roof, read. The doorstep, read. Six pages by a clerk about the cold, read until I could see the shape of what he wasn't saying. Two names in the back of a list. Four hundred questions, in order, for a man who has asked me three.

Can: hold. A feather that stays. A pot warmed from forty yards. A pencil, a candle, a cat, a pair of hands, each at a tenth, each costing like a hill.

Have not: a telephone. Two days.

He sat with that line for a long time. Then the last thing, the thing he was going to carry up the drive like a lamp, in the words he would give the reader in fifty years, because he wanted them to know what he had thought he was for, at ten, two days before:

I am not going to that school to learn magic. I have learned the magic that is in the books; I learned it on a kitchen floor and in a cupboard and on a bench in a street with an old woman holding my hand. I am going to that school because it is where the telephone is, and the hospital is, and the man who can listen, and the cold, and because the two halves of one country have never once sat down at a table and somebody has to be the table. I have read both sides. I am the only one who has. I am the better part of two thousand years old and I have a doctorate and a biscuit tin and I am eleven on Wednesday.

Let them deal with it. That is their job. Mine is to stop making it hard.

Outside, Petunia was deadheading the roses by the fence, right-handed, with her back to the window, and he watched her for a moment at the speed he always watched things, which was slowly, and then he went out to do the gutters, because he had promised, and because a doctor who kept his promises to his aunt was, he had decided, the only kind worth being.

The date on the inside of the cupboard door had two days left on it. He had stopped looking at it some time ago. He did not need to. He had known since a garden bench at five exactly how long he had, and he had used every day, and on Wednesday morning at nine o’clock somebody from the far side was going to knock on the front door of Number Four, Privet Drive, and he was going to open it, and whoever it was had better, he thought, up a ladder in the July sun with his hands full of leaves, have brought a very strong cup of tea.
