Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 16

Flourish and Blotts

The cap was Dudley’s, an old one from a football club Dudley had supported for a fortnight in 1987, and it came down over Harry’s forehead to the eyebrows, and he had tested it in the oven door for a week and was satisfied that nothing above his glasses could be seen.

He stood on the pavement of the Charing Cross Road on the third Saturday in July with the cap on and a hundred and eighty-four pounds in an envelope inside his shirt and a canvas bag folded flat in his pocket, and let the hand on the back of his head push him towards the record shop, and declined it, and looked at the low door with the greenish glass, and found that his heart was going quite hard, and let it.

He had come alone on the train. He had told his aunt the truth — “London. The street. I’m going to buy books, and I’ll be back for tea” — and she had stood at the sink for a long moment with the face with the wall out of it and said, finally, “Keep your money inside your shirt,” which he had already planned to do, and which he took, correctly, as the nearest thing to a blessing she had in her.

He crossed the road, and pushed the door, and went in.

It was dark, and it smelled of beer and woodsmoke and something else, something that reminded him of the kitchen floor at Mrs Figg’s with his hand on a cat. A low room, beams, a fire going in July. A dozen people at tables. A man with no teeth behind the bar polishing a glass with a rag that looked older than the glass. Nobody looked at him for longer than it took to see child in a cap, and that was exactly as long as he had planned for.

He had thought hard about this part, because he knew there was a door and did not know how it opened. The tweed man on the step had said take it to Gringotts as if the taking were obvious, and the family with the boy and the parcel had gone out through the pub, not into the street, so the way to the street called Diagon Alley was somewhere in here. He had assumed a door at the back and had decided, if the door needed something he didn’t have, to do the thing he had done at Stonewall and the sports hall and every other threshold of his life: stand still, and watch, and wait for someone to show him.

It took four minutes, which was most of a day, and he spent it at the end of the bar pretending to read a notice about something called the Knight Bus.

A family came in from the street — a tall thin father, a mother with a list, two girls in summer dresses, one of them hopping — and went straight through the room without pausing and out into a little walled yard at the back, and the father took a stick from his sleeve and counted bricks, three up, two across, and tapped one.

Harry was eight feet behind them, in the doorway to the yard, and he saw it.

He saw the brick wriggle, which nobody else could have, because to anyone at one-to-one the brick simply moved; but to him it wriggled, a little shiver running through the clay like a dog shaking off rain, and then the brick beside it, and then the wall was doing something — not opening, not folding, but rearranging, each brick turning on an axis it should not have had and sliding past its neighbour in an order, a long patient order that he could read, that had the same spine as the roses and the tea, the pointing and the pushing and a bracket in the middle so large and so old that it took him the whole of the half-second to get to the end of it. It was a sentence. It was a sentence somebody had written into a wall a very long time ago, so well that it had been running every day since without anyone needing to think about it, and it said, in effect, for those who know the knock, a way, and he stood in the doorway of the Leaky Cauldron’s back yard and watched it say so, brick by brick, and thought: I could write that. Not today. Not this year. But he could see every mark of it, and nothing he could see had ever stayed closed to him for long.

Then the wall was an archway, and beyond the archway was a street, and the hopping girl said “Come on,” and the family went through, and Harry Potter, in a football cap, went through eight feet behind them like a child whose mother had told him to keep up, and nobody turned round, and the wall closed behind him with a sound like a sigh.

He stopped. He could not help it; he had stopped on every threshold of his life that mattered, and this was the one all the others had been practice for.

A street. Cobbled, crooked, climbing a little as it went, and full — of shops with bowed windows and hanging signs, of people in cloaks and robes and pointed hats and ordinary cardigans, of noise, of smells, of owls. There were owls in cages outside a shop, dozens of them, blinking at the daylight. There was a stack of cauldrons on the pavement with a sign that said ALL SIZES — COPPER, BRASS, PEWTER, SILVER — SELF-STIRRING. There was a shop whose window was entirely broomsticks, and a crowd of boys in front of it, and a woman going past with a basket in which something was unmistakably moving, and nobody minding. The sky was the same sky. The sun was the same sun. And under it ten thousand people were going about a Saturday morning’s shopping in a street that nobody on the far side of a wall had ever seen, and the air was thick with the thing from Mrs Figg’s kitchen floor, so thick he could almost see it, the whole street humming at a pitch just under hearing like a struck bell that had never been allowed to stop.

He had known it was real. He had the word for it, and a blue notebook of its grammar, and a candle he could light. He had not known it would be this. This ordinary. This busy. A mother losing patience with a child over the price of something. An old man reading a newspaper on a bench. A boy his own age weighed down with parcels and a toad. Somewhere behind the world, every day, a whole town had been getting on with its morning, and all it had taken to find it was to decline a biscuit.

He put the cap down another quarter-inch and started walking.

The bank was at the top, and it was white, and it was the first thing he had seen on either side of the wall that had been built to be looked at.

Everything else on the street leaned. The bank stood up straight and shone. There were bronze doors, and on either side of them, in a uniform of scarlet and gold, a person who was not a person — shorter than Harry, swarthy, long-fingered, with a face like a very clever walnut and an expression of such complete and professional contempt for everything passing in front of it that Harry, who had spent nine years studying faces, felt an instant and slightly alarming kinship. Goblins, the tweed man had said. As if you’d brought it in on your shoe.

He went up the steps. The goblin on the left looked at him, and at the cap, and at the school jumper, and did not bow, and did not sneer either, and Harry understood that he had been assessed, weighed to the ounce, and filed as not worth the effort of an opinion, and he found he preferred it to anything that had happened to him in Little Whinging in a decade.

Inside was marble, and height, and a hundred goblins at a long counter doing a hundred things with brass scales and ledgers and piles of coin that caught the light and threw it on the ceiling, and he stood for a moment in the great cool hush of it and then went, as he had planned, to the shortest queue.

“Yes,” said the goblin behind the counter, not looking up. He was writing in a ledger with a quill, very fast.

“I’d like to change Muggle money, please,” said Harry. He had practised Muggle. It was the one word of their language he had, and it sat oddly in his mouth, a word for himself that other people had made. “Into yours.”

The quill stopped. The goblin looked up — a long look, down a long nose, taking in the cap, the jumper, the fact that he was alone and nine — and then held out one hand, palm up, without a word.

Harry took the envelope out of his shirt and put it in the hand.

The goblin counted it. He counted it twice, fast, with the air of a man who could have done it once and was being insulting on purpose, and then he did something with the brass scales and a small book of tables and wrote a figure, and swept the notes into a drawer, and began counting coins out of another drawer onto the marble in three stacks — gold, silver, bronze — with a sound like the beginning of rain.

“Thirty-six galleons,” said the goblin. “Fourteen sickles. Nineteen knuts. Seventeen to the galleon, twenty-nine to the sickle, if you don’t know, and you don’t.” He pushed the stacks across. “Next.”

“Thank you,” said Harry.

The goblin had already looked down at his ledger. But the quill paused — a quarter-second, which was most of a minute — and the long nose came up a fraction, and the clever walnut regarded Harry Potter with something that was not warmth, nothing so vulgar, but was perhaps the faintest flicker of a professional noticing that it had been thanked, correctly, by a child who had not been told to.

“Keep it inside your shirt,” said the goblin. “Next.”

He kept it inside his shirt. Thirty-six galleons weighed a surprising amount. He went back down the white steps into the sun with the coins against his ribs and his heart still going and the whole street waiting, and he had, he reckoned, about four hours before tea, which was the better part of three years, and he knew exactly where he was going to spend them.

Flourish and Blotts had a window full of books stacked to the ceiling, and a bell on the door, and inside it was every library he had ever walked into and none of them, because the books moved.

Not all of them. Most of them sat on their shelves like books, floor to ceiling, in leather and cloth and something that might have been dragon, with ladders on rails and a smell of paper and dust and binding glue that went into him like the first day at the branch. But some of them shifted. A row of fat volumes on a high shelf were, very slowly, rearranging themselves into a better order. A thin book near the door kept trying to turn its own pages and being prevented by a strap. And in a cage by the till — he stopped, and looked, and thought of a woman in a garden saying some of them bite — a dozen heavy books with fur on their covers lay in a heap, breathing, and one of them opened one eye at him and closed it again.

He had thirty-six galleons, fourteen sickles and nineteen knuts, and he had planned this hour the way he had planned the sports hall, and he went to work.

He did not know what the books were. That was the whole difficulty, and he had thought about it for two months, and had concluded that there was only one honest method: read the spines. All of them. He had read ten thousand spines in the Survey at five and knew what a shelf of spines could tell you — what a field thought was basic, what it thought was advanced, what it had too much of and what it was embarrassed about — and he walked the shop, slowly, with his hands behind his back like a boy told not to touch, and read, at eye-speed, every title in Flourish and Blotts in about forty minutes, which in the hours he actually lived was a solid week of cataloguing, and came out the far end with a map.

The field had a canon. There were books that appeared in stacks — set texts, he guessed, for the school — and books that appeared singly and dusty, and the stacked ones were cheap and the single ones were not, and he had budget discipline from a toffee tin and the memory of what it cost to be without shoes. He let himself have the stacked ones that mattered: The Standard Book of Spells, Grade 1, and then, after a moment’s thought and a look at the price, Grades 2 and 3, because the grammar of the thing was what he was after and three years of its sentences for the price of a good dinner was not a thing you walked past. Magical Theory, by a man called Waffling, which he opened at random and read a page of standing up and found to be exactly what the title said and badly in need of a Mr Prentice in its margins. A History of Magic. Hogwarts: A History, thick, and a copy of today’s Daily Prophet from a pile by the door, because a newspaper was the fastest way into a place he had ever found.

And then, on a high shelf in the back room where the single dusty books lived, a thin grey volume with no title on the spine at all, which he only took down because it was the one book in the shop his eyes had slid past, twice — and he had learned that spring what it meant when his eyes slid past a thing — and which turned out, when he got it open, to be called On the Composition of Charms: Notes Towards a Grammar, by nobody he had heard of, printed in 1911, and which began, on the first page, with the sentence: It has long been held that a spell is a thing one learns. The present writer wishes to suggest that it is a thing one reads.

He stood in the back room of Flourish and Blotts with the thin grey book in both hands and the breathing books in their cage and the street humming outside, and read the first chapter standing up, and when he had finished he found that he had to sit down on the bottom rung of the ladder for a moment, because somebody — seventy-nine years ago, in a hand that disliked writing — had stood where he was standing.

Not as far. Nowhere near as far; the man had worked at one-to-one and said so, apologetically, in a footnote, the present writer regrets that observation of the casting moment is beyond any human faculty and must be inferred. He had inferred. He had got the spine, and the pointing part, and a bad guess at the bracket, and then he had run out of road, and the book was thin because the road was short. But he had seen there was a road. And nobody, judging by the dust, had read him since the war.

Harry bought it. It cost more than all the others together, and he paid without a word, and the assistant at the till — a young woman with ink on her fingers who had watched a child in a cap read every spine in the shop without touching one and had decided, visibly, not to have an opinion about it — wrapped the thin grey book separately, in brown paper, and said as she handed it over, “Nobody’s bought that in the whole time I’ve worked here. What did you want it for?”

“The margins,” said Harry, which was true, and she laughed, not understanding, and he put it in the canvas bag with the others and went out into the sun with four galleons left and a bag that cut his shoulder, and sat down on the first empty bench he came to, outside a shop that sold quills, and took out A History of Magic, because it was the thickest, and turned, as he had turned every book since he was five, to the index first.

He was looking for his mother’s name. That was the whole of it. He had a photograph of her on a swing and a word in a dream and your mum especially, everyone said, and he had come to a street with a bookshop in the hope that a street with a bookshop would have a page.

He found Potter in the index of A History of Magic. It had seven entries. The last one said Potter, Harry — see also Boy Who Lived, The.

He sat on the bench for a very long time.

Not reading. He had read it — the whole entry, and the four pages the entry pointed to, and the three further pages in Hogwarts: A History that those pages pointed to, and the front page of the Daily Prophet, which happened, with the kind of timing that would once have made him believe in luck, to carry a small piece on the lower half about the ninth anniversary that autumn of the fall of You-Know-Who — all of it in about four minutes, with the street going past, and then he had closed the books and put them in the bag and sat with his hands on his knees and let the four minutes catch up with him, which took, by the only clock that counted, most of a year.

He had known. He kept coming back to that, the way you press a bruise. He had known since a violet hat that strangers were glad to see him, since a garden bench that his parents had been of this world and had died in it, since a kitchen table that there had been a wizard, a bad one, and that he had lived when he should not have, and that nobody knew why. He had known all the pieces. He had simply never had them in print, in order, in the plain cold prose of a reference book that did not know he was reading it, under a heading with his own name on it.

Lily Potter (née Evans) and James Potter were murdered at their home in Godric’s Hollow on the night of 31 October 1981 by the Dark wizard Lord Voldemort, whose name most of the wizarding community still declines to speak. Their son Harry, then fifteen months old, survived an attempt on his life by the same wizard — the only person ever known to have survived the Killing Curse — and Voldemort’s power was broken in the attempt, by means which remain, at the time of writing, unexplained. The scar on the child’s forehead is believed to be a consequence of the curse. He is widely referred to as the Boy Who Lived.

The green light. Nine years of a word in a dream, and it had been this. It had been a man in his parents’ house, and his mother’s voice saying his name, and then a thing that had killed her and had been meant to kill him and had — for reasons the reference books did not know — not. And had, instead, perhaps, done something else. Spun a wheel. He had wondered since he was four where the two hundred had come from. He had never once let himself wonder whether it had come from the same place as the scar.

He wondered now. He found, sitting on a bench in Diagon Alley with the sun on his hands, that he could not stop.

The books did not say the man was dead. They were careful about that, the way careful books were. Vanished. His power broken. Many believe. And Harry, who had been raised by a reference book’s habits, read what the careful books did not say, and understood at last what a protection on a roof was for, and why a squib had been posted two streets away to watch for cloaks in the wrong season, and why a kind man with reasons had not come up the garden path in nine years. He had thought the watching was for him. It was. It had also, quite plainly, been for something else, and the something else had a name that nobody said, and the books thought it might still be out there.

He turned that over for a long time, carefully, the way he turned over anything with edges. Then he put it on the shelf — not the shelf of things that had made him who he was, a different shelf, a new one, which he labelled, in the private alphabet, things I will deal with when I am ready and not one day before — and he let it sit there, and found that he could.

Because the other thing was true too. The glad faces. Mr Potter, an honour. The whole street going past him this minute, buying cauldrons and losing patience with children, was a street that had been, nine years ago this autumn, afraid in a way he could read in the careful prose of the history books — the way a reference book goes quiet around a thing — and had stopped being afraid, on one night, because of a thing that had happened in a house in Godric’s Hollow that nobody understood. And the thing that had happened had been him. Not anything he had done. He was fifteen months old; he had done nothing, except, apparently, not die. But every person in this street had woken up on the first of November nine years ago into a world that was suddenly, inexplicably, safe, and had been told that a baby had done it, and had been glad.

They had bowed to him in Little Whinging because he was the morning they had woken up to. That was all. He sat with it. He had not asked for it and did not deserve it, and he found he could not, quite, resent them for it, any more than he could resent Mrs Oduya for drinking the tea.

And Dumbledore. The man had a whole paragraph in Hogwarts: A History and a line in every account of the war, and the line was always the same: the only one he ever feared. The kind man. The man who had written to a girl of eleven in his own hand and told her no. The man who had left a baby on a step in Surrey with a warming charm and a letter and a protection that cost his aunt nine years, and had set a sentry, and had not come. He had reasons, Petunia had said. Ask him.

Harry sat on the bench in the sun with his mother’s name in print in a bag against his leg and thought that he would. He thought that he would ask him a great many things, and that he would do it as a colleague, not as a child, and that the man had better have good answers, and that — this surprised him, and he looked at it carefully to make sure it was real — he rather hoped that he did.

Then he stood up, and shouldered the bag, and walked back down the crooked street past the owls and the cauldrons and the boy with the toad, and nobody looked at him, and at the wall he waited for a family and went through eight feet behind them, and the pub was dark and smelled of beer, and the Charing Cross Road was loud and ordinary and full of Saturday, and he walked to the station in it with his own history in a canvas bag cutting into his shoulder, and somewhere between the pub and the platform, for the first time in nine years, properly, with facts, in the middle of a London afternoon with nobody looking, he cried.

Not for long. He let it come, the way he let everything come now, and then he let it go, and he bought a ticket, and he was home for tea.

“Did you get your books?” said Petunia, at the door.

“Yes.” He put the bag down in the hall. It made a sound like a small library. “There’s a photograph. In one of them. Of — of them. Both of them. At their wedding. It’s a good one.”

She stood very still.

“I thought you might like to see it,” said Harry. “If you wanted. You don’t have to.”

Petunia looked at the bag on the hall floor for a long moment, with the face with the wall out of it, and then — slowly, the way she did everything that mattered — she wiped her hands on her apron, and knelt down on the hall carpet beside a canvas bag full of books that ran on the second set of rules, and said, not looking at him:

“Show me.”
