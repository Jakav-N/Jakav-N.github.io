Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 17

Interlude: The Watchers’ File

The Deputy Headmistress’s office, Hogwarts, a Thursday evening in late June 1991

Minerva McGonagall had forty-one letters to prepare that summer, and forty of them took her an hour and a half.

It was the same every June. The Book gave up its names; the Quill wrote the addresses in its slanting green hand; she checked each one against the register, because the Quill was old and had once, in 1974, sent a letter to a boy who had been dead for eleven years, and she had never entirely trusted it since. Then the Muggle-born and the Muggle-raised went into a separate pile for the visits, and the rest went to the owlery, and she had a cup of tea, and it was done.

The forty-first letter was on her desk by itself, and beside it was not a file. It was a drawer. Albus had sent the whole drawer down, that afternoon, by house-elf, with a note that said you had better have all of it, and it stood on her side table like a piece of furniture that had got lost, and she had been not opening it for two hours.

She opened it.

Five years of Arabella Figg, in order, in a hand that had changed in the winter of 1987 from a careful left-handed slant to something freer and faster and rather less legible. Two trays of cuttings — no; three; the third had been started in the spring — with a small boy in them growing up at the rate of a photograph a year: a chess cup, two sheets of paper, a scroll in a gown that did not fit, a scroll in a gown that did. Four receipts, itemised, each with Albus’s thank you for the itemisation in the margin and, on the second, in a smaller hand, tell him to read chapter nine twice, which she had not seen before and looked at for some time. A slim blue book with a child’s face on the cover and the words Three and Thirteen. Four offprints from Muggle journals. A thesis, in a box file, a hundred and sixty pages, which she opened and read the first paragraph of and closed again, because it was not her field and would not have been anybody’s at Hogwarts, and because the first paragraph had been written by a ten-year-old in a hand that went at exactly the pace a reader needed and not one step faster, and she had taught for thirty-four years and had never once seen that in a student of any age.

And at the bottom of the drawer, in an envelope of its own, four letters from Albus to Arabella and four from Arabella to Albus that were not reports. They were questions. Albus had asked the boy — through Arabella, in Arabella’s hand, with Arabella’s evident disapproval of the method in every line — four things. A dosage table in a Healing text. A thing about wards that Minerva did not follow. The year of a particular dissenting pamphlet. And, in the fourth, the one from May, a single sentence: Ask him what he thinks the guards at Azkaban are made of.

The boy had answered all four. The fourth answer was two pages long, in Arabella’s copying hand, and McGonagall read it standing, twice, and then sat down, which was the sequence in which she received news she did not like, and the news was this: that a child of ten in a cupboard in Surrey had read a Ministry clerk’s six pages from 1931 and concluded, with reasons, in order, that a Dementor was a sentence that eats sentences, and that nobody had ever tried to read one, and that he intended to.

She sat for a long time with the two pages in her hand and the June light going long across the desk and the forty ordinary letters in their neat pile, and then she said, aloud, to nobody:

“Doctor.”

Then she picked up the drawer — it was heavy; she did not care — and went to find the Headmaster.

“Albus. This child has a doctorate.”

“So I see. So, I think, does most of the world; Arabella sent the cutting from the Telegraph and three others, one of them in French. I have been meaning to ask you what a Vice-Chancellor is.” Dumbledore did not look up from the thing he was doing, which involved a small silver instrument and a very long pair of tweezers. “A sort of senior Deputy Headmaster, I believe. In a gown.”

“He is ten. He was nine on the day. He is eleven in a month and Arabella says it as though the month were a mercy.” McGonagall put the drawer down on his desk, on top of the instrument, which he permitted. “I have read the drawer. All of it. I have read a thesis I do not understand and a children’s book I did, and four questions you asked a child through a squib because you had forbidden yourself every other method, and his four answers, and the fourth answer, Albus, the fourth answer is about Dementors, and he is right, or if he is not right he is wrong in a way that nobody in this castle has had the wit to be wrong in for three hundred years.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “I thought that too. I read it four times. I have shown it to Filius, who went quite pale and asked whether the boy had ever seen one, and I said no, he had read six pages by a clerk, and Filius said then God help us all when he does.” He set the tweezers down. The instrument, released, gave a small puff of lilac smoke and settled. “Sit down, Minerva. You have the face.”

“I have several faces. I have been accumulating them since the Quill wrote his address this afternoon and I have not yet decided which to wear.” She did not sit. “That is the other thing. The letter.”

She took it from her robes and turned it and held it so he could see the green ink, and the second line.

Mr H. Potter

The Cupboard under the Stairs

4 Privet Drive

Dumbledore read it. He read it for a long time, for a man who could read a page in a glance, and when he had finished he did not look up, and the room was very quiet, and Fawkes, on his perch, did not sing.

“I did not know,” he said.

“No.”

“Arabella did not know.”

“No. She could not have. In five years she has never crossed the step; she waits at the gate; she has told me so. The aunt has never asked her in and she has never asked to be.” McGonagall’s voice was perfectly level, which was how he knew. “Five years of letters, Albus. Three trays. A thesis. He has told her about the roses and the cat and the pot and the wall in the yard and the sentence in the bricks, and he has answered four questions you had no right to ask, and he has never once, in five years, mentioned where he sleeps. Nobody asked him. It never occurred to anyone in this drawer to wonder what was behind the front door, because everything in front of it was so—” she searched for the word and found it in his own file “—so remarkable.”

He closed his eyes.

She let him have the moment, because thirty-five years had taught her when to, and because she had had her own in her office with the Quill and knew what it cost. Then she said, because it needed saying and he was the only one who could hear it:

“It is in the file now. The Quill has put it there. We will not pretend afterwards that it was not.”

“No,” said Dumbledore. He opened his eyes. The mildness had not come down; she watched him decline to let it. “No, we will not. I wrote a letter in July of 1989, Minerva, that I did not send, and in it I said that I did enough to him on that step. I did not know the half of what I meant.” He looked at the green line. “And yet.”

“And yet?”

“And yet he wrote a thesis in it. And nine hundred pages of something Arabella says nobody can read, in a box file, under the bed. And a children’s book whose money goes to a Muggle hospital. He read the roof from the step with the milk going cold, Arabella says, and told his aunt it was the best thing he had ever seen anyone write, and she told him it was anchored to her and she had no intention of making a liar of a piece of paper.” He put one finger on the second line of the address. “I do not know what this means, Minerva. I know what it looks like. I have spent five years learning, from shopping lists, not to trust what things look like when they are about this boy. When you go — and you are going; it is in the file, in your own green ink, three times — ask him. Do not assume. He may have reasons for a cupboard that are his own. He seems to have reasons for everything else.”

“I shall ask,” said McGonagall. “I had already decided to. I shall ask, and I shall listen, and then, Albus, whatever he says, we are going to sit in this room and you are going to tell me the whole of the roof, and the whole of the step, and the whole of why you asked a child about Dementors through a squib, because I have spent five years watching you decide I did not need to know, and I have decided that I do.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “Yes. I have the letter. It is in the drawer — the other drawer; this desk has several. I wrote it for him. You may read it after he has.” He took his spectacles off. He did not polish them. He simply held them, and looked at the thesis in its box file, and she saw the window open and stay open, which she had not seen in sixty years. “Minerva. Three weeks ago I said to Filius that I should prepare to receive a colleague who was eleven.”

“You did.”

“I think I underestimated the matter. In both directions at once. He is going to walk into this castle with a doctorate from a world that has never heard of one, and nine hundred pages in a box file that nobody here can read, and a theory of Dementors, and four hundred questions for me, in order, and he is going to be eleven, and homesick, and a boy, and all of those things will be true at the same time, and I have no idea how to hold them, and I have been a headmaster for thirty-five years.” He put the spectacles back on. “I find the prospect — I shall be honest with you, since you have the drawer — I find it the most frightening and the most interesting thing that has happened to this school in my lifetime, and I am not certain which of those is the larger.”

McGonagall looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up the letter, and the drawer, and held them.

“Thirty-first of July,” she said. “Nine in the morning. I shall wear the tartan. He will have seen a witch — he has seen Arabella in a mackintosh for five years and Arabella is a squib in a housecoat and would tell you so herself — but he has not seen one who looked like something, and I should like the first one he sees to.” She went to the door. At it she stopped, and looked at the letter once more — The Cupboard under the Stairs — and then at him, and her face did the thing it did for students who had done something clever and were about to be told it did not excuse them, except that under the severity there was something else, which he had not seen there in some years.

“You asked a child of ten what Dementors are made of,” she said. “Through a squib. Because you wanted to know.”

“Yes.”

“And he told you. In two pages. With reasons.”

“Yes.”

“Then, Albus, I think you had better have a very strong cup of tea ready on the first of September, because Arabella says he has four hundred of those, in order, and I have met the sort of boy who writes things down in order, and they do not stop at four hundred.” She opened the door. “I shall look like something,” said Minerva McGonagall, and went to teach nobody, because it was June, and sat instead in her own office for a long while with a children’s book called Three and Thirteen open on her knee at page forty, where a boy in a story said enough for one night, and read it, and did not entirely understand why her eyes had gone hot, and did not look for the reason.

Wisteria Walk, the twenty-second of July

Dear Albus, This is the last of these, I think, in the old way. I shall write from the summers, of course; but not as a watcher. He will be eleven in nine days and the watching is yours after that, and you will do it from a good deal closer than two streets, and I hope you do it well, and I shall know if you don't. He knows the date. He has known it five years; he pinned it to the inside of a door at five and three-quarters and it has been there since, with other things written under it that I have not been shown and have not asked to see. He asked me on Saturday what to pack, and I told him warm things and a good pair of boots, and he thanked me for it as if I were somebody. The trunk is in the cupboard. I bought it on the fourth trip; the bill is enclosed and I shall thank you to pay it without comment, as usual. He has a photograph of his mother on a swing that he carries in his inside pocket, from the aunt, and I have seen him take it out when he thinks nobody is looking and put it back. He has done the gutters. The aunt asked. A Vice-Chancellor called him Doctor in front of nine countries a year ago, and on Monday he went up the ladder and did the gutters, because she asked, and because — he said this to me, on the train, and I shall put it down exactly — "a doctor who keeps his promises to his aunt is the only kind worth being." I do not think you will have had many first-years who said that, Albus. I do not think you will have had any. You asked me once, years ago, to tell you if he was happy. I did, and it was true, and it is still true. I should like now to tell you the thing I should have said first, five years ago, in the first letter after the cat, and did not have the words for. He is kind. Not polite — he is that too; he was that at three — kind. He mended my hands because he was sorry for them, and then he was sorry he hadn't noticed sooner, and said so. He gave half his money to a famine at eight and the whole of a book to a ward at ten and kept the rest for a reason he could say in one sentence. He makes his aunt tea. He asked me what I was so that I would not have to choose between him and you, and he waited two years to ask it, and when I told him he said thank you — for writing. He kept a library open with eleven sentences in a newspaper because a woman in it had held a door for him, and he has never once said so to her, and she has never once said so to him, and they do the crossword on Thursdays. He asked you for the names of two dead people because somebody should have them written down. He asked me, on Saturday, after the boots, whether I thought you would mind if he asked his four hundred questions in order or whether he should pick the most important ones first, and I said I thought you would mind very much if he picked, and he said good, because he had been worried about being rude. I have not been afraid for him since the hands. I find I am still, a little, afraid for the rest of you. But less, Albus. Much less, lately. He has read everything you have, and some things you haven't, and he has decided — I have watched him decide; it took him five years and he did it in a cupboard — that the two halves of one country have never once sat down at a table and that somebody has to be the table. He has a box file with nine hundred pages in it that he says is the grammar. He has a thing he calls the asking and a thing he calls the seal and he will not explain either to me because he says it would take a year and I haven't got one, and he said it kindly. He is going to walk into your school with all of that and a doctorate and his mother's eyes, and I think when you meet him you will understand why I have spent five years in a café in Egham with a pot of tea and never once minded. The Forth Bridge tin is nearly empty. I shall buy another for the summers. I find I am looking forward to the summers. Arabella P.S. He said to tell you — these are his words — that he hopes you have a very strong cup of tea ready, and that he has one question he would like to ask before the four hundred, and that it is the one on the step, and that he has been told you will have an answer and that it will be good and true and not the whole thing, and that he would like, if it is not too much to ask, to have the whole thing. He said you would know what he meant. I do not. I expect you do.

Dumbledore read it in the evening, at his desk, with the castle quiet around him and the lake going gold, and when he reached kind he stopped, as he had known he would, and read the word again, and sat for a long while with the letter in his hand and the window open, looking at nothing, in the way of a man who has asked for one thing for five years and has just, at the last possible moment, been given it by the one person he trusted to say so.

Then he read the postscript, and read it again, and sat for a while longer.

Then he opened the other drawer, the one with the four letters from sixty years in it, and took out the one from July of 1989, and read it through, and did not change a word, and put it in his inside pocket, where it would be on the first of September, and closed the drawer.

“The whole thing,” said Albus Dumbledore, quietly, to the empty office. “Yes. I rather think he had better.”

Then he took Arabella’s letter, and put it in the file, at the very end, after the green line and the cupboard and the thesis and the two pages about the cold, and closed the file, and did not open it again until the boy did.
