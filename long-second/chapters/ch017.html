Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 17

Theory

The first spell in The Standard Book of Spells, Grade 1 was the one for making light, and he read the description of it under the stairs at half past eleven that night with the torch on and the house asleep, and halfway down the page he put the torch down, because he did not need it.

Not because he’d done the spell. He had no wand, and the book was very clear that one needed a wand, and said so in a tone that suggested the question had never seriously been raised. He put the torch down because he had read the description — the Wand-Lighting Charm produces a steady light at the tip of the caster’s wand, sufficient to read by; the incantation is accompanied by a short upward flick — and under the description, in the place where he had learned, at four, to look for the real meaning of a thing, he had found a shape he knew.

It was the candle.

Not exactly. The candle had been fire, a happening, a thing he had spent six weeks learning to bracket. This was light, which was a happening of a different family — but the spine was the same, the pointing and the pushing, and the bracket in the middle was a cousin of his own, a cleaner one, a bracket that someone had plainly spent a great deal of time sanding down. He could see it. He sat in the dark with the book open on his knees and the words Lumos and upward flick on the page, and behind the words, the way the pub had been behind the record shop, he could see the sentence they stood for — and it was his sentence. A version of it. Written by somebody else, long ago, so well that it had been given a name and a wrist movement and put in a book for eleven-year-olds, who would say the name and do the movement and never once, in seven years of schooling, see the sentence underneath.

He had been reading their language off roses in a blue notebook for two years, with no dictionary. Here was the dictionary.

He read Grade 1 that night, all of it, every spell, with the blue notebook open beside it, and for each one he did the same thing: read the description, found the sentence under it, wrote the sentence in his marks, and then — this was the part that kept him up till the boiler — compared. His bracket for warm against their bracket for warm. His mend against their mend, which they called Reparo and did in a way he found both clumsier and more general than his own, like a spanner against a key. Theirs were better, mostly. Not always. Theirs were polished, that was the thing, worn smooth by centuries of hands, and his were rough-cut from a kitchen floor, and where theirs were better it was the way a road is better than a path, and where his were better it was the way a path sometimes goes where the road can’t.

And in the margin of the thin grey book from 1911, where its author had written, in despair, the present writer regrets that observation of the casting moment is beyond any human faculty, Harry wrote, in pencil, in the round hand, small, the first margin of his own life:

Not quite. — H.P., July 1990

He slept two hours. It was, he wrote the next morning, the best night since the pencil.

The A-level results came in the third week of August, and he found that he did not care about them at all, which surprised him, and then didn’t.

He had sat the four papers in June at Stonewall’s sixth form with the eighteen-year-olds — mathematics, further mathematics, physics, chemistry — in a sports hall he was no longer afraid of, and he had used the twelve days this time to work through the Grade 2 spells in his head, which was a far better use of a sports hall than the mathematics pads had been, and he had come out knowing he had done well, and had not thought about it since. The results were a formality for the county. The county needed its paper. So he went to the sixth-form corridor on the Thursday with his hands in his pockets, and Mr Haines was not behind the table this time, because Mr Haines did not teach the sixth form; but he was in the corridor, leaning against the noticeboard with the cardigan and the chalk, pretending to read a notice about the cricket.

“Four,” said Harry, when he had opened it.

“All of them?”

“Yes, sir.”

Mr Haines looked at the ceiling for a moment. “Youngest, I suppose.”

“I think so. By some years.”

“Hm.” He pushed himself off the noticeboard. “Well. I’ll tell you what I told you two years ago, Potter, and it’s still true: the moments one was straightforward.” And then, in a different voice, lower, the voice from behind the results table: “They’re saying the university will have you properly in September. Not Thursdays. Properly.”

“Professor Carrow wants me for the autumn term. The county’s said yes. It’s—” he hesitated, because the sentence had a door in it that he did not open for people on this side, “—it’s only for a year, sir. I’ve got somewhere to be after that.”

Mr Haines looked down at him from the height of thirty-three years of fourth-years.

“Somewhere to be,” he said. “At eleven.”

“Yes.”

“Boarding school, is it.”

“A sort of one.”

“Hm.” Mr Haines put his hands in his cardigan pockets, and seemed to come to a decision. “When I was a young man, Potter, I taught a boy for two years who was nearly as quick as you — not as quick, nobody is, but near enough that I used to lie awake. And one summer he simply went. Scholarship somewhere, his mother said, very grand, up north, and she wouldn’t say where, and he never wrote, and I never found out what became of him. I’ve wondered on and off for thirty years.” He looked at the cricket notice. “You’ll write. If you can. From wherever it is. Because I am too old to do that twice.”

Harry stood in the sixth-form corridor with four A’s in his hand and found that the thing in his throat was not for the A’s.

“I’ll write, sir,” he said. “I promise. If there’s a post from where I’m going, you’ll get a letter.”

“There’s a post from everywhere,” said Mr Haines. “It’s only a question of stamps.” And went off to find a cup of tea, and Harry stood a while longer, and then went home, and the newspapers did what the newspapers did — BOY, 10, YOUNGEST EVER A-LEVELS — and he declined television again, through Mrs Bell, with pleasure, and gave no interview this time at all, because he had planted the one line he’d wanted to plant a year ago and had nothing to say to the Telegraph that he could not say better to the far side in person in eleven months.

Vernon bought eight copies. The frame in the lounge got a brother.

They found the floor on a Tuesday in November, in Carrow’s office, at twenty past four, with the rain coming down the windows and the radiator ticking and the board so covered in chalk that she had started writing on the back of a door.

It had taken a year. The colouring problem — she had been stuck on it since 1983; he had found a corner of it in twenty-two minutes on a train in October of 1989; and for thirteen months since, two afternoons a week, the two of them had gone at the general case from every side either of them could think of and a great many sides that neither of them would have thought of alone. That was the thing he had not expected about working with someone. He had thought it would be faster. It wasn’t faster. He could think a year’s worth in a week; she couldn’t; the pace of the thing was set by the slowest mind in the room, and the slowest mind in the room was a professor of mathematics, and it was still slower than him by a factor of two hundred, and he had spent the first month quietly frantic about it, and then the second month understanding why it didn’t matter.

It didn’t matter because she could see things he couldn’t. Not faster. Differently. She had thirty years of knowing where the bodies were buried in six fields he had read but never lived in, and when he came at the problem down a road that looked clear to him she could say, without looking up, “That’s the Hungarian trap, Harry, three people have died in it, go round,” and he would go round, and the road he hadn’t seen would be the one. He had a thousand years of thought. She had a map. And a map, it turned out, was worth more than most of a thousand years, if the years had all been spent in one cupboard.

So on the Tuesday in November, with the rain coming down, he was at the board, and she was in the chair with her feet on the paper stack, and he was two-thirds of the way through a construction they had built together over the autumn and had both believed, for a month, would finally hold — and he stopped.

“Say it,” said Carrow.

“It doesn’t hold.”

“Where.”

“Here. The step from the third case to the general one. I’ve been assuming the pieces don’t overlap. They can overlap. I’ve just seen how.” He drew it. “Like that. They slide past each other.”

Carrow took her feet off the paper and came to the board and looked at it for a long moment, and said something under her breath that he had not heard her say before and that he filed, with interest, for later.

“Yes,” she said. “Yes, damn it. They do.” She took the chalk out of his hand. “All right. Then we don’t go through the third case. We go—” She stopped. The chalk stopped. He watched her face do a thing he had been waiting thirteen months to see it do, and the thing was the ice: the world going vast and slow and bright, except that it was happening to someone else, at one-to-one, and he could see every frame of it. “We go under. If they can slide past each other, they can be made to. Harry — if we force them to overlap, on purpose, all of them, the whole way down—”

“—then the overlap is the thing we count,” said Harry, “not the pieces,” and he had it, and she had it, at the same moment, two people standing in front of a board in the rain with their mouths slightly open, and neither of them said anything for a full three seconds, which for him was most of a morning, and he spent it looking at Helen Carrow’s face and thinking that he had never in his life seen anything so entirely worth the price of admission.

It took them till seven to write it down so that it held. She would not let him leave before it held; she rang the Dursleys herself, and spoke to Vernon in the voice she used for the head of faculty, and Vernon said of course, Professor, and she put the phone down and said, “That man would sign anything,” and went back to the board. When it held she stood back and looked at it, nine feet of chalk and the back of a door, and took her glasses off, and said quite quietly, “Seven years,” and then, in the ordinary voice, “Right. We write it up. Properly. Both names.”

“Carrow and Potter,” said Harry.

“Alphabetical. It’s the convention and I don’t hold with fiddling it.” She put the glasses back on and looked at him over them. “You understand what this is. It’s a paper. In a journal. With your name on it. Not the Surrey Advertiser. The real thing — the conversation, the one I told you about, the one that goes back a hundred years. You’ll be in it. At ten.” A pause. “They won’t believe it, you know. The referees. They’ll think I’m having a joke.”

“Then we’d better make the proof very good,” said Harry.

Carrow laughed — the second time he had heard it; he kept a tally — and threw him his coat.

The paper went off in January. The referees did not believe it, and said so, politely, in a report Carrow read aloud to him with great relish, and then went through the proof line by line and found it held, and said that too, less politely, in the particular grudging prose of mathematicians who have been made to change their minds. It appeared in April. Eleven pages. H. Carrow and H. Potter, Royal Holloway and Bedford New College, University of London. He held the offprint in the office with the rain on the windows again and read his own name in the font of a journal that had been in print since 1865 and thought about Mr Prentice’s margins, and the reader in fifty years, and found that he was not, for once, thinking about the far side at all.

“It gets easier,” said Carrow, watching him. “The first one. You think it’s the thing. It isn’t the thing. It’s the start of the thing.” She took the offprint back and put it on the stack. “Now. There’s a problem I’ve been stuck on since 1979 that I think you ought to see.”

The feather held on the second of February, in the cupboard, at a quarter to midnight, and it was the first time his magic had ever looked like theirs.

He had been working towards it since the summer. Grade 1, chapter four: the Levitation Charm, Wingardium Leviosa, swish and flick, the single most-practised charm in the first-year curriculum, according to Waffling, who disapproved of it. He had read the description and found the sentence under it in July and had known at once that it was a thing he could not do — not for want of understanding; he had the sentence whole, could write it in the blue notebook cleaner than the book described it — but because he had no wand, and the wand, he had worked out that autumn from Waffling and the grey book and his own two years of pencil-and-candle, was not a symbol or a tradition or a stick. It was a tool. A specific one. The thing in him made sentences and sent them; the sending cost; the cost, for a pencil, had been like carrying it up a hill. A wand — he was nearly sure of it now; he had the chapter half-written — was what you sent the sentence through, so that it arrived with most of its strength instead of a tenth of it. He had been shouting across a field for two years. They had a telephone.

But he wanted to know how far the shouting could carry, if the sentence was perfect. His own sentences never had been; they were rough-cut, paths not roads. Theirs were polished. So he spent the autumn and the whole of January learning to hold their sentence — not his version, theirs, every mark of it, the way they had sanded it — and to build the purpose true, which took, for a feather, the same honesty it had taken for a pencil, so that I know whether I can; and on the second of February, with a feather from the pillow on the camp bed and his hand flat beside it and the house asleep, he held Wingardium Leviosa in his head the way he would have held a note in singing, and sent it, and the feather rose.

An inch. Then two. And then — this was the thing, this was what he had been working six months for — it stopped. Not fell. Stopped, two inches above the blanket, and hung there, perfectly still, turning very slightly in the air from the cupboard door, and held, while he held the sentence, for four seconds, which for him was the better part of a quarter of an hour of looking at a feather hanging in the dark doing exactly, precisely, to the hair, what he had told it to.

He let it down. He was shaking, and not from the cost, though the cost was real and he slept till the boiler.

Because he understood, lying there, what he had just seen. Not that he could do it — he had known since the pencil he could do something. That it had held. The book said the feather would rise and the student would struggle to control it; Waffling had a whole sour paragraph on first-years whose feathers shot into the ceiling or wandered off sideways; the grey book, in 1911, had said that imprecision in the casting moment is the universal experience and may be regarded as a law. And his feather had not wandered. His feather had gone two inches and stopped, and turned slightly in the draught, and stayed, because he had held the sentence steady for the whole of a casting moment that nobody else could even see, and so there was nothing in it to wander.

They could not do that. He was increasingly sure of it. Not the children in the book; not, he suspected, most of the adults. They had a telephone and he had none, and they could send a hundred times what he could — but they sent it with their eyes shut, in a quarter-second they could not watch, and the sentence went out ragged, and the feather shot into the ceiling. When he had a wand, he would be sending with his eyes open.

He wrote that down at the end of the chapter he had been writing all autumn, the one that began where the grey book had stopped, and he gave the chapter a title, because he had learned from Carrow that a result should be named, and the title was On Holding. Then under it, smaller, in the round hand, for the reader in fifty years:

This is what the two hundred is for. Not the maths. Not the chess. I have been reading sentences nobody else can see since I was seven, and in six months I will be given a telephone. I do not think anyone on the far side has any idea what happens when you give one of those to someone who can hold.

I intend to find out carefully.

He asked Mrs Figg about Dumbledore in April, in the garden, with the cats, because he had read every line about the man that was in print on either side of the wall and found that none of it told him the one thing he wanted to know.

“What’s he like?”

She didn’t pretend not to know who he meant. She had stopped pretending anything, since the summer; the gates in her were all on the latch now, and some of them open.

“Kind,” she said, and then stopped, as if the word had come out first and she was checking it. “Yes. Kind. Everyone says great, and he is, I suppose, I wouldn’t know; but what I’d say is kind, and I’d say it first. He wrote to me when my brother died. Three pages. He’d met Edgar twice.” She snipped. “And he’s sure. That’s the other thing. He thinks a long way ahead and he’s sure, and mostly he’s right, and when he’s wrong he’s wrong for a long time before anyone can tell him, because who’s going to.” She looked at Harry sideways. “You’re going to ask him about the step.”

“Yes.”

“He knows that. I expect he’s known it nine years.” Snip. “He’ll have an answer. It’ll be a good one, and it’ll be true, and it won’t be the whole thing, and you’ll know that, and so will he. That’s what he’s like.” She dropped a stem in the trug. “I’ve a great deal of time for him, Harry, and I’ve told him he was wrong to my face twice in my life, and both times he listened, and both times he did it anyway. Make of that what you can.”

He made of it what he could, walking home. It was, he thought, the most useful thing anyone had told him about anyone.

A good answer, and true, and not the whole thing. He had built a childhood on reading the sides of people’s faces for the part they weren’t saying. He would be meeting, in a few months, a man who had been doing that for a hundred years, and was sure, and was kind, and had reasons. He did not think he could out-read him. He thought — turning into Privet Drive, past the garden gate, with the swing photograph and the grey book and a chapter called On Holding under the stairs — that he might not have to.

Because he would not be arriving as a child with a question. He would be arriving as somebody with a paper in a journal and a notebook of a language nobody spoke and three years of their curriculum read dry and a feather that held, and a List, and a very clear idea of what he was for. And a man who was kind and sure and thought a long way ahead would, he suspected, recognise another one when it walked into his office.

He hoped so. He found that he was looking forward to it the way he had looked forward to Carrow’s first problem — not as a test to pass, but as a floor to find, with someone.

The last page of the plan had a date at the top and a column of months underneath it, crossed off one by one in the blue pen since a garden bench in June of 1989, and on the first of May he crossed off April, and there were three left.

He turned back through the pad, the whole of it, from the five-part plan with its four ticks to Faster to the date at the top of a page in a cupboard, and he read it all, which took him no time at all and a long while, and then he wrote underneath, in English, the thing he had, because Carrow had taught him that you took stock before you went on.

Grades 1 to 3 read dry. Every sentence in them written in the blue book and compared to mine. Theory to the end of what I can buy. One chapter of my own, On Holding, that I think is new. A paper in a journal on this side with my name on it. A photograph of my mother on a swing. The name of the school and the name of the man. The street, the bank, the pub, the knock on the wall — and the hand on the back of the head, which I can decline. A feather that holds.

Three months.

I have not wasted them. I am not going to start.

Then he went down to the lounge in his socks, because it was ten past one, and a man in a brown jacket was about to explain the structure of the atom to nobody, and Harry Potter, ten years and nine months old by the hall clock and some eighteen hundred by his own, sat down on the carpet eighteen inches from the screen with the sound at two, for very nearly the last time, and listened.
