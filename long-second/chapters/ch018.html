Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 18

The Thirty-First

He did not sleep, and he had known he would not, and he had decided what the year would be for.

He spent the first part of it going through the plan — the whole of it, from be small in a shoebox to the five parts on a kitchen table to Faster to the paper on the door with its date and its four lines underneath — and he did not change a word, and he found when he had finished that there was nothing in it he was ashamed of, which he had not been sure of going in. He spent the second part on the biscuit tin: the four hundred and twelve pages, the forty sentences, the asking, the seal, until he could have written any of them in the dark, which he could anyway. And the third part he spent on nothing. Lying under the stairs with the trunk at his feet and the swing on the wall and the house ticking round him, in the long quiet that had been the whole of his life, doing nothing at all, on purpose, for the last time in the smallest office in England.

At half past five he heard the kitchen light go on.

He got up. He went through in his socks, and Petunia was standing at the sink in her dressing gown with the kettle not yet on, looking out at the garden, where there was nothing yet but grey, and she did not turn round, and he put the kettle on, and got two cups down, and neither of them said anything until it had boiled.

“They’ll come today,” she said.

“Yes.”

“A letter. It was a letter, for her. An owl, in the morning, on the step, and she screamed the house down.” She took the cup he gave her. “Your uncle doesn’t know what day it is.”

“I’ll manage Uncle Vernon.”

“You always do.” It was not quite a smile. “He thinks it’s a boarding school. He’s been telling the Rotary it’s a boarding school since the degree. I let him.”

“It is a boarding school.”

“Harry.” She looked at him for the first time that morning, over the cup, with the face with the wall out of it, and under the face he saw the thing that must have been there at eleven, in a town up north, watching a sister read a letter in green ink: not envy. The other side of it. The thing envy is the shadow of. “They’re going to take you somewhere I can’t follow. I know that. I’ve known it ten years. I’m telling you now, before they come, so that I’ve said it once and you’ve heard it: I’d have kept you. If there’d been no letter. If you’d been — ordinary, if a boy with a doctorate at ten can be called that. I’d have kept you, badly, and you’d have gone to Egham every day with that woman and her cats, and I’d have put the cuttings in my purse, and it would have been a life.” She put the cup down. “I wanted you to know there was a version where I kept you. That’s all.”

He stood in the kitchen with his own cup going cold and found, for the third time in his life, that he had nothing ready.

“I know,” he said. “Aunt Petunia. I know. I’ll come back in the summers. For the roof.” And then, because it was the morning it was: “And for the tea.”

Petunia Dursley made the sound that was not a laugh, and turned back to the garden, and said, “Lay the table. He’ll be down at seven and he’ll want the paper,” and that was all, and it was a great deal.

The doorbell rang at nine o’clock exactly, and he had known it would, and he was already in the hall.

Behind him, in the kitchen, Vernon said “Who the devil calls at nine on a—” and stopped, because Petunia had said his name in a voice Harry had never heard her use, low and final, like a door being locked from the inside. Dudley, at the table, home from Smeltings for the summer and twelve and enormous, went on eating toast and watched the hall with the expression Harry had filed at the ceremony as interest, and which had, over the summer, been getting slowly more frequent and no less surprising.

He opened the door.

The woman on the step was tall, and severe, and dressed from collar to hem in a tartan he did not know the name of, with a pointed hat squarely on her head and square spectacles on her nose, and she was looking down at him with an expression he had seen before, on Dr Prosser and Mrs Prentice and Professor Carrow and Professor Aldous of Cambridge and no one else: a professional, declining to be impressed until given cause.

“Good morning,” said the woman. Her voice was Scottish, clipped, and gave nothing away. “My name is Professor McGonagall. I am the Deputy Headmistress of Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. I have come about Harry Potter.”

“Good morning, Professor,” said Harry. “That’s me. Please come in. I’ve been expecting you.”

The eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch. He had learned from Mr Haines what that meant and what it cost.

“Expecting me.”

“Since the last Saturday in March, 1986. The date, I mean, not you in particular — I didn’t know who they’d send. I hoped it would be someone who’d look like something.” He stepped back from the door. “The lounge is on the left. My uncle is going to shout for about ninety seconds and then stop. I’d be grateful if you’d let me handle him; I’ve had five years’ practice and it’ll be quicker.”

Professor McGonagall stood on the step of Number Four, Privet Drive, in her tartan, with a letter in her hand addressed in green ink, and looked at the small boy in the doorway for a long moment — three seconds; most of a morning — and he watched her decide, the way he had watched Carrow decide over forty pages at a long table, to take the thing she had been given and see where it went.

“Very well, Dr Potter,” she said. “Lead on.”

He had not expected the Doctor. He put it on the shelf, walking ahead of her into the lounge, and found it had gone in warm.

Vernon shouted for seventy-one seconds. Harry timed it.

It was not the shouting he had expected. He had modelled, over five years, a Vernon who roared we swore when we took him in we’d stamp it out — Petunia had told him, at a kitchen table, what had been said in 1981, and he had built his handling round it. But five years of Rotary dinners had done something to Vernon Dursley that no amount of stamping could undo. He had boasted. He had bought every copy the newsagent had. He had told Marge to take her bad blood to the Daily Mail. He had sat in a hall with long windows and heard a Vice-Chancellor say Doctor in front of nine countries and had gone home and told the man from the garage. Vernon Dursley had spent five years building, in public, in front of everyone he knew, the character of a man whose nephew was a genius, and he could no more tear it down in front of a stranger in a hat than he could have returned the frames to the shop.

So the shouting was confused. It wanted to be get out of my house and kept turning, halfway through, into and what sort of school did you say, and Harry stood on the lounge carpet between his uncle and the Deputy Headmistress of Hogwarts and let it run for seventy-one seconds, and then said, in the voice he had used on Dudley’s tables:

“Uncle Vernon. It’s a boarding school. It’s very old — older than Eton — and it takes about forty children a year in the whole country, and you can’t apply, you have to be chosen, and it chose me. Mr Pargeter’s trust will pay for anything the school doesn’t. You won’t be out of pocket.” He let that land. “And you can tell the Rotary it’s in Scotland. That part’s true.”

Vernon stood in the lounge, purple, with his mouth open, and Harry watched the two Vernons fight it out behind his face — the one from 1981 and the one from the Rotary — and it was no contest, and had not been for four years, and the Rotary won on points inside three seconds.

“Chosen,” said Vernon.

“Chosen, Mr Dursley,” said Professor McGonagall, who had been watching this with an expression Harry could not read and was beginning to suspect he would enjoy learning to. “Hogwarts does not advertise.”

“Hm,” said Vernon. He sat down in his chair. He looked at the wall — the chess cup, the fifteen letters, the two scrolls, the nine countries — and Harry watched him do the sum he had been doing for five years, and arrive, as he always did, at the same comfortable total. “Well. I suppose. If it’s exclusive.”

“I have never heard it called anything else,” said McGonagall drily, and Harry understood that he was going to like her very much, and that it was going to take her some time to decide whether she liked him, and that both of those things were correct.

Petunia had not sat down. She stood in the lounge doorway with her arms folded, in the dressing gown still, and looked at the tartan, and the hat, and the letter, and her face did not do the white thing, not once, and when McGonagall glanced at her — a brief look, professional, taking the measure — Petunia Dursley met it and held it and said, flatly:

“You’ll be wanting to talk to him. Not to us. Dudley, upstairs.”

“But I—”

“Upstairs, Dudley.”

Dudley went, with his toast, looking back at the hat, and at the top of the stairs he stopped and said, not loudly, down the well of the hall, “Good luck, Harry,” and went into his room and shut the door, and Harry stood very still for a quarter of a second, which was most of a minute, because in ten years it was the first time his cousin had ever wished him anything, and he put it on the shelf next to the Doctor, and found that it too had gone in warm.

Vernon, after a moment, with great dignity, took the paper and went into the kitchen and shut the door. Petunia stood a second longer in the doorway, looking at Harry, and said, “I’ll be in the garden,” and went, and the lounge was empty but for a witch and a boy and a wall full of frames.

McGonagall looked at the frames for a long moment before she sat down.

“Your uncle,” she said, “appears to have changed his mind about a number of things, Dr Potter.”

“I changed it for him. It took five years.” He sat in the other chair. “He had to be proud of me in public before you came, or he’d have thrown you out. So I made sure he was. I’m sorry about the seventy-one seconds. I’d hoped for sixty.”

“You timed it.”

“I time everything, Professor. It’s a habit.” He took a folded sheet from his pocket and smoothed it on his knee. “I have four hundred and three questions. Most of them are for the Headmaster, in order, and I’ll keep them for him. Eleven of them are for you, because they’re about the school and you run it. May I?”

She let him ask the eleven, and answered nine, and the two she did not answer she said so plainly — that is a matter for the Headmaster and I do not know, and I will not pretend to — and he put a mark beside each and moved on, and somewhere around the third question she stopped looking at him like a woman delivering a letter and started looking at him like a woman conducting an interview, which was what he had wanted.

Was the protection on this house what he thought it was — anchored to his aunt and to his calling the place home, both or neither, and would it hold through the school year if he came back in the summers? She went still at that. She asked how he knew. He said he had read it, from the step, four mornings in October of 1989, with the milk going cold, and that it was the best thing he had ever seen anyone write, and that he would like to know who had written it so that he could say so. She said, after a moment, that the matter of the protection was for the Headmaster, and that she had been promised the whole of it herself only last month, and that she would see to it that he was told. He marked it and went on.

What, precisely, did the Trace detect, and had it ever detected anything at Number Four? It detected magic in the vicinity of the underage, she said, and sent a letter; and no, it had not; she had checked, that week, because it had occurred to her to. He said that was what he had thought, and that he had a provisional theory about why, and that he would rather not lean on it until someone who knew more had looked at it, and would she? She said she would.

Did she know where his parents were buried? She did. Godric’s Hollow. A churchyard, in the West Country, with a white stone. She said it quietly, and then she said — not as a Deputy Headmistress; he could hear the difference — that she had known them both, taught them both, and that if he wished, one day, when he was ready, she would take him. He put a mark beside that one that was not like the others, and said thank you, and did not trust himself to say more, and she let it go.

Did the school have a library, and what was in it, and were there books in it that bit? It did; a great deal; and yes, some of them, and he was to leave those to the librarian, who was called Madam Pince and had opinions. Pince, he thought, and put it with the names.

Did the school teach anyone how a spell was made? There was a pause before that one. Not in the ordinary curriculum, she said. There were books. There was a Charms master who thought about it more than most. There had been, long ago, a man called Blishwick. He said he had Blishwick, and had written in the margin, and she said, “Yes. I had heard,” in a voice that told him she had heard a great deal, and he decided not to ask how, because she would tell him when she chose, and because he had learned from Carrow that a person who has decided to stand to one side of a door should not be pushed through it.

And then the tenth, which was the one he most wanted to ask and had kept for tenth on purpose, because he had learned from the viva that the real question came after the examiners had stopped going for the throat.

“Professor. I have a design. It’s on paper; I can’t build it; I don’t have a — I don’t have a wand. It’s a thing that would take a sentence — a charm — and put it into an object, and then put it into other objects, the same way, over and over, like a seal. A press. I think it would work. I think nobody has built one because nobody could hold the first copy steady enough, and I think I can, and I’ve written down why.” He kept his voice level, because it was the sentence he had built on the train from Egham a hundred times and he wanted it to carry. “The first thing I would put in it is a warming charm I know. One that checks how cold a person is and gives exactly the difference. And the first place I would want to put the blankets is a children’s hospital in London, where a girl of six had her photograph in a newspaper two years ago because the ward was cold at night and the heating was a line in a budget.” He looked at her. “I have read the Statute of Secrecy. The whole of it. The preamble and the 1692 pamphlets. I do not think it allows that. I want to know whether I have read it right, and I want to know before I do anything, not after, because I have a rule about that. Have I read it right?”

The lounge was very quiet.

Minerva McGonagall, Deputy Headmistress of Hogwarts, who had delivered letters to Muggle-raised children for thirty-four years and had believed, until nine o’clock that morning, that she had seen every possible version of the conversation that followed, sat in a chair in Little Whinging with her hands folded on her tartan knee and looked at a boy of eleven — eleven today — who had just asked her, formally, in order, with reasons, whether he was permitted to warm a Muggle hospital.

“Yes,” she said at last. “You have read it right. As it stands, it does not allow that.”

“I thought not.”

“As it stands.” She said it with great precision, the way one reports an instrument reading one has decided to believe. “Dr Potter, the Statute of Secrecy has been amended eleven times since 1692. Each time, it was amended because someone brought the Wizengamot a case it had not imagined when it wrote the clause. I have never, in thirty-four years, heard a case it had not imagined put more plainly than you have just put one to me, in a lounge, before breakfast.” She unfolded her hands. “I cannot tell you it is allowed. I will tell you that the Headmaster sits on the Wizengamot, and that you have four hundred and three questions for him, and that I think you had better move that one rather higher up the list than tenth.”

“It’s first,” said Harry. “After the step. The step is first. Then this.”

“Yes,” said McGonagall. “I rather thought it might be.” And then, because she had asked properly and listened properly, and because she had a question of her own and he had seen it coming since she sat down: “Dr Potter. I have one question that is not on anybody’s list, and I have been asked to ask it, and to listen to the answer, and not to arrive with my own in my pocket.”

She took the letter from her robes, and turned it, and held it so he could see the green ink of the address, and the second line.

“The Quill that writes these does not make mistakes about rooms. It writes where a child sleeps.”

He looked at the green line. The Cupboard under the Stairs. He had wondered, since Hogwarts: A History, whether the letter would know, and had decided it probably would, and had decided also what he would say, and he said it.

“It’s mine,” he said. “I took it when I was three. Not — I wasn’t put there, Professor, or not the way that line makes it sound. There was a box room, and there still is, and I could have it tomorrow if I asked; my uncle would give it me with a bow, now. I don’t want it. The cupboard has a door that shuts and a light with its own switch and nobody has opened it in eight years except me. I learned to read in there. I wrote a thesis in there. There’s a biscuit tin under the bed with four hundred pages in it that I’d not have written anywhere with a window. It’s the only room in this house that was ever mine, and I chose it, and I’d be grateful if you didn’t make it into something it wasn’t.” He stopped. Then, because she had asked properly, and because he had made a rule at a kitchen table about the truth: “It was what it looks like, when I was two. Before I chose it. I won’t pretend otherwise. But I was two for a very short time, Professor, and I’ve been the person who chose it for a great deal longer, and I’d like to be judged as him.”

McGonagall did not say anything for a while.

She looked at the letter, and at him, and then — this surprised him; he had not modelled it — at the frames on the wall again, the chess cup and the fifteen letters and the two scrolls and the nine countries, and he saw her put the two things side by side, the green line and the frames, and hold them, and not resolve them, which he thought was exactly right and which almost nobody could do.

“I will not make it into anything,” she said at last. “I will report what you have said, in your words, to the one person who needs to hear it. He asked me to ask you, and to listen, and I have, and I will tell him that you answered like—” she paused, and he saw her choose “—like a person who had thought about the question before I arrived, which I am beginning to think is going to be the shape of my year.” She put the letter down on the arm of the chair between them. “It is yours. You have not opened it.”

“I wanted to ask the questions first. In case the letter changed them.”

“Open it, Dr Potter.”

He opened it. He read it — the parchment, the crest, Dear Mr Potter, We are pleased to inform you — in the time it took her to blink, and then he read it again at the speed of his hand turning the page, for the pleasure of it, and then he looked at the second sheet, the list, Uniform, Set Books, Other Equipment, and he read down the set books, and stopped, and something happened at the corner of his mouth that he did not try to prevent.

“What is it?”

“I have all of these,” said Harry. “The books. Mrs Figg took me. Four times. You’ll have — I expect you’ll have had the receipts.”

“I have had,” said Professor McGonagall, “the receipts.” And for the first time since she had stood on the step, the severe mouth did something that he would spend a good part of the next seven years learning to provoke on purpose. “I have also had a thesis, which I did not understand, and a children’s book, which I did, and two pages on a subject I will not name in a lounge, which I understood rather better than I should have liked. I know what you have, Dr Potter. I came to find out what you are, and I have been asked to find that out by looking, and I have looked, and I am going to need to sit down somewhere with a very strong cup of tea before I attempt to put any of it into a report.” She stood, and smoothed the tartan. “In the meantime I believe you have some shopping to finish. You have the books. You do not have a wand. You do not, I think, have robes, or a cauldron, or the smallest idea what is in your vault, and I have a key.”

“A vault.”

“Your parents did not leave you poor.” She looked at the frames one last time, and then at the door through which his aunt had gone to the garden, and her voice changed, very slightly, into something he had heard once before in that lounge, that morning, from the other woman in the house. “And Dr Potter. Many happy returns. I knew your mother. She would have opened the letter first and asked the questions after. You are — I think — rather more like your father in that one respect, and nothing whatever like either of them in all the others, and I find I do not know yet what to make of that, and I intend to find out.”

“Thank you, Professor,” said Harry, and went to fetch his coat, and in the hall, out of sight, he stood for one second — a working afternoon — with his hand on the hook and his eyes shut and the letter in his pocket and the swing in his inside pocket and let the morning catch up with him, and then he opened his eyes and went out to the garden to tell his aunt he was going to the shops.

She was deadheading the roses by the fence. She did not look up.

“Well?” she said.

“Scottish,” said Harry. “Tartan. She knew Mum.”

Petunia’s secateurs paused.

“Go on, then,” she said, to the roses. “Tea’s at six.” And then, as he turned to go, still to the roses, in the voice from half past five: “Harry. She called you Doctor. At the door. I heard her.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” said Petunia Dursley, and snipped a stem, and dropped it in the trug, and did not say anything else, and did not need to.
