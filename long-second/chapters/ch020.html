Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 20

Diagon Alley, Properly

“How did you travel, last July?”

“The train, Professor. From Little Whinging to Waterloo and then the Northern line.”

“Then we shall take the train,” said Professor McGonagall, and did, in the tartan, with the pointed hat in a carrier bag from a shop that had closed in 1979, and sat for twenty-two minutes in a second-class carriage on a Wednesday morning being looked at by every person who got on, and looked back at each of them until they stopped, and Harry sat opposite her with his hands in his lap and did not say a word, because he had decided somewhere around Clapham Junction that this was the most entertaining journey of his life and he was not going to spoil it.

“You are enjoying this,” she said, at Vauxhall, without turning from the window.

“Yes, Professor.”

“Hm.” A pause. “Your mother would have enjoyed it too. She had no respect whatever for a Floo.”

He put that on the shelf with the others. It was getting to be a long shelf.

The Leaky Cauldron was different without the cap.

He had known it would be. He had modelled it on the train — the bowing man, the glad faces, the paragraph in A History of Magic — and had decided there was nothing to be done but walk in, and he walked in, a half-step behind the tartan, and the toothless barman looked up from his glass and his rag, and saw McGonagall, and nodded, and saw Harry, and the rag stopped.

“Bless my soul,” said the barman, quite softly. “Professor — is that—”

“It is,” said McGonagall, in a voice that had closed staffroom doors for thirty years. “And he has a good deal of shopping to do, Tom, and a train to catch home, and I will take it as a personal kindness if the room lets him do it.”

The room did not entirely let him. He had not expected it to. Chairs scraped. An old woman with a long pipe stood up and simply looked at him, both hands pressed flat to her chest, and said nothing, and sat down again. A man in a purple top hat — the man, the violet hat, five years older and exactly as small — came across the floor at a half-run and seized Harry’s hand in both of his and said, “Mr Potter! Dedalus Diggle! You won’t remember — in Little Whinging — the bus—”

“I remember,” said Harry. “You bowed. My aunt bought my cousin a second ice lolly, which was against the rules.” He shook the hand that was shaking his. “Thank you. It was the first time anyone was glad to see me. I’ve thought about it a lot since.”

Dedalus Diggle stood with his mouth open and his hat in his hand and his eyes going wet, and McGonagall said, “Dedalus,” in a tone that put him back in his chair as if on a string, and steered Harry through the room with one hand not quite on his shoulder, and out into the little walled yard, and shut the door.

“You remembered his bow.”

“I remember everything, Professor. It’s—” he hesitated, because it was the first time he had come anywhere near the edge of the real thing with anyone on this side, and then stepped back from it, because it was not yet time and because she would ask how “—it’s a habit I have. He was glad to see me. I was six. People weren’t, generally.”

McGonagall looked at him in the yard, with the bins and the weeds and the wall, for a moment longer than the exchange had warranted.

“Three up, two across,” she said, and took out her wand, and he watched the wall say its sentence again, every brick, and this time he did not try to hide that he was watching, and she saw him watch, and said nothing, and filed it, and they went through.

She took him to the bank first, which he had known she would, and the bank was the same white building and the same bronze doors and the same goblins in scarlet, and this time the goblin on the left looked at him, and at the tartan, and stepped back.

Not a bow. Nothing so vulgar. A quarter-pace, the way one steps back for a thing that has a right of way. Harry noticed, and knew why, and found he liked the contempt better.

The goblin at the counter this time was older, with a pair of spectacles on a chain, and McGonagall put a small gold key on the marble and said, “Mr Potter’s vault. He will want to make a withdrawal,” and the goblin took the key and looked at it, and at Harry, and said, “Griphook,” to the air, and a younger goblin appeared, and Harry — who had been in this hall before, under a cap, with a hundred and eighty-four pounds inside his shirt — said, before he could stop himself:

“Is the exchange counter still at the far end? I was here last July. A goblin with a long nose counted my money twice.”

The older goblin’s spectacles came up.

“Bogrod,” he said, after a moment. “He counts everyone’s money twice. He considers it a courtesy.” And then — a flicker, the professional flicker, the thing Harry had seen once before in this hall and had filed as the finest compliment Little Whinging had never paid him — “You changed Muggle money. Last July. Alone.”

“Yes. A hundred and eighty-four pounds. Thirty-six galleons, fourteen sickles and nineteen knuts, less four galleons I have left.” He had it in his pocket. He put it on the marble. “I’d like to deposit these, please, before I draw anything. They were the first money I ever had on this side and I’d rather they were in the vault than in my shirt.”

There was a silence at the counter. Griphook looked at the older goblin. The older goblin looked at the four galleons, and then at the boy, over the spectacles, for a long moment — three seconds — and Harry, who had spent eleven years reading the sides of faces, could not read this one at all, and understood that goblins had been doing to wizards for a thousand years what he had been doing to the Dursleys for eleven, and that he was in the presence of professionals.

“Noted,” said the goblin, and swept the four galleons into a drawer, and wrote a line in a ledger, and the line was longer than four galleons warranted. “Griphook. The vault.”

The cart went down into the dark at a speed that would have been alarming to anyone who could not see it coming, and Harry could see it coming, every turn, and spent the ride instead looking at the walls — rock, old, and under the rock, faint, the hum of the street outside turned up until it was almost a sound, the whole mountain under London full of sentences a thousand years deep — and when the cart stopped and Griphook opened a door with the key and the green smoke cleared, he stood in the doorway of a vault full of gold and did not go in for a moment.

Mounds of it. Columns of silver. Heaps of little bronze. He had read vault on McGonagall’s lips in the lounge and written it beside four galleons and had not, he found, believed it until now.

He thought about a toffee tin under a returns desk. Nineteen pounds and twelve pence, in pencil, on the back of a catalogue card. A hundred and six pounds to the famine in an envelope. Mr Pargeter’s bowling-green voice saying such other expenses as the trustees shall in their discretion deem conducive. Eleven years of counting, of shoes, of the far-side half kept in a tin for a reason written down, and here it had been the whole time, under London, in the dark, waiting for a key.

“Mr Potter,” said McGonagall, behind him. “Is something the matter?”

“No, Professor.” He went in. He took what the list needed and what he had worked out on the train that a year would need, and no more, and counted it into the bag Griphook handed him, and did the sum in sickles aloud so that Griphook would hear him do it right, and Griphook heard, and said nothing, and that was the second compliment of the morning.

“The money problem of my life,” said Harry, on the way back up, mostly to himself, with the dark going past, “just ended in about a minute. I’d planned for it to take until I was twenty-one.” He looked at his hands. “I don’t quite know what to do with the plan.”

“Keep it,” said McGonagall, unexpectedly. “In my experience, Mr Potter, the people who have had to count are the only ones who can be trusted not to.”

Ollivander’s was narrow and dim and smelled of dust and something under the dust, like the kitchen floor at Mrs Figg’s turned up loud, and he had been looking forward to it for three years and found, on the step, that his heart was going as hard as it had at the pub.

A telephone. Three years of shouting across a field, and behind this door was a telephone.

The old man came out of the back of the shop like something surfacing, with pale eyes that did not blink enough and a voice like paper, and said, “Ah. Yes. I thought I’d be seeing you soon, Harry Potter,” and then went on about Harry’s mother’s wand and his father’s, willow and mahogany, lengths and tempers, and Harry stood very still and listened to a man describe his parents as a pair of instruments — swishy, pliable, good for charm work — and put every word of it on the shelf, because nobody had ever given him that before, the shape of their hands.

Then the pale eyes went to his forehead.

“And that is where—”

“Yes,” said Harry. “I know what did it. I’d rather we got on, if you don’t mind, Mr Ollivander. I’ve read about the wand that did it in three books and I’d like to find out what mine is before I think about his.”

Ollivander looked at him for a long moment with the unblinking eyes, and something moved behind them that was not quite surprise.

“Yes,” he said softly. “Yes, I think that is the correct order.” And took out a tape measure that began to measure Harry by itself, and went to the shelves.

The trying took forty minutes by the shop’s clock and he loved it beyond reason, and he could not have explained to anyone why, except that it was the first time in his life anyone had handed him instruments, one after another, and said try. Beechwood and dragon heartstring. Maple and phoenix feather. Ebony and unicorn. He took each one, and lifted it, and Ollivander snatched each one back almost before he had, saying no, no, and Harry did not mind, because every one — every single one — had done something that nothing in eleven years had done.

They answered. Badly. Like a telephone with the line crackling, a wrong number, a voice on the far end that was not meant for him. But he felt it each time: the sentence he had been shouting across a field for three years going somewhere. Into the wood. Down the grain. Looking for a way out and not finding it, in the wrong wands, and finding a little of one in the nearly-right ones, and he held each nearly-right one a half-second longer than Ollivander wanted and listened, the way he had listened to the roses, and heard the shape of the line, and understood what Waffling had been fumbling towards for two hundred pages and what the grey book’s author had regretted he could not see.

The wand was not a symbol. It was a channel, exactly as he had written. And the channel had a grain, and the grain had to match the shape of the one sending, or the sentence went down it sideways and came out ragged or not at all, and that was why the feathers shot into the ceiling, and that was why it mattered which wand, and he stood in the middle of Ollivander’s shop with his thirty-first rejection in his hand and said, aloud, before he could stop himself:

“They have a grain.”

Ollivander, at the shelves, went still.

“I beg your pardon?”

“The wands. They — I’m sorry, Mr Ollivander, I don’t know your words for it. But each one has a shape inside it that the — the sending — has to fit. Like a key. The nearly-right ones, I can feel the sending go in and get stuck, partway, and come back. The wrong ones it doesn’t go in at all.” He looked at the ebony in his hand. “This one it goes in about a third and stops. It’s very close. It’s just the wrong — turn. I don’t know how to say it. The wrong turn.”

Ollivander did not say anything for a very long time, for a man who had been talking without pause for forty minutes.

“The wrong turn,” he said at last, in the paper voice, very quietly. “Yes. That is — that is not a bad word for it. I have been in this shop for seventy years, Mr Potter, and my father before me, and I have never once heard a customer describe the fit from the inside.” He turned back to the shelves, and his hand went, without looking, to a box high up and far back that Harry had the strong sense he had been leaving for last. “I wonder,” said Ollivander. “Yes. Why not. Holly and phoenix feather, eleven inches, nice and supple. An unusual combination. Try.”

Harry took it.

He had been waiting three years for a telephone and had thought he knew what one would feel like, and he did not. It was not a crackle. It was not a fit, even, the key-and-lock thing he had felt in the ebony. It was the thing on the ice, the morning of the milk: the warmth coming up through him and finding the way already open — a line that ran straight from the kitchen floor at Mrs Figg’s to the tip of a piece of holly, with no field between, no shouting, no loss — and he felt the sentence he had been holding in the back of his mind since the shop door, the simplest one he knew, the candle’s cousin, light, go down the grain of the wand like water down a pipe that had been built for it, and he did not let it out.

He held it.

He had learned to hold on a feather at a quarter to midnight in a cupboard, and he held now, with the whole shop watching, the sentence at the tip of the wand like a note in singing, and then he let it out slowly — not the shower of sparks that the books said, that the first-years did, that Ollivander was plainly waiting for — but a single steady point of white light, at the tip, that came up out of nothing like the candle had, and stood, and held, and did not waver, and lit the dust in the air of the shop so that every mote of it hung still and separate and bright, for four seconds, which was most of a quarter of an hour of looking at a thing he had made exactly as he had meant to make it.

Then he let it go. The light went out. The dust fell.

Nobody in the shop said anything.

McGonagall, by the door, had one hand flat against the frame. Ollivander stood at the counter with his pale eyes very wide and his mouth a little open, and for the first time since Harry had come in, he blinked.

“Curious,” said Ollivander, and his voice was not paper now; it was something with more in it. “Very — Mr Potter. That was not sparks.”

“No, sir.”

“A first touch always gives sparks. Always. The wand answers and the wizard cannot yet shape the answer, and so it comes out as — as noise. Joyful noise, but noise. I have seen it ten thousand times. I have never seen it—” he searched “—steady.”

“I’ve been practising holding,” said Harry. “Without one of these. For a year. I thought if I could hold with nothing, I could hold with something.” He looked at the wand in his hand. It was warm. It had, he thought, been waiting rather a long time. “I was right. It’s — Mr Ollivander, it’s so much easier with the telephone.”

“The — telephone.”

“It’s what I call it. Sorry. The channel. Whatever your word is.” He turned the holly over. “Thank you. This is the one. I knew the moment it went in. There wasn’t a turn at all.”

Ollivander came round the counter. He did it slowly, with the eyes still wide, and he stopped in front of Harry and looked at him — not the forehead this time; at him — the way Carrow had looked at the colouring problem in the rain, the way Dr Prosser had looked across a table of blocks, and Harry stood and let himself be looked at.

“Curious,” said Ollivander again, softly. “Curious. I shall tell you, Mr Potter, because I think it is the sort of thing you would rather know than not, and because I rather think you will find out anyway. The phoenix whose feather is in your wand gave one other. Just one. Thirteen and a half inches, yew. It is very curious indeed that you should be destined for this wand when its brother—” he stopped, and the pale eyes went, briefly, to the forehead and away “—when its brother gave you that scar.”

The shop was quiet.

Harry stood with the holly in his hand and let it land, which took a while by his own clock, and then he did the thing he had learned to do on a bench in the Alley a year ago with a reference book open on his knee: he picked it up, and looked at it carefully, and put it on the shelf marked when I am ready and not one day before, and found that the shelf held.

“Thank you for telling me,” he said. “You’re right. I’d rather know.” He looked at the wand, warm, eleven inches, no turn at all. “Its brother did what it did. This one just held a light for four seconds in a shop. I think I’ll judge it on that, for now, and see how it goes.”

Ollivander looked at him a moment longer. Then, to Harry’s considerable surprise, he bowed — a small one, from the neck, the bow of a craftsman to someone who has understood his craft — and said, “Seven galleons, Mr Potter. And I think we may expect great things. I have said that to a great many customers, over seventy years, and I find I have never before said it with so little idea what the things will be.”

Madam Malkin’s took ten minutes and he said nothing in it worth recording. The cauldron, the scales, the phials, the telescope took twenty. Flourish and Blotts he would have been happy to spend the day in, and McGonagall knew it, and gave him forty minutes, during which he read every spine on the shelves he had not reached last July, and bought the set books he did not have, and Grades Four to Seven of Standard Spells over her raised eyebrow — “The grammar is what I want, Professor; three years of it was the price of a good dinner and four more is a bargain” — and two books on theory with dust on them, and at the till the young woman with ink on her fingers looked up and said:

“The margins boy! You came back!” And then, seeing the tartan, and the scar, and doing the sum in a second the way the whole street had been doing it since the pub: “Oh. Oh. You’re—”

“I was the margins boy first,” said Harry. “I’d rather be that one in here, if you don’t mind. Did anyone else ever buy the grey one? The 1911?”

“Nobody. Not in the whole year.”

“Good,” said Harry, and paid, and McGonagall, behind him, made a small sound that he would spend seven years learning was the nearest she came in public to a laugh.

The owl he chose himself. It was not on the list, and she said so, and he said he had made a promise to a physics teacher that he would write, and that there was a post from everywhere and it was only a question of stamps, and she looked at him over the spectacles and said nothing further, and he went into Eeylops alone and came out with a snowy owl in a cage who regarded him with the exact expression of a cat on a wall in Wisteria Walk, and whom he named, that night, out of A History of Magic, Hedwig, because the Hedwig in the book had been a woman who could not be told anything, and he had a feeling.

“Mr Potter,” said McGonagall, outside the Leaky Cauldron at half past four, in the Charing Cross Road, with the pointed hat back in its carrier bag and the holly in his pocket and the owl in his hand and the Saturday crowd going round them like water round a stone, “I shall see you on the first of September. The ticket is in your letter. Platform nine and three-quarters, which does not exist, and you will find it anyway, I have no doubt, by declining.” The mouth did its thing. “I have one more question. It is not on anybody’s list.”

“Yes, Professor?”

“The light. In the shop. Four seconds, steady, on a first touch.” She looked at him with the square spectacles catching the afternoon, and her voice was not the Deputy Headmistress’s. “Mr Potter, I have taught Charms-adjacent transfiguration for thirty-four years and I have examined some two thousand first-years, and what you did in that shop is not possible. I do not say it is unusual. I say that I have, until half past twelve today, believed it to be impossible, for anyone, at any age, with any wand. I should like to know — not how. I do not think you will tell me how, and I will not ask. I should like to know whether you knew it was impossible.”

He thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how to think about anything by then.

“Yes,” he said. “I read it in a book from 1911. The man said it couldn’t be done and apologised for it. I wrote not quite in the margin.” He shifted the cage to his other hand. “I’m sorry, Professor. I know it’s going to make your year complicated. I’ve been trying to think of a way to make it less so and I haven’t found one yet. But I promise you I’ll be careful, and I promise you I’ll tell you before I do anything I think might be impossible again, so that at least you’re not finding out in a shop.”

Minerva McGonagall looked at the Boy Who Lived on the pavement of the Charing Cross Road for a long moment, with her hand on the strap of a carrier bag from a shop that had closed in 1979.

“Tea at six,” she said at last. “Your aunt told me. Go and catch your train.”

The Headmaster’s office, Hogwarts, that evening

“Albus.”

“Minerva. Sit down. You have the face.”

“I have several faces. I have been accumulating them since nine o’clock this morning and I have not yet decided which to wear.” She did not sit. She put the file on his desk, and on top of the file she put a sheet of her own notes in green ink, closely written on both sides, and she stood over it with her hands flat on the desk and said: “You asked me for two things. Whether he is happy and whether he is kind. I will give you those first, because they are the simple ones.”

“Please.”

“He is happy. Not content — happy. He enjoyed the train. He enjoyed my being stared at. He laughed at a bookseller. He went into a vault full of more gold than he will spend in his life and stood in the door of it for four seconds and the thing on his face was not greed, Albus, it was grief, for something — I did not ask, I will find out — and then he took exactly what a year needs and counted it aloud in sickles so the goblin would hear him do it right.” She breathed. “And he is kind. He asked the goblin’s name. He remembered Dedalus Diggle’s bow, from five years ago, and thanked him for it, and Dedalus wept. He bought an owl because he promised a Muggle physics teacher he would write. He asked Ollivander to tell him about his own wand before the one that killed his parents, and when Ollivander told him about the feather anyway, he said — I have it here — its brother did what it did; this one just held a light for four seconds in a shop; I think I’ll judge it on that.”

Dumbledore had gone very still at feather.

“He knows about the brother wand.”

“Ollivander told him. He took it—” she searched her notes “—he put it on a shelf. His words. For things he will deal with when he is ready and not one day before. He has a method. He has, Albus, a great many methods, and I spent the day watching them and I have the strong impression that I saw about a tenth.” She turned the page. “Now the complicated ones. You told me three weeks ago, in this room, that he had not bought the books. That Arabella would know.”

“Yes.”

“He bought the books. Last July. On his own. In a cap. He found the Leaky Cauldron from the Muggle side by standing on the Charing Cross Road until he could feel the Muggle-repelling charm pushing his attention, and then — his word — declining. He followed a family through the wall. He changed a hundred and eighty-four Muggle pounds at Gringotts; the goblins remember him; they stepped back for him today, Albus, I have never seen a goblin step back for anyone. He spent it all at Flourish and Blotts, including a monograph from 1911 that nobody has bought in a year, and he went home for tea, and he has read the first three grades of Standard Spells eleven times and can — he says, and I have no reason whatever to doubt him — write every spell in them in a notation of his own.”

Dumbledore said nothing for a long time.

“Arabella did not know,” he said at last.

“He told his aunt and nobody else. He does not lie to his aunt. He makes her tea.” She turned the last page. “The cupboard. You asked me to ask, and listen, and not arrive with an answer. I asked. He said—” and she read it, in his words, all of it, it’s mine, I took it when I was three, I’d like to be judged as him, and Dumbledore listened with his eyes closed and did not interrupt, and when she had finished he kept them closed a moment longer.

“He said that.”

“In those words. In a lounge with his own newspaper cuttings framed on the wall, which his uncle put there because the boy spent four years arranging for him to be proud. He timed the uncle’s shouting. Eighty-four seconds. He apologised that it was not sixty.” She straightened. “And Albus. The shop. Ollivander’s. I have left it to last because I do not have words for it and I have been looking since half past twelve.”

“Tell me.”

“Holly and phoenix. His first touch. And he did not give sparks.” She said it very precisely, the way one reports an instrument reading one does not believe. “He held the wand, and he held whatever it is one holds, for a second — I saw him do it, I saw him wait — and then a single point of white light came out at the tip, steady, and stayed, for four seconds, and lit the dust, and went out when he chose. Ollivander blinked. Ollivander has not blinked in my presence in thirty years. He said he had seen ten thousand first touches and never once a steady one, and the boy said he had been practising holding without a wand for a year so that he could hold with one, and then he called the wand a telephone, and apologised for the word.”

She stopped. The office was very quiet. Fawkes, on his perch, had his head up.

“And then, outside, on the pavement, I asked him whether he had known it was impossible, and he said yes, he had read it in a book from 1911, and had written not quite in the margin, and he was sorry, Albus, he said he was sorry it was going to make my year complicated and that he would try to warn me next time so that I did not find out in a shop.”

Albus Dumbledore sat behind his desk with his hands folded and the file in front of him and the green notes on top of the file, and for a long time he did not say anything at all, and McGonagall let him, because she had been letting him for thirty-five years and knew the difference between the silence that was a blind coming down and the silence that was a man standing in front of something with his own eyes open.

“Minerva,” he said finally. “Three weeks ago I said I should prepare to receive a colleague who was eleven.”

“You did.”

“I think I underestimated the matter in both directions at once.” He took his spectacles off. He did not polish them. He simply held them, and looked at the file, and she saw the window open and stay open, which she had not seen in sixty years. “He put the feather on a shelf. He asked the goblin’s name. He is sorry for your year. And he has been reading sentences off — what did Arabella say — off roses, for four years, in a cupboard, in a notation of his own, and he walked into Ollivander’s and held a light.” He put the spectacles back on. “I asked you whether he was kind because I believed it was the only thing that would matter. I find I was right, and I find also that I had not the faintest idea what I was asking.”

“No,” said McGonagall. “Nor had I. Nor, I think, had Arabella, and she has been in love with him for seven years.” She picked up the file, and the notes, and held them a moment. “He is going to ask you why you left him on the step. On the first of September, or soon after. He has it on a list, in order, and he told me he wanted it on record that he had asked before he saw the school. I told him you would have an answer.”

“I have an answer.”

“Then, Albus, I should spend the next month making very sure it is a good one.” She went to the door. “Because I have met him now, and I will tell you what Arabella could not, having never crossed the step: he will know the difference. He has spent eleven years learning to read the sides of faces for the part that is not being said. He told me so. I think he was being kind.”

She opened the door.

“Albus,” she said, from the threshold — and it was not a sentence; it was the whole of the day in a name — and went to find a very strong cup of tea.
