Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 21

Platform

Vernon drove, because Vernon drove, and talked about the traffic for the whole of the A3, and Petunia sat in the front with the flask on her knee and said nothing, and Dudley sat in the back with Harry and the owl and did not elbow him once, which was a thing Harry noticed at the Guildford turn and filed, and was still thinking about at Wandsworth.

King’s Cross at half past ten on the first of September smelled of diesel and pigeons, and he stood on the concourse with the trunk on a trolley and Hedwig on top of the trunk and his family round him in the attitudes of people who did not know how to do this, and looked at the departure boards, and then at the platforms, nine and ten, and the brick pillar between them with a newspaper kiosk against it, and did not feel the hand come.

It was there. He could see it working — the same age as the Charing Cross Road, and written, he could tell now, with five years of grammar behind him, by the same school of sentence that had built the wall in the yard: for those who know, a way. A woman with a pushchair went past the pillar and her eyes slid off a stretch of brick and she did not know they had. But it did not touch him. He had been told, on a pavement, by a woman in tartan, where the door was, and the sentence could tell that he knew, and left him alone.

So he read it instead. The hand was only the first half; the second half was the brick itself, which was a door that did not look like one and did not open like one, and he could see, now that nothing was turning his eyes, exactly where the sentence said go.

“Through there,” said Harry. “The pillar. Between nine and ten. You walk at it.”

“You walk at a wall,” said Vernon.

“Yes, Uncle Vernon. It isn’t a wall for me. It’s a wall for you.” He looked at Petunia. “Aunt Petunia. I don’t think you can come through. I think it’s — I think it’s for people who can see it. I’m sorry.”

Petunia looked at the pillar for a long moment with the flask on her knee — she had carried it from the car and had not yet handed it over — and her face did a thing he had seen once, at a lake, on a bench, with a magazine she had not read.

“No,” she said. “No. I know. It was the same for her. Mum and Dad stood where I’m standing.” She held out the flask. “Take this. It’s tea. The sandwiches are in the trunk, on top, in the greaseproof.”

He took the flask.

“Aunt Petunia—”

“Go on,” said Petunia Dursley, in the fence voice, and then, not in it: “Write to the house. Not to him.” A jerk of the head at Vernon, who was examining the departure boards. “To the house. I shall know it’s for me.”

“Yes.”

“And come back in the summer.”

“For the roof.”

“For the roof,” she agreed, and did not say the other thing, and did not need to, and he saw her hands, which were in her good gloves, close once on nothing, and open.

Dudley had been standing with his hands in the pockets of the Smeltings blazer that he hated, looking at the owl. Now he took one hand out, and there was a thing in it, and the thing was a football cap, old, from a club Dudley had supported for a fortnight in 1987, that came down over the forehead to the eyebrows, and that had been in a drawer in Dudley’s room for four years and had not, Harry had assumed, been missed.

“You’ll want this,” said Dudley. He did not look at Harry. He looked at the owl. “For — you know. If you don’t want them looking at you.”

Harry took the cap.

He had worn it four times through a wall with an old woman’s hand in his, and read his own history in it on a bench, and he had put it back in Dudley’s drawer each time without a word, and he had not known until this moment, on the concourse at King’s Cross, with the diesel and the pigeons, that Dudley had known. Had known, every time. Had said nothing, every time. Had kept the cap in the drawer and not asked and not told Vernon, for four years, which was — Harry did the sum, standing there, and it came out larger than he could easily hold — which was the longest Dudley Dursley had ever kept anything in his life, including a secret, including a kindness.

“Thank you,” said Harry. “Dudley. Thank you. I didn’t know you knew.”

“Course I knew,” said Dudley, to the owl. “It’s my cap.” And then, red, fast, to the pillar: “Good luck. Again. At the — thing.”

“Good luck at Smeltings.”

“Yeah,” said Dudley, and put his hand back in his pocket, and that was all, and it was the first conversation the two of them had ever had that was not about a want, and Harry put it on the shelf with the straight fingers and the swing, and went to push a trolley at a wall.

He did not look back. He had decided he would not, in the car, somewhere around Wandsworth, because he had learned at the bottom of some steps in Egham what a person looks like who is watching someone go somewhere they cannot follow, and he did not want his aunt to have to arrange her face for him twice in one morning.

He walked at the pillar, and declined, and went through.

The train was scarlet and the platform was full and the noise of it came at him like the first day at Waterloo, except that every face in it was theirs — cloaks and hats and cardigans, a boy with a toad, a family of redheads in a knot round a mother, an owl shouting at another owl — and he stopped, on the threshold, as he had stopped on every threshold of his life that mattered, and looked.

Ten seconds. A long while.

Then he found an empty compartment at the back, because the back was where the people who did not know anyone sat, and he wanted to see who did not know anyone. He got the trunk up with help from two redheaded twins who were plainly used to it and who looked at his forehead and at each other and did not say anything, which he filed as decent, and put Hedwig on the seat by the window and the Forth Bridge tin on the seat beside him, and sat down with his back to the engine, which was the way to see the platform, and watched.

He had decided on the train from Egham in August what he was for, on this train. Not to be looked at. He was going to be looked at; he had read the index; there was nothing to be done about it except wear the cap, and he had decided he would not wear the cap, because a boy who hid his face on the first morning would have to go on hiding it. He was for looking. Figg had said there would be somebody, and that you gave them the good ones first, and he had a tin, and seven hours, and every face on that platform was a page he had not read.

He read them. The redheaded knot — six of them, and a mother who hugged like she meant it, and a small girl who was not going and was crying about it, and a tall one with a badge who was being important at the others. The pale boy from Madam Malkin’s, with a father who looked like a man who had never once been looked at the way Harry looked at him now, and who looked back, briefly, down the platform, and whose face did a thing that Harry filed under knows exactly who I am and has thought about what to do with that, and set on the shelf for later. A girl with a great deal of brown hair and her nose in a book, being seen off by two people in Muggle coats who had the faces of dentists or accountants and were trying not to cry, and who were, he saw at once, the first people on that platform who looked the way Petunia had looked at the pillar. A round boy with a toad and a grandmother in a hat with a bird on it, and the toad already looking for a way out.

Somebody. There was always somebody.

The whistle went, and the platform began to move, and he did not wave at anyone, because there was no one on that platform to wave at, and he found that he minded that less than he had expected and more than he would have liked, and he put it on the shelf too.

The round boy with the toad was the first, and he came in crying, which Harry had expected, because he had watched the toad make its decision through the window at a quarter past eleven.

“Have you — sorry — have you seen a toad? Trevor. He’s — I’ve lost him, again, I always—”

“Sit down,” said Harry. “Please. Have a biscuit; they’re the good ones. I saw him go. He went left out of your compartment at eleven sixteen, and toads go down and damp, and the nearest down-and-damp on a train is the lavatory at the end of this carriage, and the door was open at the time. He’ll be behind the pipe under the basin. They always get behind the pipe.”

The round boy stood in the doorway with his mouth open.

“How do you — you saw him?”

“I see most things. It’s a habit. I’m Harry.” He held out the tin. “What’s your name?”

“Neville. Longbottom.” He took a biscuit, mechanically, and did not eat it. “Behind the pipe?”

“Behind the pipe. I’ll come, if you like. He’ll be cold; it’s a cold pipe. We should put him somewhere warm after.”

He was behind the pipe. Neville got him out, with Harry holding the door, and stood in the corridor with a cold toad in both hands and his face doing the thing Harry had seen Mrs Osei’s do over an acceptance letter, and said, “Nobody ever — people don’t usually help. With Trevor. They just sort of—”

“I know,” said Harry. “I’ve been the toad.” He said it before he had decided to, and it was true, and Neville looked at him, and something in the round frightened face settled a quarter-inch, and Harry thought: there. Good ones first.

They put Trevor in the Forth Bridge tin, with the lid off and the biscuits moved to a seat, because the tin had been warm in Harry’s lap for an hour and a toad is not particular about what it sits in. Neville sat down opposite and did not leave, and Harry did not ask him to, and that was the first.

The second was the redhead, and he came in because every other compartment was full, and said so, and sat down.

“Ron. Weasley. Everywhere else is — is that a toad? In a biscuit tin?”

“He’s cold,” said Neville, defensively.

“Right,” said Ron. He looked at Harry. He looked at Harry’s forehead. He looked away, red, and then — this was the thing Harry noticed, and liked at once, and filed under decent with the twins — he looked back, deliberately, at Harry’s face and not his forehead, and said, in the voice of someone making himself do a thing, “Sorry. Fred and George said you were on. I didn’t — you don’t have to—”

“I know,” said Harry. “It’s all right. You can look. Everyone’s going to; I decided this morning I wasn’t going to mind, and I nearly don’t.” He pushed the biscuits across. “Have one of the good ones. My — a woman who looked after me packed them. She said to give the good ones first.”

Ron took one. He ate it. He looked at the tin, and the toad, and Neville, and the owl, and Harry, and said, with his mouth full, “Is it true you’ve got a degree? Fred said — but Fred says things—”

“Yes.”

“A Muggle degree.”

“Yes. In mathematics. It’s a kind of — it’s a piece of paper that says you can do a thing.” He hesitated, because this was the first time he had said any of it to someone on this side who was his own age, and he found he wanted to say it right. “It doesn’t mean anything here. I know that. I didn’t get it to mean anything here. I got it to find out whether I could, and I could, and now I’m here, and I don’t know anything about this—” he gestured at the train, the owl, the toad in the tin “—except what’s in books, and I’ve been told the books are wrong about a lot of it, and I was hoping somebody would tell me where.”

Ron looked at him for a long moment, with the biscuit halfway to his mouth, and Harry watched him do the sum that everyone in that world did — Boy Who Lived; a degree; here, on a train, asking — and arrive at a total that was not the one he had come in with.

“The books are wrong about Quidditch,” said Ron, with great firmness. “Quidditch Through the Ages says the Cannons are a mid-table side. They’re not. They’re rubbish. They’re my team.” He took another biscuit. “What else d’you want to know?”

“Everything,” said Harry. “In order. Start with the Cannons.”

Ron started with the Cannons. It took forty minutes, and it was the first forty minutes of Harry’s life in which someone his own age had told him something he did not know and could not have read, and he listened to all of it, at a speed he had never once in ten years been able to listen to anyone, which was to say with a quarter of his mind, and the other three-quarters watching Ron Weasley’s face do the thing faces did when they were telling you about a thing they loved, and he thought: this is the thing of which the café was the small dose.

The third was the girl with the hair, and she did not come in because the other compartments were full. She came in because she had heard, three compartments down, that Harry Potter was in the back carriage with a degree, and she wanted to see whether it was true, and she said so, in the doorway, in a sentence with no pause in it anywhere.

“Is it true you’ve got a doctorate from the University of London because I read about it in the Telegraph last year and my parents said it must be a different Harry Potter because the Harry Potter in Modern Magical History is a wizard and wizards don’t go to Muggle universities but then I saw you on the platform and you’ve got the — well — and I thought it can’t be but I wanted to ask because nobody ever just asks they just say things, I’m Hermione Granger, is it true?”

Neville had gone small. Ron had the face of a boy watching a thing happen that he had been warned about and had not believed. Harry, who had spent ten years reading faces at two hundred to one and had never once been talked at faster than he could think, sat with the Forth Bridge tin on his knee and the toad in it and found that he was — he examined it carefully, because it was new — he was delighted.

“Yes,” he said. “It’s true. Sit down. Have a biscuit; they’re the good ones. What’s your question?”

“That was my question.”

“No it wasn’t. That was the thing you asked. Your question is why. You want to know why a wizard would bother.” He moved the biscuits to make room. “It’s a good question. Nobody else has asked it. The Deputy Headmistress hasn’t asked it. I’d like to answer it, if you’ll tell me yours first. Why did you read Modern Magical History before you got here? Nobody reads that. It’s dull. I know; I’ve read it four times.”

Hermione Granger sat down. She did it the way Carrow had sat down over forty pages at a long table: as if her legs had made the decision before she had.

“Because I wanted to know what I was walking into,” she said. “Because they sent a letter and a woman in a hat and said I was a witch and gave me a list, and nobody told me what the rules were, and I don’t go anywhere without knowing the rules, so I read everything on the list and then I read everything the shop had that wasn’t on the list, and Modern Magical History was the only one that was about now, and it was mostly about you, and you were on the platform.” She took a biscuit. “That’s not an answer to why you’d bother with a Muggle degree.”

“No. Here’s mine. I bothered because it was the only room I’d ever been in where they judged the thing on the board instead of the face of the person who wrote it. A child of eight can stand up in front of a man from Cambridge who came to fail him, and if the thing holds, it holds, and the man has to say so. I wanted to find out what that felt like. It felt like being seen.” He looked at her. “You read everything on the list and everything not on the list because nobody told you the rules. I did the same thing, for the same reason, starting at five. I think we’re going to get on, Hermione Granger, and I think the first thing you should know about me is that I am going to ask you questions back, and I am going to ask them fast, and nobody has ever once kept up, and I would very much like it if you did.”

She looked at him for a long moment, with a biscuit in her hand, and he watched her — he had all the time in the world to watch her — go through surprise, and suspicion, and arithmetic, and arrive somewhere he recognised, because he had seen Carrow arrive there over page nine.

“Go on, then,” said Hermione.

“What is a spell?”

“A — it’s a — ” She stopped. He watched her stop. It was, he thought, the finest thing he had seen on the train. “The book says it’s an incantation and a wand movement that—”

“That’s what it does. What is it?”

“I don’t—” She put the biscuit down. “Nobody’s asked me that. I’ve read seven books and none of them say. They say what to say and where to point and what happens. They don’t say what it is.”

“No,” said Harry. “They don’t. I’ve read all seven and forty others and I can tell you exactly where each of them stops saying, and it’s always the same page. Page one. They stop before they start.” He leaned forward. “I think I know what it is. I’ve thought I knew since I was six. I’ve written it down and nobody can read what I wrote because I had to invent the marks. I’m going to find out this year whether I’m right, in a classroom, with a man who’s got a chair ready, and I would like — I think I would like someone who reads everything on the list and everything not on the list to be in the room when I do, because I have never once had anyone in the room, and I’m told it’s better.”

Hermione Granger did not say anything for three seconds, which for him was most of a morning, and he spent the morning watching her face and thinking that he had been right on the platform, that there was always somebody, and that Figg had been right about the biscuits, and that this was what chasing looked like from the outside, because he had never once in his life been able to see it from the inside.

“What marks?” said Hermione.

“I’ll show you,” said Harry. “Not today. When I know you better. They took me four years and they’re in a biscuit tin and there’s a toad in the other one.” He pushed the tin across. “Have another. Ron was explaining the Cannons. He says the books are wrong about them.”

“The books are wrong about everything,” said Hermione, with feeling, and took another, and did not leave, and that was the third.

The fourth came at half past one, and did not sit down, and was the pale boy from Madam Malkin’s, with two larger boys behind him in the attitudes of furniture.

“So it’s true. They’re saying all down the train. Harry Potter’s in the back with—” he looked at Ron, at Neville, at Hermione, at the toad in the tin, and his face arranged itself into a thing it had plainly been practising since the stool “—with company. I’m Malfoy. Draco Malfoy.”

Ron made a sound. Draco Malfoy heard it, and turned, and the practised face got a line ready, and Harry — who had watched this boy’s father on the platform for eleven seconds and read the whole of what he intended off the side of his face — said, before the line could land:

“Your father knows exactly who I am. I saw him look. He’s thought about what to do about it; I saw that too. You haven’t. You’ve come to see what he’d want you to see.” He said it without any edge at all, because he had none; he had seen this boy’s face in the shop go quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the father, and he had filed it under later, and later had arrived. “Sit down, if you like. Have a biscuit. They’re good ones. I’d rather find out what you think than what he does, and I don’t think anybody has ever asked you that, because I know what a boy looks like who’s never been asked. I was one.”

The compartment was very quiet.

Draco Malfoy stood in the doorway with his hand half-out for a line he had rehearsed and found that the line had nowhere to go. Harry watched him look for it — at Ron, who was red; at Hermione, who had her chin up; at Neville, who had gone small — and not find it anywhere, because nobody in the compartment had done anything he had been told to expect, and the boy had not, Harry saw, been told what to do when that happened. Nobody had told him there was a when.

“I don’t—” said Draco, and stopped.

“No,” said Harry. “I didn’t think you would. It’s all right. You don’t have to decide anything on a train.” He put a biscuit on the seat by the door, where a boy could take it or not without anyone watching him do either. “That one’s yours if you want it. The offer’s open. So’s the question. I ask everyone the same one; you can have it now or later. What is a spell?”

Draco looked at the biscuit.

“That’s a first-year question,” he said, with contempt that did not quite reach his face.

“It is,” said Harry. “Nobody in seven books can answer it. Try.”

The pale boy stood a moment longer. Then he said, to the two large boys behind him, without turning, “Come on,” and went, and the biscuit stayed on the seat, and nobody touched it, and at Peterborough it was gone, and Harry had not seen who took it, which was the first thing in a year he had not seen, and he filed that too, under later, and under something else that he did not yet have a word for, and thought might be hope.

“That,” said Ron, when the door had shut, “was Malfoy. His dad was — my dad says — you gave him a biscuit.”

“I gave him a question,” said Harry. “The biscuit was so he wouldn’t have to answer it in front of you. His father knows who I am. I’d like to know who he is before his father tells him.” He looked at the empty seat by the door. “And I’ve been the boy nobody asked. It’s a cold place. I don’t leave people in it if I can help it, even ones I don’t like yet.”

Hermione was looking at him with the face from over the biscuit.

“Yet,” she said.

“Yet. I don’t know him. I know his face in a shop. That’s a page, not a book.” He turned to Neville. “Trevor’s warm. Feel the tin.”

Neville felt the tin.

The sandwiches were in greaseproof, on top of the trunk, and there were enough for four, which he had not expected and which told him that his aunt had seen the platform in her head before she had seen it with her eyes, and had known there would be somebody, and had cut accordingly.

He gave them out. Ron had three. Hermione had one and talked through it. Neville had one and fed the crust to the toad, who was not interested. Harry had the flask, which was tea, and was still warm, and he put his hand on the side of it and let it be warm for a quarter-second longer than it had been and nobody saw, and he thought: two hundred miles. She made it for the whole way.

“Your mum made these?” said Ron, with his mouth full.

“My aunt. My — I live with my aunt and uncle. My parents are—” He stopped, because it was the first time he had had to say it to anyone who did not already know, and found he did not know the shape of the sentence. “You know what my parents are. It’s in the book Hermione read.”

“Sorry,” said Ron, red. “I didn’t—”

“It’s all right. You asked about the sandwiches. The sandwiches are my aunt. She didn’t want me to come here and she’s kept a promise about it for ten years and she cut enough for four.” He looked at the greaseproof. “She can’t see the platform. She stood where her parents stood when my mother went, and handed me a flask, and said write to the house. I said I would.”

The compartment was quiet for a moment, in the way compartments are when something has been said that is larger than the sandwiches.

“Mine cried,” said Hermione, abruptly. “On the platform. Both of them. They tried not to.”

“I saw. They looked like my aunt.” He hesitated, and then said it, because she had chased and had earned it: “I think everyone whose parents stood on that side of the barrier this morning should probably sit together. I think we’re going to need each other for things the others won’t understand. That’s not a rule. It’s a — it’s a thing I noticed.”

“You notice a lot,” said Hermione.

“Yes. It’s the only thing I’m actually good at. Everything else came from that.” He put the flask down. “What house do you want?”

She told him. At length, with reasons, from Hogwarts: A History, and he listened to all of it, and then asked her why the book said Gryffindor was the best and whether a book written by a Gryffindor might have a view, and she opened her mouth and shut it and said, “Oh,” in the voice of someone who had just found a comma in the wrong place, and he thought: there. She’s going to be the first one I show the marks to. Not today. But soon.

The country went north and went dark, and the train went on, and at some point Neville fell asleep with his hand on the Forth Bridge tin, and Ron fell asleep with his head against the window and his mouth open, and Hermione did not fall asleep but stopped talking, which was rarer, and sat with Hogwarts: A History open on her knee at the chapter about the Sorting and did not read it, and looked out at the dark.

Harry did not sleep. He had not expected to. He sat with his back to the engine and the swing in his inside pocket and the holly in his other pocket, on the hook still, and Mr Prentice’s pencil and Carrow’s chalk and Mrs Osei’s brass instrument from 1958 in the bag at his feet, and the box file in the trunk with nine hundred and forty pages in it that nobody alive could read and that he had decided on this train, somewhere past Peterborough, that a girl with a great deal of hair was going to learn to, and he looked out at Scotland coming, and took stock, because Carrow had taught him to, and he did it without a pen, in the long quiet, for nobody.

A toad found by looking. A boy who told me about the Cannons for forty minutes and I listened with a quarter of my mind and it was the best forty minutes. A girl who chases. A boy who didn’t take the biscuit in front of anyone and took it at Peterborough. Four sandwiches. A flask still warm, which was me, a little, and her, mostly.

Figg was right. There was somebody. There were three.

Tonight: a hat, and a hall, and a man with a very strong cup of tea and a letter in his pocket, and a question first, and then four hundred and two others. Tomorrow a chair.

Then the step. Then the blankets. Then the cold.

Then everything else.

The train began to slow. Hermione looked up from the chapter about the Sorting.

“Are you nervous?” she said. Quietly, so as not to wake the others. It was, he realised, the first personal question anyone his own age had ever asked him.

He thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how.

“No,” he said. “I’ve been getting ready for this since I was five. I’ve been getting ready for it longer than that, in a way I can’t explain yet. I’m not nervous.” He looked out at the dark, where there were lights now, a long way off, high up, a great many of them. “I’m something else. I haven’t got a word for it. I’ve got seven languages and none of them have it.”

“What does it feel like?”

He thought about a doorstep, and a cupboard, and a kitchen floor, and a long table, and a bench, and a board with chalk on the back of a door, and a woman in tartan saying as it stands, and a flask that was still warm.

“Like the lights coming on,” said Harry Potter. “In a library. When you’re already inside.”
