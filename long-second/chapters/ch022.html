Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 22

The Hat

The castle came up out of the dark across the lake like a thing that had been waiting a long time to be looked at properly, and he looked at it properly, from a boat, with Neville gripping the gunwale and Hermione saying the dates of the towers under her breath and Ron saying nothing at all for the first time in seven hours, and he thought: a sentence in a place. The biggest one he had ever stood in front of. Older than the wall, older than the roof, so old and so many-handed that he could not read it from the boat at all, only feel the hum of it come across the water like the street in the Alley turned up to the sky.

He stopped reading. He had learned at the roses that there were things you read and things you let pass through you, and he let the castle pass through him, in a boat, at eleven, and did not try to understand it, and that was the first time in his life he had decided not to.

McGonagall was in the entrance hall in the tartan, which he had known she would be, and she looked down the line of first-years with the face that had declined to be impressed in a lounge in Surrey five weeks ago, and when her eyes came to him they stopped for exactly as long as they stopped on everyone else, and then went on, and he thought: good. She’s not going to make it easy. He had not wanted her to.

She told them about the houses. He had read it. He listened anyway, in case she went a different way, and she did, slightly, at the end, where the book said your house will be like your family and she said your house will be where you are judged first, by people who have not met you, and I should like you to bear in mind that they will be wrong, and he filed that, and looked at Hermione, who had heard it too, and whose chin had gone up.

Then the doors.

The Great Hall he had read about in two books and seen a drawing of in one, and none of them had said the thing that mattered, which was the sound. Four hundred people and a ceiling that was the sky, and the hum of the place under all of it — the castle’s sentence, up close now, in the stone under his feet and the air over his head, so thick that the candles floating in it seemed to be floating on it — and he walked up the middle of it in a line of eleven-year-olds with every face in the room turning to find the scar, and he did not wear the cap, and he did not look at the floor, and he found that he could do it, and that he had known since the pillar that he could.

The staff table. He read it in the four seconds it took to walk the last twenty feet, which was most of a quarter-hour, and he did it the way he had read the platform, for who did not know anyone.

A very small man at the end with a great deal of white hair and an expression of such naked, bouncing delight at the sight of the first-years that Harry knew him at once — a chair ready — and liked him before he had said a word. A woman with earth under her nails. A woman with a face like a hawk and a tray of something. A pale thin man in a turban who was looking at the first-years and whose face Harry read, and read again, and stopped on, because there was something in it that did not sit right — not the nervousness; nervousness was ordinary; something under the nervousness, a wrongness in the way the face was held, as though two people were holding it and had not agreed how. He had never seen a face like it. He filed it where he filed things that had edges, under later, and under look again, and went on.

A man with black hair and a face like a shut door, who was looking at Harry, and whose look went into him like a splinter, and who looked away, and whose face, in the quarter-second before it shut entirely, did a thing Harry had seen once, on his aunt, across a kitchen table, at the word eyes. He filed that too, carefully, under later, and under her, and did not understand it, and intended to.

And in the middle, in the big chair, with half-moon spectacles catching the candles and a beard that went to his belt, the man who had written a sentence on a doorstep in the time it took to put down a basket, and had paid for books without a word, and had told him which chapter to read, and had asked him four questions through a squib and had the answers in a drawer, and had a letter in his pocket that Harry could not see and knew was there the way he knew the hum of the castle was there.

Dumbledore was looking at him.

Not the way the room was looking — at the scar, at the story, at the morning they had woken up to. The way Carrow had looked at forty pages. The way Aldous had looked at page ninety. The way a man looks at a thing he has read about for five years in other people’s handwriting and is now, for the first time, allowed to see.

Harry looked back. He had decided on the train that he would, and he did, for the whole of the four seconds, and at the end of them Albus Dumbledore inclined his head, one inch, the way a man from Cambridge had in a hall with long windows, and Harry understood that the man had read his receipts and his two pages about the cold and had decided what he was before he arrived, and had just, in one inch, let him know it.

He inclined his own, one inch, back. Tomorrow at nine, he thought, as clearly as if it were on a shelf. The step first.

Then there was a stool, and a hat.

The hat sang. He had read that it did, and had not believed the book, and it did, and the song was doggerel, and he listened to all of it with a quarter of his mind, because the other three-quarters were on the thing under the song, which was the hat itself, which was — he stood in the line of first-years with his heart beginning to go — which was a sentence.

Not a sentence in a place, like the castle. Not a sentence in a thing, like the wall. A sentence that thought. He could feel it, from twenty feet, the way he had felt the cold off six pages by a clerk: a thing that held itself, that cast itself over and over, that had been doing so for a thousand years in a hat — and that was kind. That was the difference, and he felt it so clearly that he nearly said it aloud. The clerk’s shapes ate. This one asked.

A sentence that eats sentences, except that it doesn’t eat. It reads.

He had written, at nine, under the stairs, nobody has tried to read one; everybody has tried to keep them off; different question. He had been writing about the cold. He had not known there was one in a hat.

“Abbott, Hannah.”

He watched. He had five years of watching faces behind him and he watched the hat go on and come off, and he timed it — four seconds, nine, two, eleven — and he watched what happened to the children’s faces in the gap, and it was the thing he had seen in the sports hall at six: the weather of a mind being asked something. Some of them argued. He could see it. Some of them simply sat.

“Granger, Hermione.”

She went up the way she had come into the compartment, with no pause in her anywhere, and sat, and the hat went on, and he watched her face, and her face did a thing he had not seen it do on the train, which was stop. Hold. The way Carrow’s had on the back of a door. And then — he could see it; nobody else in the hall could have — she argued. Her lips did not move but her chin went up and her eyes went to the ceiling that was the sky and she was, he would have bet the Forth Bridge tin, laying out the case for Gryffindor from Hogwarts: A History, and the hat was asking her who had written Hogwarts: A History, and she was saying oh.

Forty-one seconds. A long time, for them.

“RAVENCLAW!”

The blue table cheered. Hermione took the hat off with the face of someone who had just lost an argument and was not sorry, and went to the blue table, and looked back once at the line — at him — with a look that said you did that, and he thought: I asked a question. You did the rest.

“Longbottom, Neville.”

Neville sat on the stool as if it might be taken away from him. The hat went on and stayed on, and Harry watched the round face go through fear and more fear and then, very slowly, something else, something he had seen on it in a corridor with a cold toad in both hands — nobody ever helps — and the hat said “GRYFFINDOR!” and Neville got up and walked off with the hat still on and had to come back for it, and the red table laughed, and Harry did not, because he had read what the hat had seen, and it had seen what Harry had seen behind a pipe, and he thought: good. Somebody else looked.

“Malfoy, Draco.”

The hat barely touched him. “SLYTHERIN!” And the pale boy went to the green table with his father’s walk and did not look at the line, and then, sitting down, did — once, quickly, at Harry — and looked away, and Harry thought about a biscuit on a seat at Peterborough and did not know what the look meant and did not pretend to.

And then the M’s went by, and the N’s, and the O’s, and a girl called Patil went to the blue table and her twin to the red, and the hall had got quiet in a way it had not been quiet before, because everyone in it could count, and then:

“Potter, Harry.”

The silence as he walked to the stool was the loudest thing he had ever heard. He had read the index. He had sat on a bench. He knew what he was to them. He walked through it the way he had walked at the pillar, declining, and sat down on the stool, and McGonagall’s hands put the hat on his head, and it came down over his eyes, and the hall went away, and there was a voice.

Oh, said the voice. Oh. You’re fast.

He had never in his life heard anyone say that to him. He had never in his life been in a conversation where the other party could tell. He sat on the stool in the dark of the hat with the hall gone and found that his heart, which had been going since the song, had stopped going and started doing something else, something he had not felt since a kitchen floor at six when a thing in him had come up to meet a thing that was wrong.

You can tell, he said. He did not say it with his mouth. He had never needed to; the thing in him had never needed a mouth; and the hat, it seemed, did not either.

Of course I can tell. I’m sitting in it. A pause, which was not a pause, which was the hat doing something very like what Harry did when he went back along a shelf. Good heavens. Good heavens. How long have you been—

Since a doorstep. Fifteen months old. Two hundred to one, or near enough; I measured it at four with washers and string.

Two hundred. Another not-pause. I have been on heads for a thousand years, boy, and I have never once been on one where I was the slow party. Do you know how strange this is? I am having to wait for you to finish thinking. Nobody has ever— The voice stopped, and when it came back it had a thing in it that Harry recognised from Carrow, from Aldous, from a very small man at the end of the staff table with bouncing hair: Oh, this is going to be the best Sorting I have had since 1710. Show me. All of it. I can keep up. Go.

He showed it.

He had never shown anyone. There had never been anyone who could be shown; Mrs Figg had the shape of it, Carrow had the mathematics, McGonagall had the receipts, and none of them had ever once been able to stand inside a quarter-second with him and see. The hat could. He opened the shelves — not all of them; he had decided on the stool what the hat would get and what it would not, because he was eleven and had a rule about rooms — and let the hat walk them, at his speed, which the hat could nearly match, and the hat walked.

A cupboard. A playpen, and a star crossing a gap between rooftops. A woman at a cooker saying just like. The marks. A cat getting up off a kitchen floor. It has parts. A pot warmed from forty yards. A pub found by declining. An index on a bench with an old woman’s hand in his. A long table and page nine. A roof read from a step. Six pages by a clerk. Four hundred and twelve pages in a biscuit tin. The asking. The seal. The feather that held. A light in a shop for four seconds. A woman in tartan saying as it stands. A flask still warm at York.

It took the hat most of a minute to walk it, which for Harry was most of a morning, and he sat in the dark and let it, and when the hat came back it did not say anything for a long moment, which was the first time Harry had ever felt a thing older than himself be quiet because it did not know what to say.

Well, said the hat at last. Well. I am sworn to secrecy, you know. Always have been. It is in the sentence they made me with; I could not tell him if I wanted to and I find — this is new; I shall have to think about it — I find I don’t want to. This is yours. You should be the one to say it, when you say it. A pause. You know what I am.

A sentence that thinks. Self-holding. Cast once, a thousand years ago, and casting itself ever since. Harry hesitated, and then said it, because the hat had earned it. I read about the things in the prison in the sea when I was nine. A clerk said they fed. I wrote down that if they ate sentences they were made of them, and that nobody had ever tried to read one. You’re what I think they are. Except you don’t eat. You read.

The hat was quiet again.

Yes, it said, eventually, in a voice that had lost its bounce. Yes. Nobody has ever put it that way to me. I do not like it. But yes. We are — cousins, perhaps. The same grammar, bent two ways. And then, sharper: And you mean to read them.

Yes.

It will be the most dangerous thing anyone has ever done in this school.

I know. It’s eighth on a list. I am going to do the first seven first.

Hm, said the hat, which was, Harry thought, the exact sound Mrs Prentice made, and he nearly laughed on the stool.

And then the hat did a thing he had not been ready for, and it did it gently, the way you would hold out your hands to a child to show him a trick.

There is something in here that isn’t yours.

He went very still, in the dark.

You know that, said the hat. I can see that you know. You have it behind a door.

Yes. He said it carefully, because this was the one shelf he had decided the hat would not be shown, and the hat had found it anyway, the way the asking would have, and he supposed that was fair. I found it when I was seven. The night I read the index, on the train home, with the book in a bag against my leg. I went back along the shelf to the green light and I found — there’s a grain in it. In the memory, and in me. Small, and cold, and it isn’t mine, and it doesn’t like me, and I didn’t know what it was and I knew what it wasn’t, which was me. So I put a door on it. The way I put a door on the cupboard when I was three. It can’t get out and I don’t go in. It’s on the list. Item nine. I’m going to do the first eight first.

And you have told no one.

There was no one who could stand still long enough to be told. You’re the first. A pause. Will you?

I cannot. It is in my sentence. The hat’s voice had changed again, and what was in it now Harry had heard once, from a squib on a garden bench, and once from a woman with a knife for a face in a room with the rain on the windows. But I will tell you this, boy, since I am the first and may be the only for a while: you have done exactly right. A door, and a list, and the first eight first. There are men in this castle who could not have done that at seventy. Do not open it alone. When the time comes, you will know who to open it with, and it will not be who you think.

All right, said Harry. Thank you. And then, because it was the only being in the world that could keep up with him and he was eleven and had never once been able to ask: Can I ask you something that isn’t about the Sorting?

You have been asking me things that are not about the Sorting for a minute and a half. The hall is beginning to wonder. Go on.

Does it hurt? Being a sentence that thinks. For a thousand years. In a hat.

The silence this time was different from the others.

Nobody, said the hat, very quietly, has ever asked me that.

I know. I ask everyone the same question and nobody has ever asked it back. I wanted to be the one who did. Does it?

Sometimes, said the hat. Once a year, for about an hour, it is the best thing in the world. The rest of the time I sit on a shelf in his office and think. A pause. You would know about that.

Yes, said Harry. I would.

Then we had better get you sorted, cousin, before Minerva comes and takes me off, which she has done once, in 1952, and has never let me forget. The bounce came back into the voice, deliberately, the way Carrow put her glasses back on. You know what the choices are. You have read the book. You have, I perceive, already had an opinion about who wrote the book. Make your case. I like a case.

He made it. He had been making it since the compartment.

Gryffindor, for my parents, and because the asking takes a kind of nerve, and because a round boy with a toad is going there and I said I’d been the toad. Slytherin, because I have a plan with eight items on it and a lever and a press and I intend to move a world, and that is what the book says they are for, and I think the book is being rude about it. Hufflepuff, because every single person who ever held a door for me would have gone there, and I have been thinking since a garden bench that holding doors is the only thing worth being good at. And Ravenclaw, because of the grammar, and because the biscuit tin, and because a girl who reads everything on the list and everything not on it has just gone to the blue table with the face of somebody who lost an argument and wasn’t sorry.

That is four cases, said the hat. You are supposed to make one.

I know. Here it is. He took a breath, in the dark, on the stool, in the hall that had gone silent. I don’t think it’s a house question. I think the four houses are four doors into the same building and I am going to walk through all of them before I am done, because I have to; the table I mean to be has to have a chair at it from every one. So the question isn’t which door is mine. The question is which door has the people in it who will chase. Who will keep up. Who will ask the question back. I have had exactly one person in my life do that, on a train, this afternoon, and she is at the blue table, and I would like to be where she is, because I have been the only one in the room for a very long time and I am told it is better with company.

The hat was quiet for a long moment. Then it made a sound that was not a word and was, he thought, the nearest a thousand-year-old sentence came to laughing.

You, said the hat, are going to be the most interesting seven years of my existence, and I have had Dumbledore.

Will you put me there?

I am going to put you where you asked to go, said the hat, which nobody has ever asked me to do in those words, and which I find is exactly where I would have put you anyway, and I shall let you decide which of those two facts to believe. Go and sit with the girl who chases. Give her the good biscuits. And Harry—

Yes?

Come and see me. He keeps me on a shelf. Nobody comes. You know the way to a shelf.

I’ll come, said Harry. I’ve got four hundred questions and I’m saving them for him, but I’ll think of some for you. I’m good at questions.

I had noticed, said the hat, and then, aloud, to the hall, in the voice it used for the hall, which was not the voice it had been using for him:

“RAVENCLAW!”

Four minutes and nine seconds. He had timed it. The hall had been silent for the last two, and when the hat came off and the candles came back and the ceiling that was the sky came back with them, four hundred faces were looking at him with an expression that he recognised, because he had seen it on Mr Dyer of the county in a corridor, breathing: the face of people who have just watched a thing go past the end of the sheet.

The blue table found its voice first. It was not a cheer, exactly; it was something more considered, the sound of a table of people who had just been handed a thing and were working out what it was, and then it became a cheer, and he walked to it through the sound, and sat down beside a girl with a great deal of hair who was looking at him with the face from the compartment.

“Four minutes,” said Hermione.

“Four minutes and nine seconds.”

“What did you talk about?”

“Everything,” said Harry. “It could keep up. Nobody’s ever been able to keep up. I had a lot saved.” He looked at her. “You argued with it. About the book. I saw your chin.”

“It asked me who wrote Hogwarts: A History,” said Hermione, with the air of someone still slightly affronted, “and I said a Gryffindor, obviously, and it said well then, and I said oh.” She paused. “You did that.”

“I asked a question. You did the rest. That’s how it works.”

“How what works?”

“I don’t know yet,” said Harry. “I’ve never had anyone to do the rest before. I think it’s going to be the whole of the next seven years.” He turned, because the last of the first-years were going up — Weasley, Ronald — and watched Ron sit on the stool with the face of a boy who has been told by five brothers what will happen and does not believe them, and the hat went on, and Ron’s face did nothing at all for three seconds and then did everything at once, and “GRYFFINDOR!” and the red table went up like a flock off a field, and Ron walked to it looking back at the blue table, once, with the expression of a boy who had just been told something he had already known and was finding out it was true.

Harry raised one hand, an inch. Ron raised his back. That was all. It was, Harry thought, the second conversation of his life that was not about a want.

He did not hear the speech. He had read it — Hermione had told him on the train what Hogwarts: A History said the Headmaster usually said, and the Headmaster said something else entirely, four words, which Hermione would be indignant about for a week — but he did not hear it, because he was looking at the man who said it, and the man was looking back, over the half-moons, over four hundred heads, and the look was the one from the staff table, the allowed to see look, and under it, now, something else, which Harry read, and read again, and placed.

The man was afraid. Not of him. The way Mrs Figg had been afraid, in a garden, five years ago — not of him; for the rest of you. Dumbledore was sitting at the high table of his own school looking at a boy of eleven whom he had left on a step and read about in receipts and asked four questions of through a squib, and he was afraid, Harry saw, of what he was going to be asked at nine o’clock tomorrow morning, and he was going to answer it anyway, and he had the answer in his pocket, and he was — this was the part that went in warm — he was glad. Under the fear. The way the street had been glad. The way Dedalus Diggle had been glad. Glad to see him, and afraid of him, and sure, and kind, and all of those at once, which Figg had said was what he was like, and Figg had never once been wrong about a face.

Harry inclined his head, one inch, for the second time.

Dumbledore smiled. It was a small smile, and it did not reach the fear, and it did not try to, and Harry found that he respected it more for that than he would have for a larger one.

Then there was food, and he ate it, and listened to Hermione and a girl called Padma argue about the ceiling, and watched the staff table with a quarter of his mind, and the pale man in the turban with another quarter, and the man with the shut face with a third, and kept the fourth for the very small man at the end with the bouncing hair who had, Harry saw, not taken his eyes off the blue table since the hat had said the word, and who caught Harry looking, and beamed, and raised his goblet an inch, and Harry raised his, and thought: tomorrow. A chair.

Ravenclaw Tower had a door with a bronze eagle on it that asked a riddle, and he answered it in the time it took the eagle to finish asking, and then said nothing, and waited, and Hermione, beside him, said, “Wait — don’t — let me—” and got it in eleven seconds, and the door opened, and she looked at him with the face from over the biscuit and said, “You knew.”

“I had a head start. I’ve had a head start since I was fifteen months old. It isn’t fair and I’ve stopped pretending it is.” He held the door. “You got it in eleven seconds. That’s not a head start. That’s you. I’d rather watch you do it than do it myself; I’ve done it myself for ten years and I know how it comes out.”

“That’s—” Hermione stopped in the doorway. “That’s a very odd thing to say.”

“Yes. I’m a very odd person. I did warn you, on the train. I said I’d ask questions fast and nobody had ever kept up.” He looked at her. “You kept up. In the hat. Forty-one seconds and you argued. I saw.”

“I lost.”

“You didn’t lose. You said oh. That’s the only thing worth saying to a hat.” He went through the door. “Come on. There’s a window, I’m told, that looks at the mountains. I’ve never seen a mountain. I’ve read about them.”

The dormitory was round, and blue, and had five beds, and his was by the window, which he had not arranged and suspected a very small man with bouncing hair of having arranged for him, and he sat on it at midnight with the others asleep and the mountains outside being mountains in the dark, and took out the forty-seventh pad, and the blue pen, and did the thing he had done at every threshold of his life, which was to take stock.

But he did not write what he had. He had written that in a cupboard the night before, and it had not changed, except that there were three more people in it now and a hat.

He wrote the List instead. The whole of it, again, from the top, because Carrow had taught him that when a thing had changed you did not amend it, you rewrote it, so that the reader in fifty years could see what you had thought after and not only before.

Then he put the pad down, and took out a second sheet, and wrote the first letter, because he had promised, and because there was a post from everywhere and an owl on the windowsill who could not be told anything.

I am in a tower with a window that looks at mountains. The woman in tartan is the Deputy Head and she does not make it easy, which I wanted. He gave it to Hedwig, who took it the way Mr Tibbles had always taken instruction, with the air of a professional who had expected nothing less, and went out of the window into the dark over the lake, north and then south, and he watched her go until he could not see her, which was a long time, and then he lay down in a bed by a window in a tower in Scotland, eleven years old, the better part of two thousand, with a wand on the hook on the table beside him and a chair waiting in the morning and a step to ask about at nine, and he did not sleep, and he had known he would not, and he lay in the dark and listened to a castle hum, and found that he could not tell how long each tick was, because the castle did not tick; it sang, one long note, a thousand years old, under everything.

He let it pass through him. He did not try to read it.

That could wait till morning. He had, as usual, plenty of time.
