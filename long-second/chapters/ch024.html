Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 24

The Chair

What the man had said, for the rest of that morning, came to three things, and Harry put them in order that night, by the window, in the words he would use if anyone ever asked him what he had agreed to.

One. He had seen it too. A fortnight ago, at a staff meeting, in the set of the man’s shoulders when nobody was speaking to him; and had set two of the hundred eyes watching, and had told himself it was nerves. He said so plainly, which I respected. He said: I have been pretending not to see it because the alternative was a thing I was not ready to see in my own school, and you have made me ready, and I am not grateful yet but I shall be.

Two. I am to watch. Not approach. Not test. Watch, the way I watch everything, and tell him on Thursdays what the face is doing, because I can read it and he cannot, and that is — he said this — the first time in sixty years that anyone has been able to do a thing in this castle that he could not, and he would like to get used to it slowly.

Three. He would not tell me what he thinks is in the face. He said he had a guess and that the guess was the second kind of reason — the fear kind — and that he was not going to give me a fear and ask me to carry it to Charms. He said: when I know, you will know, within the hour. I said that was the same silence he had just apologised for. He said yes, it was, and that he was going to commit it anyway for a fortnight, and that I could hold him to it, and I shall.

So: a fortnight. Then I ask again. In the meantime: a chair.

The first Charms lesson was on the Wednesday, in a room on the third floor with high windows and a lectern that had been built for a man of ordinary height and had a stack of books on it that had not, and the man standing on the books was the very small one from the end of the staff table, with the white hair and the bouncing delight, and he had plainly not slept.

“Good morning, good morning. Sit anywhere. Not you, Mr Boot, that chair has a leg. Good morning. I am Professor Flitwick, and this is Charms, and I have been teaching it for forty-one years, and I should like to begin by telling you that I have never once been bored, and I intend to find out this morning whether that record holds.” He beamed at them. He had a face built for beaming and had plainly used it hard. “Wands out. Not pointed at anyone. Mr Goldstein, that is someone.”

Harry took the holly out of his pocket. It was the first time he had taken it out in a room with other people in it since a shop in July, and he felt the room notice, the way the Great Hall had noticed the scar — a turning of heads, a quiet — and he did not look up, and he did not look down, and he laid the wand on the desk in front of him with the tip towards the window and his hands flat beside it, and waited.

Beside him, Hermione had hers out and was holding it the way the book said and looking at him, not the book.

“Now,” said Flitwick. “I know what the other houses do in the first lesson. They do the register and they tell you what a charm is and they send you away with a chapter. I am not going to do that, because I have never in forty-one years seen the point of telling a child what a thing is before they have done one, and because I have twenty-six eleven-year-olds in front of me with wands they have never used, and the single most important thing I can do with them this morning is let them find out what it feels like.” He picked up his own wand, which was short and dark and had been in his hand so long it looked like a finger. “The simplest charm there is. Light. The word is Lumos. The movement is a short flick, up, like this. You are going to say the word and do the flick and most of you are going to get nothing, and some of you are going to get a spark, and one or two of you are going to get a flicker, and I am going to be delighted with every single one of those outcomes, because every one of them is the first time, and the first time is the only time it is ever new.”

He looked, for a quarter-second, at the back of the room. Harry saw it. Nobody else could have.

“Go on, then. All together. Nobody will hear you over everyone else.”

Twenty-six eleven-year-olds said Lumos and flicked.

Most of them got nothing. Padma Patil got a spark, bright and gone. Terry Boot got a flicker that went out when he breathed. Hermione, beside him, got a light — a real one, small, yellow, wavering at the tip of her wand like a candle in a draught — and made a sound Harry had heard from Mrs Osei over an acceptance letter, and the light went out, and she said “Oh,” and did it again, and got it again.

Harry had not said anything. He had not flicked. He was sitting with his hands flat on the desk beside the holly and watching Hermione’s light, because he had never once in his life been in a room where someone else was doing it for the first time, and it was, he thought, the finest thing he had seen since a board in the rain.

“Dr Potter.”

The room went quiet in a different way.

Flitwick was looking at him from the stack of books with the beam turned down to something more careful, something Harry recognised from a woman in tartan on a step: a professional, who had read the receipts, and had a chair ready, and had decided to find out.

“You have not tried it.”

“No, sir. I was watching Hermione. I’ve never seen anyone do it for the first time. I wanted to.” He put his hand on the holly. “I’ll try it now, if you’d like. I should tell you first that I have done it once before, in a shop, and that it didn’t come out the way the book says, and that I promised Professor McGonagall on a pavement I would warn people before I did a thing that might be impossible so that nobody found out in a shop again. So: this might not be sparks. I’m sorry. I don’t know how to make it be sparks.”

Flitwick looked at him for a long moment.

“Dr Potter,” he said, “I have been waiting since February of 1987 to see what you do with a wand, and I have read two pages about Dementors four times and a professor of mathematics’ letter to a county council and a children’s book about prime numbers, and I have a chair in my office that I borrowed back from the Headmaster yesterday afternoon because I was told I might need it, and I should like you to do whatever it is, in front of these twenty-five children, because they are going to spend seven years in a school with you and they had better find out on the first morning what that means.” He put his own wand down. “Lumos. Please.”

Harry picked up the holly.

He had held it for sixteen years without sending anything down it, and had learned what it was by the holding — not a telephone; a thing that edited, that sanded a ragged sentence on its way through according to its grain — and he had wondered, for sixteen years, what it would do with a sentence that had nothing to sand. He had found out once, for four seconds, in a shop. He found out now.

He did not say the word. He had never needed the word; the word was a handle for people who could not see the thing; he had been seeing the thing since he was six. He built light — the candle’s cousin, the bracket sanded smooth by a thousand hands, held in the back of his mind the way you hold a note — and he did the flick, because the room expected a flick and he had decided on the stair at nine o’clock the day before that he would give rooms what they expected where it cost nothing, and he sent it, and held.

The light came up at the tip of the holly like a thought arriving.

White. Steady. A single point, not a flicker, not a spark, lighting the dust in the air of the room so that every mote of it hung still and separate, and he held it — one second, two, three — and the room did not breathe, and Flitwick on his stack of books had gone perfectly still with his hand on the lectern.

Then Harry did the thing he had done in a cupboard the night of the Standard Books, in his head, and had never done with a wand, because he had never had one: he found the pushing part of the sentence, the part that said how much, and he turned it down. Slowly. The way you would turn a lamp. The white point dimmed — not went out; dimmed — to half, to a quarter, to a glow at the tip no brighter than the end of a cigarette, and held there, and he turned it back up, to full, and down again, and let it go.

The dust fell.

Nobody said anything for a long time, and the long time was long for them.

“That,” said Terry Boot, from the chair with a leg, in a voice that had gone very small, “isn’t in the book.”

“No,” said Harry. “The book has Lumos. It doesn’t have a dimmer. I wrote the dimmer when I was seven, on paper, in a cupboard, because it seemed to me that a light you couldn’t turn down was only half a light, and nobody in seven books had thought to ask. That’s the first time I’ve done it with a wand. It works.” He put the holly down on the desk with the tip towards the window. “I’m sorry. I did say it might not be sparks.”

Flitwick had not moved. He was still standing on his stack of books with his hand on the lectern, and his face was doing a thing that Harry had seen on exactly one other face in his life, and the face had been Mrs Figg’s, in a kitchen, over a cat that had just gone up the stairs.

“Dr Potter,” said Filius Flitwick, very quietly. “I think I should like to sit down.”

“There’s a chair, sir. You said.”

“It is in my office.”

“Then I think,” said Harry, “that we had better go to your office. After class. If that’s all right. I’ve got something in a biscuit tin I’d like to show you, and I’d like Hermione to come, because she got a light on the first go and I saw her hold it steadier the second time by watching, and I think she can learn to see it, and I’ve never had anyone who could.”

Flitwick looked at Hermione, who had gone red to the hair and had her wand still in her hand with nothing at the end of it and was looking at Harry with the face from over the biscuit on the train.

“Miss Granger,” said Flitwick. “Did you.”

“I — yes, sir. I think so. I watched how still he kept his hand. It wasn’t his hand. It was — I don’t know what it was. But I watched it and mine stopped wobbling.” She swallowed. “Is that cheating?”

“Miss Granger,” said Flitwick, and the beam came back into his face like a tide, “that is Charms. That is the whole of it. Forty-one years I have been trying to teach children to watch, and you have just done it on the first morning because the thing you were watching was worth it.” He got down off his stack of books. “Class. You have all cast your first charm, and I am delighted with every one of you, and that is not a thing I say; it is a thing I mean. Chapter one for Friday. Dr Potter and Miss Granger will stay. Mr Boot, mind the leg.”

Flitwick’s office was on the same floor and was small and warm and full of books that had been read, and the chair was in it, and Flitwick sat down in it the moment he was through the door, with the air of a man who had been standing on a stack of books for forty-one years and had just been given permission to stop.

“Show me,” he said.

Harry put the biscuit tin on the desk. It was the old Edinburgh one, with the lid gone soft at the hinge, and he opened it and took out the Second Edition — four hundred and twelve pages, in the private marks, with the nearest English beside each in the round hand — and put the first page in front of a very small man who had thought about the shape of a charm for forty-one years and had, Harry suspected, never once been shown one.

Flitwick looked at it for a long time.

He could not read it. Harry had known he would not be able to; nobody could; the marks were a language with one speaker. But he watched Flitwick’s eyes go down the page the way Carrow’s had gone down forty pages at a long table — checking — and he watched them stop, a third of the way down, at the place where the spine of the thing was drawn, the pointing and the pushing, and Flitwick put one small finger on it and said, in a voice that had lost all its bounce:

“That’s Blishwick.”

“Yes, sir. The spine is. He got the spine. He got the pointing part and the pushing part and he guessed at the middle and the guess was wrong and he said so in a footnote and apologised. I wrote not quite in his margin when I was seven. This is the rest.”

“The rest.” Flitwick turned the page. The second page was brackets — the first twenty of the hundred and sixty, each with its name in their language and his, each with what it did and what it cost and how it had been sanded. He turned another. He turned twelve, and did not speak, and Hermione stood by the door with her hands clasped and her chin up and did not speak either, and Harry sat on the edge of a stool that had been made for first-years and waited, which he was good at.

“I can’t read it,” said Flitwick at last. He said it the way Carrow had said page nine is wrong: as a fact, to be stated plainly and faced. “Not a word of it. I know what it is — I have been looking for this page for forty-one years and I know it when I see it, the way a man who has never seen the sea knows it from the smell — but I cannot read a single mark.” He looked up. “Can anyone?”

“No, sir. Not yet. I made the marks when I was six because English had no words, and I’ve been the only one who could see what they stand for, because I’m the only one who could—” he stopped, at the edge, and stepped back from it, because it was not yet time and because this man would ask how and deserve an answer “—who could stand still long enough to see the thing go by. Blishwick said it couldn’t be seen. He was right, for him. It can be seen. I’ve been seeing it since I was six and writing it down since I was seven and I have never once had anyone to show, and I would like — Professor, I would very much like to teach you to read it. And Hermione. I think it can be taught. I think the seeing can’t, or not yet, but the reading can, the way you can read Greek without having been to Athens. It would take a year. I have a term for the first thing and seven years for the rest, and I’d rather not spend them as the only speaker.”

Flitwick looked at the page under his finger for a long moment.

“Dr Potter,” he said, “when I was twenty-two I wrote to the Department of Mysteries and asked whether anyone there had ever observed the casting moment, and they wrote back and said that the question was not well-formed. I have had the letter in a drawer for sixty years. I should like you to know that I am going to burn it this evening.” He closed the Second Edition, carefully, with both hands, the way Harry held things on stages. “Saturday mornings. Here. Nine o’clock. You will teach me to read it and I will teach you what I know about this—” he touched his own wand, the short dark one that looked like a finger “—which is not nothing, and which I suspect you will find has holes in it, and I should like you to tell me where. Miss Granger will attend. Miss Granger will attend every session, because she held a light steady on the second go by watching and I have never once seen that and I want to see it again.”

“Yes, sir,” said Hermione, in a voice that had very nearly gone.

“And now,” said Flitwick, “I should like to ask the question I have been wanting to ask since a squib wrote to the Headmaster in 1987 that you sit on the floor and are sorry for a cat until the cat gets up. I have read that sentence perhaps forty times. I told the Headmaster it had the shape of a prayer.” He leaned forward in the chair. “Is it?”

Harry thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how to think about anything.

“No, sir,” he said. “A prayer doesn’t expect an answer you can check. This does. You ask, and either the cat gets up or it doesn’t, and if it doesn’t you’ve asked wrong and you go back and find out where. It’s more like—” he searched, and found it in a room with chalk on the back of a door “—it’s more like a proof. You build it honestly, every step, and you put it in front of the world, and the world either says yes or it says fourth line from the bottom, you’ve let the thing have two names. It doesn’t care who you are. It only cares whether the thing holds.” He paused. “I didn’t know that about proofs until I met a professor of mathematics. I knew it about cats first.”

Flitwick sat back in the chair.

“A proof,” he said. “Hm. Yes. That is — yes.” He looked at the biscuit tin. “Dr Potter. The thing you did in my classroom. The dimmer. I have been teaching Lumos for forty-one years and I have never once thought to ask whether it could be turned down, and I am a Charms master, and that is my job. Why has nobody asked?”

“Because nobody could see the part that says how much. It happens in the quarter-second. You say the word and flick and the light comes and you think the word did it, and you never see that there was a sentence under the word with a part in it you could have turned.” He looked at the very small man in the chair. “You’re all shouting down a telephone with your eyes shut, sir. It works. It’s worked for a thousand years. But you’ve never once seen what you were saying, and so you’ve never once edited it, and there are a hundred and sixty charms in the Standard Books and I think about forty of them have a dimmer nobody’s found.”

Flitwick did not say anything for a while. Then he laughed — short, startled, the way Dumbledore had over a handkerchief — and the laugh had forty-one years in it.

“Forty,” he said. “Forty. Miss Granger, make a note. Saturday. Nine. Bring the tin.”

“The dimmer,” said Hermione, on the stairs down, in a sentence with no pause in it anywhere. “You turned it down. You said there’s a part that says how much and you turned it and nobody has ever — how did you — what does it look like, the part, is it in the marks, is that what the marks are, can I—”

“Saturday,” said Harry.

“But—”

“Saturday. I promised him first. You’ll be in the room; you’ll see it the same minute he does.” He stopped on the landing, where there was a window that looked at the mountains, and looked at them, because he had never seen a mountain before Sunday and had decided to look at them whenever there was one. “Hermione. You held yours steady on the second go. By watching. I want you to understand what that was, because Flitwick does and I don’t think you do yet.”

“I watched your hand.”

“You watched past my hand. You said so; you said it wasn’t the hand. You watched the thing the hand was doing, which you can’t see yet, and your own went steadier anyway, because something in you saw it even though you didn’t.” He turned from the window. “I spent four years learning to see it. You did it in a second by looking at someone who could. I don’t know what that means. I think it means that it can be caught. Like an accent. I think it means that if I stand in rooms with people long enough and hold things steady, some of them are going to start holding things steady without knowing why, and then I can show them the marks and they’ll already half know them.” He paused. “I think that’s how it spreads. Not the tin. The watching. The tin is for after.”

Hermione looked at him on the landing with the face from over the biscuit, and he watched her — he had all the time in the world to watch her — go through surprise, and suspicion, and arithmetic, and arrive at the place she had arrived at on the train, which was oh.

“That’s why you want me in every room,” she said slowly. “Not because I chase. Because I catch.”

“Both. The chasing is why I like you. The catching is why it matters.” He started down the stairs. “And because I’ve been the only one in the room for ten years and I’m told it’s better. Come on. We’ve got Transfiguration, and I promised a woman in tartan I wouldn’t bore her first-years by being perfect at it, and I’ve got to work out how to do that before two o’clock.”

“How do you do that?”

“I don’t know yet,” said Harry. “I’ve never had to be bad at anything on purpose that I couldn’t make look like an accident. It’s going to be the hardest thing I do this week.”

It was.

He put Flitwick’s face on the shelf that night, by the window that looked at mountains, with the others — Carrow’s over page nine, Figg’s over the cat, Aldous’s over page ninety — because he had understood, in a small warm office with a chair in it, a thing about the man that he wanted to keep.

He has been standing on a stack of books for forty-one years, teaching children to shout down a telephone, and knowing — knowing the whole time, the way Blishwick knew — that there was a thing under the shout that he could not see. He wrote to the Department of Mysteries at twenty-two and they told him the question was not well-formed. He kept the letter sixty years. He is going to burn it tonight.

I do not know what it is like to want to see a thing for sixty years and not be able to. I have never once wanted to see a thing and not seen it; that is the whole of what I am. But I watched his face when he put his finger on the spine and said "that's Blishwick", and I think I know now what the far side of the wall looks like from the other side, and I think it looks like a very small man in a chair who has just been shown the sea.

Saturday. Nine. The tin. I am going to teach a Charms master to read. I taught myself off cereal packets and it took five months. He has sixty years of knowing there was something to read. I think it will take him a term.

Hermione catches. I did not know that could happen. If it can happen to her it can happen to others. Count them. Make a list of who holds steadier after watching. That is the first page of the table.

Hedwig was on the sill, back from Surrey with a letter in a hand he knew from a shoebox, and he took it, and it said, on one side of one sheet, in the fence voice: The swing is on the wall. The gutters are fine. Your uncle told the Rotary about the traffic. Tea was at six. — P.D. And under that, in the other voice, smaller, as if added after the ink had dried: I am glad the flask was still warm.

He put it in the tin with the Second Edition and McGonagall’s two sentences about the Trace, because it was for the house and the house was in there now too, and lay down by the window with the castle singing one long note under everything, and did not sleep, and had known he would not, and found he did not mind, because it was Wednesday, and Saturday was three days off, and three days was most of two years, and he had a Charms master to teach to read.
