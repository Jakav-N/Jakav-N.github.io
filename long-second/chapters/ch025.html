Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 25

Two Brackets

He failed at the match four times before Professor McGonagall caught him at it, and the four failures were the most difficult magic he had ever done, and she saw through every one of them from the front of the room without moving from her desk.

The lesson had begun the way Hogwarts: A History said her lessons began, which Hermione had told him on the train and he had not believed, and which turned out to be true: a desk, and a pig, and the pig a desk again, in about four seconds, with no word spoken and no visible movement of the wand beyond a thing that might have been a nod. The room had made the sound rooms made. Harry had not made any sound at all, because he had been reading it — the whole sentence, as it went past, the old bracket and the new and the long patient becoming between them, held in a mind that had been holding brackets for thirty-four years and did not know it was being watched — and when the pig was a desk again he sat very still and thought: she holds both. She holds them for a quarter of a second and does not know she is doing it and does it anyway, and she has done it ten thousand times and never once seen it go by.

“Transfiguration,” said McGonagall, “is some of the most complex and dangerous magic you will learn at this school. Anyone messing about in my class will leave and not come back. You have been warned.” She looked down the rows. Her eyes went over Harry at the same speed they went over everyone else, and he thought, again, good. “Matches. One each. By the end of the hour I should like a needle. I should be surprised to get one.”

Hermione got one in forty minutes, which McGonagall remarked upon in a voice that gave nothing away and that Hermione would carry for a week. Padma got silver on the end. Terry Boot got a match that had developed opinions about being a match and expressed them by rolling off the desk.

Harry had decided, on the stairs down from Flitwick’s office with a promise in his pocket, that he would not do it on the first try. He had reasoned it out between the third floor and the second, which was most of an afternoon. A woman in tartan had said I will not have you bore my first-years by being perfect at them, and she had meant it, and she had been right: he had watched Hermione get a light on the first morning and had understood that a room full of first times was a room full of a thing he had never once had and could not have, and that a boy at the back turning matches to needles at a glance would take the first time out of twenty-five other children’s hands. So he would be ordinary. He had never once been ordinary on purpose at anything, and he had a wand now, and the wand changed what ordinary could mean.

Because the thing on kitchen floors would not come for a lie. He had proved that forty times; it sat with its hands folded at I want the pencil to move and would not get up for a sentence he did not mean. But the wand was not the thing on kitchen floors. The wand carried. It took what he sent down it and sanded it and delivered it, and it did not, as far as sixteen years of holding had told him, ask whether he meant it. It only asked whether the sentence was there.

So he could send a sentence that was less. On purpose. A bracket with a part left out. Not a lie — he was not telling the match it was a needle and hoping; he was telling it, precisely, be about a third of the way to a needle and stop, which was a true thing to say and a thing he had never once in his life been able to do with a cat or a pot or a pair of hands, because the asking would not have stood for it. The wand stood for it. He found that out on the first try, with the match going a dull grey halfway up and stopping exactly where he had said, and sat for a quarter-second looking at it with a feeling he did not have a word for and wrote down later as the first time I have ever been able to be bad at something honestly.

He did it three more times. Silver at one end. A point and no eye. A needle that was still, obstinately, made of wood. Each one a sentence with a part turned down, sent true, held, delivered; each one the exact failure a first-year would have produced if a first-year had been able to produce exactly what he intended, which no first-year could. He was, he thought, at the fourth, rather proud of them. They were good failures. They had the texture of the real thing.

“Dr Potter.”

She had not moved from the desk. She was looking at him down the length of the room with the square spectacles catching the window, and her face was doing nothing at all, which he had learned on a step in Surrey was the face she used when she had decided something.

“Come here. Bring the match.”

He went. The room watched him go, the way the Great Hall had watched him walk to a stool, and he carried the match the way he carried things on stages.

“Put it down.”

He put it down on her desk. It was the fourth one: a needle, to the eye, in every respect, except that it was wood.

McGonagall looked at it. Then she looked at him, over the spectacles, for a long moment, and said, quietly enough that only the front row would hear and plainly enough that the front row would carry it:

“That is a very good imitation of a first-year. It is, I think, the best imitation of a first-year I have ever seen, and I have been imitated by a great many of them. The silver on the second one was particularly well judged. Do it properly.”

He looked at her. She looked back. He had the strong sense that she had been waiting since a lounge in Surrey to say do it properly to him in her own classroom, and that she had enjoyed the four matches as much as he had, and that neither of them was going to say so.

“Yes, Professor.”

He picked up the holly. He built the sentence — the old bracket, match, and the new, needle, and the becoming between them, the one he had written in his marks at seven under the stairs and saved in the back of the blue notebook for the day he had a wand — and he held both, the way the book said no student could and the woman at the desk did ten thousand times without seeing, and he sent it, and the match was a needle.

Steel. An eye. A point that caught the window. No silver halfway; no wood left; a needle, the way the desk had been a pig.

Then, because she had said properly and he had decided on the stairs that properly meant the whole thing, he held the two brackets the other way round, and sent it again, and the needle was a match.

Then a needle. Then a match. Four times, each one clean, each one in the time it took to breathe, and he put the holly down on her desk with the tip towards the window and his hands flat beside it, and the match lay there being a match, and the room did not make any sound at all.

McGonagall looked at the match for a long time.

“Back,” she said at last. “And forth.”

“Yes, Professor. It’s the same sentence. You just hold the brackets the other way. I don’t know why the book says you can’t go back; you can. I think the book means most people can’t hold both long enough to go either way on purpose, and so they go the one way they’ve practised and stop. You go both. I watched you do it with the pig.”

The room had stopped breathing. Hermione, in the second row, had her hand over her mouth.

“You watched me,” said McGonagall.

“Yes. You held the desk and the pig at the same time for about a quarter of a second and you didn’t know you were doing it. You’ve done it ten thousand times. It’s — Professor, it’s very good. It’s the cleanest I’ve seen anyone hold two since the roof. I wanted to tell you that in a room where it would be rude and I’ve decided I don’t mind being rude about this one thing.”

Minerva McGonagall looked at the Boy Who Lived across a match on her desk for a long moment, with her face doing nothing at all, and then she did a thing he had seen her do once, on a pavement in the Charing Cross Road, with a carrier bag from a shop that had closed in 1979: the severe mouth moved, at one corner, by an amount that only he could have measured.

“Dr Potter,” she said. “Sit down. Help Mr Boot with his match, which has just rolled under Miss Patil’s chair. Do not turn it into anything. Do not turn anything of Mr Boot’s into anything. Help him.” She picked up the match from her desk and looked at it. “My office. Tuesdays. Six o’clock. We shall discuss what you did and did not watch me do, and you will not do it in this classroom again, because I will not have twenty-five children spend seven years watching you turn things back and forth instead of learning to turn them the one way, and because—” the mouth did the thing again “—because I have been teaching this subject for thirty-four years and I should like, for once, to be told what I am doing by someone who can see it, and I should like to be told in private, where I can be as rude back as I please.”

“Yes, Professor.”

“And Dr Potter.” He had turned. “The four matches. I shall want to know how you did those as well. I have never once seen a student fail exactly. It is not a thing that can be done.”

“No, Professor,” said Harry. “It wasn’t. I found out at the first one that it was. I’ll tell you on Tuesday. I’ve written it down.”

He went and helped Terry Boot with his match, which had rolled under Padma Patil’s chair and was lying there with the air of a match that had made its point. He did not turn it into anything. He sat beside Terry and watched Terry try, and on the fourth try he said, “Hold the wand a bit stiller — not your hand, the — never mind your hand. Look at the match. Don’t look at the wand. The wand knows where it is,” and Terry looked at the match, and the match went silver at one end, and Terry Boot said “Oh,” in the exact voice Hermione had used over a light, and Harry sat back and thought: there. That’s two.

Tuesday at six was a small office with a fire in it and a cat basket with no cat and a desk that had been tidy for thirty-four years, and she did not offer him tea, which he respected, and she sat behind the desk with her hands folded and said, “The four matches. Begin.”

He began. He told her about the thing on kitchen floors and the thing in the wand and the difference between them — that the first would not come for a sentence he did not mean, and the second would carry any sentence that was there, and that a sentence that said a third of the way and stop was a thing that was there — and she listened with her face doing nothing, and when he had finished she said:

“You are telling me that a wand does not care whether the wizard means it.”

“I’m telling you it doesn’t check. The thing I do without one checks; it goes looking for whether the need is real and won’t move if it isn’t. The wand doesn’t go looking. It takes what you give it and sands it and sends it. I think that’s why wizards can do things they don’t mean — hurt people they didn’t mean to hurt, in a temper, with a sentence they didn’t build. The wand didn’t ask. I think that’s the most dangerous thing about them and I don’t think anyone’s said so in a book, because nobody’s ever had both to compare.”

McGonagall was quiet for a long moment.

“Nobody has ever had both,” she said.

“No. I had the one for five years before I had the other. I thought the other would be the same thing with a — with a better line. It isn’t. It’s a different thing. It’s faster and it carries and it doesn’t ask. I’ve had it eight days and I’ve done four honest failures and a dimmer and a match both ways and I’m already a little afraid of it, Professor, in a way I was never once afraid of a cat.” He looked at the fire. “I think that’s right. I think I should be. I think the first wizards were, and then somebody made a stick that sanded the fear off along with everything else, and nobody’s been properly afraid since.”

She did not say anything for a while. Then she got up, and went to a cabinet, and came back with a thing he had not expected, which was a photograph, in a frame, old, and she put it on the desk between them and did not sit down.

It was a classroom. This classroom; he knew the windows. Thirty children in robes, in rows, in the slightly stiff attitudes of children who have been told to hold still for a thing that will take a while, and in the second row, with red hair down her back and her chin up and a face that was looking at the camera as if working out how to help it and not having decided whether it deserved it, a girl of eleven. And behind her, one row back, with black hair standing straight up and an expression of elaborate innocence, a boy who was quite plainly in the middle of doing something to the chair in front of him.

“Nineteen seventy-one,” said McGonagall. “The first week. Your mother turned her match into a needle in twenty minutes, which was the fastest I had seen, and then — I had not asked for this — turned it back, because, she said, she wanted to see whether she could, and I told her that it could not be done, and she said oh, in a voice I have never forgotten, and did not do it again in my classroom for seven years, and I have wondered on and off since whether she went on doing it somewhere else.” She looked at the photograph. “Your father turned the leg of the chair in front of him into a snake in the second week and was given detention, which he treated as a compliment. He was the best natural Transfigurer I taught in thirty years and he never once, in seven years, did a thing carefully. Your mother did everything carefully. They were, between them, the two halves of what I have spent my life trying to teach, and neither of them had the other half, and I have never said that to anyone.”

Harry looked at the girl in the second row for a long time.

Turned it back. Because she wanted to see whether she could. And was told it could not be done, and said oh.

“She could see it,” he said. “Not the way I can. But she could feel there was a back. She did it once, and you told her no, and she stopped in your classroom and went on somewhere else, because she wasn’t going to argue with a professor at eleven and she wasn’t going to stop either.” He looked up. “That’s — Professor, that’s what my aunt said. She kept trying till the end. And Mrs Figg. Your mother was the same. Everyone said. And the Headmaster, on Monday: neither of us ever once saw her shout. She was asking. All along. Not the way I do; she didn’t have the marks; but she could feel the shape of it and she went where it went and she didn’t let anyone tell her it couldn’t be done, she just said oh and did it somewhere they weren’t looking.”

McGonagall did not say anything.

“And he never did a thing carefully,” said Harry, more slowly, looking at the boy with his hand on the chair leg. “Which is the wand. The wand doesn’t ask. He had the wand and the nerve and he sent, and she had the — the other thing, the asking, and she held. The two halves.” He stopped. “And I’ve got both. That’s what you’re telling me. That’s why you got the photograph out.”

“I got the photograph out,” said McGonagall, “because you turned a match back and forth four times in my classroom and said I don’t know why the book says you can’t, and I had heard that sentence once before, in that room, in 1971, in a different voice, and I have carried it for twenty years, and I wanted you to know whose it was.” She sat down. “And yes. Both. You asked me on a pavement whether I knew it was impossible. I did not say then what I thought, which was that I had taught two children who between them could have done it, and that they had not lived to find out, and that I had just watched their son do it in a shop. I am saying it now. In private. Where I may be as rude as I please.”

He sat in the small office with the fire going and the cat basket with no cat and the photograph on the desk, and found that he could not, for a moment, say anything at all, which was becoming, he thought, a habit of his in rooms with women in them who had held doors.

“May I—” he said, and stopped, and tried again. “Professor. May I look at it a while longer. I’ve got one photograph of her, on a swing, with my aunt. I haven’t got one of him at all. I didn’t know he — I didn’t know about the chair.”

“You may look at it as long as you like,” said Minerva McGonagall. “And then I am going to tell you what you watched me do with the pig, in your own words, and you are going to tell me whether I have understood them, and I warn you now that if I have not I shall want to know why, at length.” She paused. “That is what Tuesdays are. I had them explained to me by a professor of mathematics in a covering letter two years ago. I believe the phrase was she found the Hungarian trap in February and he would have been in there a month. I have never in my life been anyone’s Hungarian trap, Dr Potter, and I find I should like to try.”

He looked at the photograph a while longer. Then he told her, in his own words, what she did with the pig, and she had understood it, nearly, and where she had not she wanted to know why, at length, and it was half past eight before either of them noticed, and she said that will do in the voice she used for things that had not done at all, and he went down the stairs in the dark with a boy and a chair leg on the shelf beside a girl on a swing, and thought that he had come to Scotland with one photograph and now had two, and that the second had been in a cabinet for twenty years waiting for someone to say I don’t know why the book says you can’t.

Hermione was in the common room with A Beginner’s Guide to Transfiguration open at the chapter on reversal, which was two paragraphs long and said that it was not possible, and she looked up when he came in and said, “You were two and a half hours.”

“She had a photograph.”

“Of what?”

He thought about it. He had decided on the train that there were things that were his and things that were everyone’s and that he would learn, this year, where the line was, and he found that the line had moved while he was in a small office with a fire.

“My parents,” he said. “In her classroom. Nineteen seventy-one. My mother turned her match back and was told it couldn’t be done and said oh.” He sat down across from her. “Hermione. I need you to understand something about Tuesdays, and I’m going to say it badly because I’ve never said it before. I’m not going there to learn Transfiguration. I know Transfiguration; I’ve had the book since I was seven. I’m going there because a woman who has done a thing ten thousand times without seeing it has agreed to let me tell her what it looks like, and to be rude back, and I have never had anyone to tell. I had a professor of mathematics for five years and I could tell her the maths, but not — not this. This is the first time.”

“I know,” said Hermione. “I was in Flitwick’s office. I saw his face.” She closed the book. “Can I come? On Tuesdays?”

“No.”

She looked at him.

“Not because I don’t want you there. Because she doesn’t, yet, and she’s right not to. Saturdays are Flitwick’s and you’re in them because he said so and because you catch. Tuesdays are hers, and she’s a woman who kept a photograph in a cabinet for twenty years and got it out tonight for one person, and I’m not going to walk in next week with someone she didn’t ask for.” He looked at the fire. “Give her a term. She’ll ask. She asked me on a pavement whether I knew a thing was impossible; she’ll ask you something by Christmas, and when she does it’ll be because she’s decided, and not because I pushed her through a door. That’s how she works. I’ve read her.”

Hermione sat with that.

“You read everyone,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Have you read me?”

He had, on a train, in four seconds, and every day since, and he thought about whether to say so and decided that the line had moved far enough for one evening.

“Some,” he said. “Enough to know you’d ask that. Not enough to know what you’d want me to say.” He stood up. “Saturday. Nine. The tin. I’ll show you the pointing part first; it’s the easiest. You’ll see it in about a week and then you’ll be annoyed you didn’t see it before, and that’s how everyone’s going to feel about everything, and I think it’s the best feeling there is, and I’ve never once got to watch it happen to someone else.”

“You watched Terry.”

“Terry got a match to go silver. That’s a spark. I mean the other thing. The oh.” He went towards the stairs. “My mother said it in 1971. I’d like to hear it a great many more times before I’m done.”

He wrote it down by the window, in the forty-seventh pad, with the mountains outside being mountains, and he wrote it for the reader in fifty years, because this was the sort of thing the reader would need.

The wand does not ask. I have had it eight days and I know this now the way I know the hall clock. It carries and it sands and it sends and it does not once stop to find out whether I mean it. That is why I could fail four times exactly, which the thing on kitchen floors would never have let me do, and it is why I am a little afraid of it, and I think I am right to be, and I think the fear is the first honest thing I have learned here that is not in a book.

She held the desk and the pig at once. She did not know. I told her in a room where it was rude. She got out a photograph.

My mother turned a match back in 1971 and was told it could not be done and said oh. My father turned a chair leg into a snake and took the detention as a compliment. She asked; he sent. I have both. McGonagall has taught two thousand first-years and never said that sentence to anyone and said it to me in a room with a fire, as rude as she pleased.

I came here with one photograph. I have two.

Terry Boot said oh. That is two of twenty-five in a week. Count them.

He closed the pad. He did not sleep. He had known he would not. He lay by the window with a castle singing one long note under him and a boy with his hand on a chair leg on the shelf beside a girl on a swing, and he found that for the first time in his life he was not thinking about the next thing, and he let that be, for a while, because he had read somewhere that a person was allowed to.
