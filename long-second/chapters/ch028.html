Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 28

The Workshop

Hermione read her first whole sentence on the third Saturday, at twenty past ten, in Flitwick’s office, off page thirty-four of a biscuit tin, and the sentence was warm, and she read it the way a child reads a first sentence off a cereal packet — a mark at a time, aloud, with her finger on each — and when she got to the end of it she stopped, and did not say oh.

She said nothing at all. For four seconds. Which was long for her and most of a quarter-hour for him, and he sat on the first-year stool and watched her face and did not breathe, because he had never once in his life seen this from the outside and had wanted to since he was six.

“It’s—” she said. And stopped. And then, slowly, with her finger still on the last mark: “It’s not a description. I thought it was a description. I thought you’d written down what the charm does, in a — in a code, and I was learning the code. It isn’t that. This is — Harry, this is what happens. In the — in the moment. This is the thing I can’t see. You’ve written down the thing I can’t see, and I’m reading it, and I can’t see it and I can read it and those are—” She looked up. “Those are different. Aren’t they. Seeing and reading. You can do both. I can only do one.”

“Yes,” said Harry. “That’s exactly it. That’s the whole thing. You’ve got it in three weeks and I didn’t have the words for it till I was eight.”

“But if I can read it—” She was looking at the page again. “If I can read it, then I know what the pushing part is, and I know where it is in the sentence, and I know that in Lumos it’s the third mark and it’s the one you turned down. So if I say Lumos and I think about the third mark while I—”

She had her wand out before she had finished the sentence. Flitwick, in the chair, made a small sound that was not quite a word. Harry did not move.

“Lumos.”

The light came. Yellow, small, wavering, the light she had made on the first morning and every morning since. And then — he saw it; he was the only one in the room who could have — her face did a thing, a going-inward, a reaching for the third mark, and the light did not dim.

But it steadied.

It stopped wavering. Not all the way; it was not his white point; it was a candle in a room where someone had shut a door. But it had been a candle in a draught for three weeks and it was not now, and she held it, and looked at it, and the thing came up through her face from wherever it had been since a train, and she said it, at last, very quietly, to the light:

“Oh.”

Flitwick had both hands over his mouth.

“I didn’t turn it down,” said Hermione. “I couldn’t find the — I know where the mark is but I can’t find the thing it’s for, not in me, not yet. But I knew it was there. And knowing it was there was enough to—” She let the light go out. “Harry. Is that what it is? Is that what you meant by catching? That you don’t have to see it. You just have to know it’s there and the knowing does something.”

“I think so,” said Harry. “I didn’t know that till just now. I’ve only ever done it from the inside. I’ve never watched anyone do it from the outside who knew the marks.” He looked at Flitwick. “Sir. She steadied it by reading. Not by watching me. By reading the page and knowing the third mark was in the sentence. That means it isn’t only catching. It means the marks work — not as a record. As a handle. Like the incantation is a handle, except that the incantation is a handle for the whole sentence and the marks are handles for the parts.”

Filius Flitwick took his hands away from his mouth.

“Forty-one years,” he said, in a voice that had no bounce in it at all. “Forty-one years I have told children that the word is the handle. The word. One handle, for the whole thing, and they shout it, and it works, and that is Charms.” He looked at the page under Hermione’s finger. “And you are telling me that there are — how many marks in Lumos?”

“Seven and a bracket.”

“Seven handles. For a thing I have taught as one.” He got out of the chair, which Harry had not seen him do voluntarily since the first Saturday, and stood very small in the middle of his own office with his short dark wand in his hand, and looked at it. “Miss Granger. Do it again. I want to watch your face.”

She did it again. He watched her face. Harry watched him watch, and saw the moment — a quarter-second; most of a minute — when a Charms master of forty-one years saw something go past that he had never seen, not the thing itself, not the sentence, but the shadow of it on a girl’s face as she reached for a third mark, and understood that there was a shape where he had always been told there was a word.

“Oh,” said Filius Flitwick, very softly, to nobody.

Harry put that on the shelf, next to a woman’s face over a cat, and did not say anything, because some things were better carried than said, and because there were now, by his count, three and a half speakers of a language that had had one for five years, and he wanted to sit with the number before it changed again.

The room was Flitwick’s idea, and the people were nobody’s, and that was, Harry decided afterwards, exactly how it should have been.

“You cannot do this in my office,” Flitwick said, on the fourth Saturday, when there were three of them and a biscuit tin and a bronze disc the size of a saucer that he had got from somewhere and would not say where. “Not because I mind. Because my office is twelve feet across and you are going to need a board, and a bench, and somewhere to put the things that go wrong, and you are going to need — Dr Potter, you know what you are going to need, I have watched you not say it for a month.”

“People,” said Harry.

“People. You cannot build a thing that presses a charm into a blanket with one eleven-year-old who can see and one who can read and one old man who can do neither. You need hands. You need people who will stand in a room while you hold something and come out the far end holding steadier, because that is what you told Miss Granger on a landing and I have been thinking about it since and I believe you are right.” He pushed the bronze disc across the desk. “There is a classroom on the fourth floor that has not been used since 1964 because the floor slopes and the windows face the wrong way for anything a teacher wants. It has a board. I have the key. I am going to give it to you, and I am not going to ask who you let in, and I am going to come on Saturdays and sit in the corner and be told things, and when the Headmaster asks me what is going on in the sloping room I am going to say Charms club, which will be true.”

Harry looked at the key. It was large and old and had a sentence in it so small he nearly missed it, which said, as near as he could put it, this one opens.

“Sir,” he said. “Why?”

Flitwick considered the question with the seriousness it had not quite deserved.

“Because I wrote to the Department of Mysteries at twenty-two,” he said, “and they told me the question was not well-formed, and I kept the letter sixty years and burned it a month ago because a boy showed me a page. And because I watched Miss Granger steady a light by reading a mark, and I have taught two thousand children and never once seen a thing I did not know was there. And because—” the beam came up, and under it something older “—because you are going to do this with or without a room, Dr Potter, in a cupboard if you have to, you have the face of a boy who has done things in cupboards, and I should rather it was a room with a board, and me in the corner, and Minerva knowing where to find you.” He stood up. “Charms club. Thursdays and Saturdays. Bring who you like. Give them the good ones first; I am told that is the method.”

He did not pick them. He had decided that on the train in August — you find the door they already use — and he held to it, and the room filled the way a room fills when there is a thing in it worth coming for, which is sideways, one at a time, each for a reason that was not the reason.

Terry Boot came first, because Terry had got a match to go silver by looking at the match instead of the wand and had said oh, and had come up to Harry in the common room on the Monday and said, “The match. You said the wand knows where it is. How does it know?” — and Harry had said, “Come to the sloping room on Thursday and I’ll show you,” and Terry had come, and had sat on a bench while Harry held a feather two inches off the desk for four seconds, perfectly still, and had said, afterwards, slowly, “It wasn’t your hand. Hermione said it wasn’t your hand. It wasn’t,” and had come back on Saturday without being asked.

Padma Patil came second, because she had watched Terry come back on Saturday, and because she was the kind of person who did not go anywhere twice without a reason and did not like watching other people have reasons she did not. She came in with her chin up and sat down at the far end of the bench and said, “I want to know what you did with the match in McGonagall’s class. Not the needle. The four before it. Nobody fails exactly. I watched you do it and I have been annoyed for a month.” Harry told her. She listened with her arms folded, and at the end she said, “That is the most useful thing anyone has told me since I got here,” and stayed.

Neville came third, and came from the wrong house, and nearly did not come at all.

“I can’t,” he said, in the courtyard, when Harry asked. “It’s a Ravenclaw thing. I’d — Harry, I melted a cauldron. I can’t sit in a room with Padma Patil and—”

“Neville. What did you write in the back of your Potions book?”

Neville went red. “Plants. Gran’s shelf. What they’re for.”

“How many?”

“…Forty-one.”

“Forty-one plants and what each one does. Padma Patil has read the book. You have grown the book.” Harry looked at him. “There is going to be a thing in that room that presses a charm into a blanket, and a charm that checks whether a person is cold, and I have no idea — none — what happens when you press a sentence into wool, because wool is a plant, nearly, or it came off a thing that ate plants, and I don’t know what plants do when you ask them things, and you’re the only person in this school under thirty who does.” He paused. “That’s a real reason. I don’t make up reasons. Come on Thursday. Sit by the door if you want. Nobody is going to ask you what a bezoar is.”

Neville came on the Thursday. He sat by the door. He said nothing for an hour, and then Harry, holding the bronze disc, said, half to himself, “It’s not taking the sentence; it slides off; bronze is smooth and I don’t know why that matters—” and Neville said, from the door, in a small voice, “It’s like a seed. You can’t plant on glass. It needs something to hold on to,” and the room went quiet, and Harry turned round and looked at him for a long moment and said, “Yes. Yes, that’s — Neville, that’s it, that’s the thing I’ve been missing since March,” and Neville Longbottom sat by the door of a sloping room in a Ravenclaw tower and went a colour that was not red, and came back on Saturday, and sat one bench further in.

Penelope Clearwater came fourth, and came because she was a fifth-year prefect and had noticed that four first-years and a Gryffindor were spending Thursday evenings in a room that had not been used since 1964, and had come to send them to bed, and had stood in the doorway for forty minutes watching a match become a needle and a needle a match, and had said, at the end, in a changed voice, “Flitwick knows about this.”

“He gave us the key.”

“Right.” She had looked at the board, where Harry had drawn the seven marks of Lumos in chalk from the back of a door in Egham. “Right. Then I’m going to come on Saturdays, and if anyone asks, you’re a Charms club and I’m supervising, and Potter — I did my OWLs in June and I got an O in Charms and I have never once in five years seen anything like what you did with that needle, and I would like to know why, and I’m prepared to be a prefect about it.” She had sat down. She had not got up again.

And Ron came fifth, and came last, and came for the biscuits, and that was the reason that was not the reason.

Mrs Figg’s tin had arrived by owl on the Wednesday — a new one, enormous, with the Humber Bridge on it, and a note in the right hand that said for the room. Give them the good ones first. I am told there is a room. — A.F. — and Harry had put it on the bench in the sloping room and said nothing, and on the Thursday Ron Weasley had put his head round the door at half past six, having followed a rumour and a smell.

“Neville said there were biscuits.”

“There are. The good ones are on the left. Sit down.”

Ron sat down. He ate four. He watched Harry hold a feather and Hermione steady a light and Padma fail at a match exactly, which she had got the hang of and was unbearable about, and at the end of the hour he said, through the fifth biscuit, the thing nobody else in the room had dared to say in a month:

“This is mad. You know that. You’re eleven and you’ve got a prefect taking notes and Neville talking about seeds and you’re trying to make a — what did you call it — a press, that puts charms in blankets, for Muggles, which is illegal, and Flitwick gave you a key.” He swallowed. “Why?”

The room went quiet.

Harry put the feather down. He had been waiting a month for someone to ask that, and had known it would not be Hermione, who chased, or Terry, who caught, or Padma, who was annoyed, or Neville, who sat by the door, or Penelope, who had an O in Charms and was prepared to be a prefect about it. It would be the one who came for the biscuits and asked the dumb question, because the dumb question was the one everyone else had decided was beneath them, and it was the only one that mattered.

“Because there’s a girl in a hospital in London,” he said, “who was six two years ago and had her photograph in a newspaper because the ward was cold at night and the heating was a line in a budget. And eleven miles from her there’s a hospital on this side where they mend a spine in three places over a weekend and mention it in passing. And nobody — Ron, nobody, in three hundred years — has ever once put the two of them in the same room.” He looked at the Humber Bridge tin. “I can hold a sentence steady. That’s the only thing I can do that nobody else can. If I can hold it steady enough to press it into a bronze disc, and the disc can press it into wool, then a thousand blankets can carry a charm that asks a child how cold she is and gives exactly the difference, and the child never knows, and comes to no harm, and is warm. That’s the whole of it. That’s what the room is for. The Headmaster said bring me a case and I said give me a term and this is the term.” He paused. “It is illegal. As it stands. I asked. The woman in tartan said as it stands in a voice that meant she’d thought about it. I’m going to build the thing and press the charm and show them a blanket and then I’m going to find out what it stands as.”

Ron sat with the fifth biscuit halfway to his mouth for a long moment.

“Right,” he said. “Right. That’s — okay. That’s a reason.” He ate the biscuit. “My mum knits. Jumpers. Every Christmas. She’d — if you need someone to tell you about wool, I mean, I don’t know anything about wool, but I know someone who’s made about four hundred jumpers out of it and complains about every one.” He looked at Neville. “Is that the sort of thing you meant? Seeds?”

“Yes,” said Neville, from one bench further in. “That’s exactly the sort of thing.”

“Right,” said Ron Weasley, and reached for a sixth, and did not leave, and that was five, and a prefect, and a very small man in the corner who had said nothing for an hour and whose face Harry had been watching, and whose face was doing the thing it had done over a girl reading a mark.

Hermione waited until the room had emptied.

He had known she would. He had read her for six weeks and she had a thing she did when she was carrying a sentence she had not yet decided to say, which was to tidy, and she had tidied the bench and the board and the Humber Bridge tin, and when there was nothing left to tidy she turned round and said it.

“You’re using us.”

He did not say anything.

“As instruments. You said so, on the landing — I think that’s how it spreads; the watching. You want people in the room so they’ll catch. Terry caught. I caught. You’re counting. You told me you were counting. You said that is the first page of the table.” Her chin was up. “I don’t mind being in the room, Harry. I want to be in the room; I’d rather be in this room than anywhere I’ve ever been. But I want to know whether I’m in it because you like me or because I steady a light faster than Padma, and I’d like you to tell me the truth, because you told me on a train that you don’t lie to your aunt and I’ve been assuming that extends.”

He sat on the bench in a room that sloped, with a bronze disc in front of him that would not take a sentence because it was smooth, and thought about how to answer honestly, which was the only way he knew how to think about anything.

“Both,” he said. “I told you that on the landing and I meant it and I still do. I like you because you chase. I need you because you catch. I don’t know how to have one without the other, Hermione; I’ve never had either before, I had a professor of mathematics who was both and I didn’t know there was a difference till you asked me just now.” He looked at the disc. “And yes. I’m counting. I count everything. I counted my uncle’s shouting and I count the seconds the thing in Quirrell’s face looks at me and I count how many people in a room say oh. It’s how I know what’s real. If I didn’t count I wouldn’t know that Terry caught in two sessions and you caught in one and Padma hasn’t yet and Neville said seeds and that was the most important thing anyone has said in this room including me. Counting is how I see you. It isn’t instead of liking. It’s how the liking knows it isn’t making things up.”

Hermione looked at him for a long time with her chin up.

“That,” she said finally, “is the strangest thing anyone has ever said to me, and I’ve had a hat tell me who wrote Hogwarts: A History.” She sat down on the bench across from him. “You count because otherwise you wouldn’t trust that you liked me.”

“I count because otherwise I wouldn’t trust anything. I’ve been the only one in the room for ten years. You learn to check.” He pushed the disc an inch towards her, which was Carrow for take it. “I’m sorry. I should have said it before you had to ask. I said on the stairs I didn’t know how to have people in the room and I don’t and I’m doing it badly and I’d rather you told me every time than not.”

“I will,” said Hermione. She picked up the disc. “Every time.” She turned it in her hands. “Neville’s right. It’s smooth. It needs something to hold on to. What if you scored it? Like — like a seed tray. Grooves.”

Harry looked at the disc in her hands for a quarter-second, which was most of a minute, and in the minute he saw a bronze disc with the marks of the doorstep sentence cut into it — not pressed; cut; a sentence in a thing the way the runes were sentences in things, except in his marks instead of theirs, so that the disc did not have to hold the sentence by being asked; it held it by being it — and the whole of the design in the biscuit tin turned over, once, and came down facing a different way.

“Hermione,” he said. “Grooves.”

“Is that—”

“That’s March. That’s the thing I’ve been missing since March. I was trying to press it in. You carve it. You carve it in my marks, and then the disc is the sentence, and then the disc can press because it isn’t holding anything, it just is—” He stood up. “Flitwick. Is he still—”

“He went to dinner.”

“Then tomorrow. Nine. Bring the disc. And Hermione—” He stopped at the door. “Thank you. For asking. And for the grooves. The grooves are better than the asking and I didn’t think anything would be.”

“The asking was better,” said Hermione, with her chin up. “The grooves were just a seed tray. Go to dinner. You haven’t eaten since breakfast; I counted.”

He wrote it in the forty-seventh pad that night, by the window, with the mountains, and he wrote it for the reader in fifty years, because the reader would want to know where the table had started.

Five, and a prefect, and a very small man in a corner. Terry, who caught. Padma, who was annoyed and fails exactly now and is unbearable about it. Neville, who said seeds. Penelope, who got an O and is prepared to be a prefect about it. Ron, who came for the biscuits and asked why, which nobody else would, and then said his mother knits.

And Hermione, who read a whole sentence off page thirty-four and said oh, and steadied a light by knowing the third mark was there, which means the marks are handles and not a record, which Flitwick said oh about, which makes three and a half, and then asked me whether I was using her, and I said both, and she said grooves.

Grooves. Carve it. The disc is the sentence. I have been trying to press since March and the answer was a seed tray and a girl who tidies when she is carrying a thing she has not decided to say.

I am going to count the people who say oh in that room and I am going to tell them I am counting and I am going to let them be angry about it and tell me every time. That is the table. Not the blankets. The blankets are what the table is for. The table is six people and a tin from a woman in Surrey who has never once been in a room with any of them and sent the good ones anyway.

Thursday the thing in the face looked at me for twelve seconds and the door did not move. I am counting that too.

He closed the pad. The Humber Bridge tin was on the sill where he had put it to keep it from Ron, and he looked at it, and at the mountains, and at a bronze disc on the table beside the bed that was smooth and would not take a sentence and would, tomorrow, at nine, be cut, and he lay down and did not sleep and had known he would not, and found that for the first time since a doorstep he was not the only speaker of anything, and that it was better, as he had been told, and that nobody had told him it would be this much better, and that the reader would need to be told that too, later, when he had the words, which he did not, in any of seven languages, yet.
