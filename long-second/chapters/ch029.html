Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 29

The Asking

The graver was Flitwick’s, and was older than Flitwick, and had a handle worn to the shape of a hand that was not Harry’s, and he cut the doorstep sentence into bronze with it over nine evenings in a room that sloped, and it was the slowest thing he had ever done, and he would not have had it faster.

He had known his hands were the slow part since he was two. He had drilled a tongue under a blanket and a pen in an examination hall and a ladle over a flame, and each time the knowing had been a different kind of humbling, and this was the deepest. A sentence he could hold in his head for a quarter-hour of his own time without a mark slipping took him an hour, with a graver, to cut one mark of — the pointing part, a shape like a hook, which was the simplest of the seven and which came out, the first evening, with a wobble in it that he could see from across the room and that Neville, from his bench by the door, could not.

“It looks fine,” said Neville.

“It isn’t. The hook’s meant to close by a hair and it doesn’t. If I press from that the copy will point nearly at the thing and nearly is a cold child in the next bed.” He put the graver down. “Hermione. Would you read it back to me. From the disc. Not the page.”

She read it back from the disc. She got to the hook and stopped, and looked at it, and said, “It — doesn’t close,” and he said, “No,” and she said, “I can see that,” in a voice that had surprise in it, and he said, “Yes. That’s reading. You’re reading bronze now. That’s the first time anyone’s read a sentence off a thing that wasn’t me or a page.”

He recut the hook. It took the rest of the evening. On the second evening he cut the pushing part. On the fourth the bracket began, and the bracket was the whole of the doorstep — the part that checked, that went looking for how cold a thing was and compared it to a thing and gave exactly the difference — and it took five evenings, and on the ninth, at a quarter to nine, with Flitwick in the corner and Penelope taking notes she had stopped pretending were for anyone and Ron eating the last of the Humber Bridge, he put the graver down and the sentence was in the bronze, all of it, every mark, and he sat back and did not touch it.

“Is it done?” said Terry.

“It’s in. I don’t know if it’s done. I don’t know what a sentence does when it’s carved in my marks instead of theirs; nobody does; the runes have forty marks and I’ve got two hundred and twenty and nobody has ever cut one of mine into anything.” He looked at the disc. It lay on the bench, a saucer of bronze with grooves in it, and it was — he reached, the way he reached for a sentence going past at the roses — it was humming. Faint. Under hearing. The way the wall in the yard hummed; the way the castle sang. “It’s holding. Not because I’m holding it. Because it is it. Neville was right. It had something to hold on to.”

Flitwick got out of his chair and came and looked at the disc and did not touch it either.

“Dr Potter,” he said. “That is a rune.”

“Yes, sir. In a language with four speakers.”

“There are forty runes. There have been forty for a thousand years. Nobody has ever—” He stopped. “You have made a new rune. On a Thursday. With my graver.”

“I’ve made a sentence in a thing. The runes are sentences in things in their marks; this is one in mine. It’s the same idea. It’s only new because nobody could see the sentence to cut it.” Harry looked at the hum. “I don’t know if it’ll press. That’s next. That’s November. Tonight I just wanted to know whether bronze would hold a thing I’d written, and it will, and I’d like to sit with that for a minute before I find out what it’s for.”

He sat with it for a minute, which was most of three hours, and the room let him, and at the end of it he put the disc in the biscuit tin with the Second Edition and the two sentences in green ink and a letter for the house, and went to dinner, and did not eat much, and Hermione counted, and said so.

The asking he cast on the Saturday, in the sloping room, because he had decided in September that the first composed sentence he ever sent down holly would be that one, and because Flitwick had a chair, and because he had promised a woman on a pavement that he would tell someone before he did a thing that might be impossible, and he had told Flitwick on the Thursday and Flitwick had said bring a cup.

He brought a cup. It was from the Ravenclaw common room and had a crack in it that ran from the lip to the handle, and he put it on the bench and sat down in front of it and took out the holly, and the room went quiet, because the room had learned in six weeks what it meant when he took out the holly and did not flick.

“This is a sentence I wrote at nine,” he said. “On paper, in a cupboard. It isn’t in any book; it isn’t any of theirs; I built it from the grammar because there was a part of the thing that mends that I could see and nobody else could, and the part was the looking. Everything they’ve got mends a this. Reparo, for a thing that’s broken. A hundred Healing charms, each for a thing that’s wrong, each with its name. The thing that came up through me on a kitchen floor when I was six didn’t know what was wrong with a cat. It went and found out. I wrote that part down on its own. No mending. Just the question.” He put his hand flat on the bench beside the cup. “If it works, it won’t fix the cup. It’ll tell me what’s wrong with it. I know what’s wrong with it; I can see the crack. That’s why it’s a cup and not a person. I want to know whether it answers, and I want to be able to check.”

“And if it doesn’t work?” said Padma.

“Then I find out where I wrote it wrong, and go back. That’s what Saturdays are.” He looked at Flitwick. “Sir. You’ve got the chair?”

“I am in the chair, Dr Potter.”

Harry held the asking.

Forty lines of his marks, built at nine, held in the back of his mind for two years without once being sent, the way a note is held in singing: a bracket that was only the looking part, that went into a thing and asked of every part of it are you the shape you are for? and came back not with a mending but with a map. He had never once sent it. He had held it so long that it had worn a groove in him the way the doorstep sentence had worn one in the bronze, and he found it there, waiting, and sent it down the holly, and held.

Nothing happened to the cup.

That was right. He had not told it to do anything. He had asked. And the asking went down the wand and out through the grain and into the cup, and he felt it go — thinner than a mending, the way the pencil had been thinner than the cat, sent and not risen — and he felt it go looking, the way Mrs Prentice went along a shelf, hand out, sure of the general area, through the cup, every part of it, the lip and the handle and the glaze and the clay under the glaze, asking each part the same question, and then it came back.

It came back as a shape.

Not a picture. Not words. A shape, in the place in him where sentences arrived, the way the roses had arrived, the way the hat had — a map of the cup with the wrongness in it marked, and the wrongness was the crack, lip to handle, which he had known, and was also more than the crack: was a place under the glaze a quarter-inch from the handle where the clay had been fired too fast sixty years ago and had a weakness in it that had not yet become a crack and would, in about a year, if somebody put hot tea in it on a cold morning.

He sat very still.

“Well?” said Hermione.

“It works.” He said it quietly. “The crack, lip to handle. Which we knew. And a — there’s a place by the handle, under the glaze, where it was fired wrong. It isn’t cracked yet. It will be. The asking found it.” He looked at the cup. “I didn’t know that was there. Nobody could have known that was there. The asking looked, and it was there, and it told me.”

“Can I—” said Terry, and stopped, and said it anyway: “Can I check? Is there a way to check?”

“Yes,” said Harry. “That’s the whole point. There’s always a way to check or it isn’t worth doing.” He pushed the cup across the bench. “Hot water. From the urn. Pour it in and watch the handle.”

Terry poured. The room watched the handle. And at eleven seconds — Harry counted — a second crack opened, hairline, a quarter-inch from the handle, under the glaze, exactly where the map had said, and ran an inch, and stopped.

Nobody said anything.

Then Padma Patil, who had been annoyed for six weeks and had got the hang of failing exactly and was unbearable about it, said, in a voice with none of the annoyance in it at all: “It knew. It knew before the water. Harry, that’s not a charm. That’s—” She stopped, because she did not have the word, and Harry found that he loved her a little for stopping, because he did not have it either.

“It’s a question,” he said. “That gets an answer you can check. Carrow would call it an instrument. Mrs Osei would call it a probe. I called it the asking because I was nine and it was the first word I had.” He looked at the very small man in the chair. “Sir. I’d like to try it on something that isn’t a cup. Not a person — not yet, not here. But I’d like to ask you first, because if it works on a person it has to be asked, every time, and I want to practise the asking of the asking before I need it.”

Flitwick had not moved. He was looking at the second crack in the cup with his small hands folded in his lap and his face doing the thing it had done over a girl reading a mark, and he did not answer for a long moment, and when he did his voice had no bounce in it.

“My wrist,” he said. “The left. I broke it in 1947 in a duel I should not have fought and it was set by a man in a hurry and it has ached in the cold for forty-four years and three Healers have told me it is as good as it will get.” He held it out, across the arm of the chair, palm down, the way an old woman had once held out two hands across a table. “Ask it.”

Harry did not take the wrist. He had learned on a kitchen floor that you did not need to touch; you needed to mind. He held the asking, and sent it, and it went down the holly and across the three feet of air between a bench and a chair and into an old man’s wrist, looking, and came back.

A break, old, set wrong, a quarter-inch out of true. Which three Healers had known. And — the map was very clear about this, the way it had been clear about the glaze — a second thing, not in the bone, in the thin tough cord that ran beside it, that had been pulled in the same duel and had never been looked at because the bone had been the thing that showed, and had healed short, and was the thing that actually ached in the cold, and had ached for forty-four years while three Healers looked at a bone.

He told him. Plainly, the way Carrow told a result.

Flitwick sat in the chair with his wrist held out and did not say anything for a long time, and then he drew the wrist back, slowly, and looked at it, and turned it, and pressed with his other thumb at the place a quarter-inch from where the Healers had pressed, and his face went white and then did something else.

“There,” he said. “That’s — Dr Potter, that is exactly — forty-four years and nobody—” He stopped. “You could mend it.”

“I think so. Not today. Not without asking Madam Pomfrey, because it’s a thing in a person and I’ve only ever mended a cat and a squib and a pair of hands, and there is a hospital wing in this building with a Healer in it and I would like her to be in the room when I do, because she knows what a wrist is and I know what a sentence is and I think those are the two halves.” He put the holly down. “And because that’s the whole of what the asking is for, sir. Not me doing it. Her doing it. A Healer with this doesn’t need page ninety-one. She asks the body what’s wrong and the body tells her, and she checks, and if the two of us can get it into her hands I don’t care whose name is on it.”

Flitwick looked at him for a long moment, holding his own wrist.

“Poppy,” he said, “will not believe a word of it.”

“No, sir. Neither did Carrow. Neither did Aldous. Neither did Snape about the ring on the pewter. That’s what checking is for.” Harry stood up. “Will you introduce me? Properly. Not as — not as the thing in the index. As someone who has a question for her and would like her to ask it back.”

“Wednesday,” said Filius Flitwick, and flexed his wrist, and winced, and did not stop looking at it. “I shall take you myself. And Dr Potter — I should like it on record, in whatever you keep your records in, that I have been a Charms master for forty-one years and I have just watched a first-year find a thing in my own arm that three Healers missed, with a sentence he wrote in a cupboard at nine, and that I did not once think to ask whether the question was well-formed.”

“It’s on record, sir,” said Harry. “I count everything.”

Madam Pomfrey did not believe a word of it.

She said so, in the hospital wing, on the Wednesday evening, with the long room empty but for one Hufflepuff second-year in the end bed with a wrist in a sling, and Flitwick standing very small beside Harry with his face set, and Hermione a step behind with the forty-seventh pad open and a quill, because Harry had asked her to come and write down whatever he said in the marks as he said it, so that there would be a record that was not in his head.

“A charm that diagnoses,” said Madam Pomfrey. She was a square woman with a square face and hands that had set ten thousand bones and did not believe in anything that had not set one. “Filius. I have been a Healer for thirty-one years. There is no such charm. There are two hundred and six charms in the Healer’s Compendium and every one of them does something, and the doing is how you know what was wrong, and if a first-year had written a two hundred and seventh at nine in a cupboard I should expect to have heard about it from St Mungo’s and not from the Charms master in my own ward at eight o’clock on a Wednesday.” She looked at Harry. Not at the scar; he noted that, and liked her for it. At him. “Dr Potter. I have read about you in the Prophet. I have been told about you by Minerva, who does not exaggerate, and by Filius, who does. I should like you to understand that in this room I do not care what you are. I care what is wrong with the child in that bed, and I know what is wrong with him, because I set it, and I will not have him experimented on.”

“No, Madam Pomfrey,” said Harry. “Nor would I. May I tell you what I’m asking for, and then you can say no?”

She folded her arms. He had been folded-arms at by Carrow and Aldous and a goblin and he knew what it meant, which was go on, then.

“I’m not asking to do anything to him. I’m asking to ask him a question, from here, without touching him, that he won’t feel and that won’t change anything. The question comes back to me as a — as a map. Of where the wrongness is. If the map says what you already know, then I’ve wasted four seconds of your evening and you can send me to bed. If the map says something you don’t know, then you can check it, with your hands, which have set ten thousand bones, and if I’m wrong you’ll know in a minute and if I’m right you’ll know in a minute, and either way you’ve lost a minute.” He paused. “That’s the whole of it. A question and a check. It’s what Professor Flitwick let me do to his wrist on Saturday, and I found a thing three Healers missed, and he has been pressing the place with his thumb for four days and not believing it.”

Pomfrey looked at Flitwick. Flitwick, with great dignity, pressed the place with his thumb and winced.

“Forty-four years,” he said. “Poppy. Forty-four years and it was the cord.”

Pomfrey looked at his wrist for a long moment with a professional’s face. Then she looked at the Hufflepuff in the end bed, who had been following all of this with his mouth open.

“Mr Cadwallader,” she said. “Do you consent to being asked a question you will not feel by a first-year with a doctorate?”

“Er,” said Cadwallader. “Yes? Yes, Madam Pomfrey.”

“Four seconds,” said Pomfrey, to Harry. “And then you tell me what you have, in plain English, and I check it, and if you have made that boy’s wrist one iota worse I shall have you out of this ward and out of this school and I do not care whose name is in whose index.”

“Yes, Madam Pomfrey. Hermione — write it as I say it.”

He held the asking. He sent it, across twenty feet of ward, into a second-year’s wrist in a sling, and it went looking, and came back.

“A clean break,” he said, “across the small bone on the outside, two inches above the hand. Set well. Knitting. About a third of the way there.” Pomfrey’s face did not move; that was what she knew. “And a hairline. In the other bone, the big one, on the inside, an inch higher. Very fine. Not displaced. It didn’t show when the other broke because the other was the thing that showed. It’s also knitting, on its own, because it’s small, but it’s knitting a hair out of true and in about three weeks when the sling comes off and he leans on it it’ll go, and you’ll set it then and wonder why a boy who was mending broke again.” He let the asking go. “That’s what the map says. I don’t know whether it’s right. Check it.”

Pomfrey did not move for a moment.

Then she crossed the ward, and took the sling off Cadwallader’s arm with hands that had done it ten thousand times, and did not look at the small bone at all. She put two fingers on the big one, an inch above the break, on the inside, where the map had said, and pressed, gently, and Cadwallader said “Ow,” in a surprised voice, and Pomfrey’s face did a thing that Harry had seen on exactly one Healer’s face in his life, and the Healer had been in a letter from a squib about a man called Amos.

She took out her wand and said a word and the wrist glowed faintly along its length for a second and she looked at the glow where her fingers had been, and was still.

“Filius,” she said, without turning round. “Come here.”

Flitwick went. He looked at the glow. He said nothing.

“Hairline,” said Madam Pomfrey, very quietly, to the wrist. “Radius. An inch proximal. Non-displaced. I did not see it. I set the ulna and I did not look at the radius because the ulna was the thing that showed.” She put the sling back on, carefully, the way Harry held things on stages. “Mr Cadwallader, you will keep that on for five weeks and not three, and you will not lean on it, and you will tell no one why, and if you do I shall know.” She turned round.

Harry had never once in his life been looked at by a Healer who had just found a thing she had missed, and he found that it was a face he would have crossed a very long way to see, and that he had, in a sense, since a bus stop.

“What is it?” said Madam Pomfrey. “Dr Potter. In plain English. What is that.”

“A question,” said Harry. “That the body answers. That’s all. There’s a part of mending that goes looking before it mends, and nobody can see the part because it happens in the quarter-second before the charm, and I can see it, and I wrote it down on its own with the mending left off. It doesn’t do anything. It asks. The body says. You check.” He hesitated, and then said the rest, because she had crossed a ward on his word and had found a thing and had said I did not see it in front of a second-year. “Madam Pomfrey. The Healer’s Compendium. The fourth chapter. The dosage table on page ninety-one.”

She went very still.

“The columns are transposed. A printer did it sixty years ago and nobody checked because the table looks right and tables that look right are where—” He stopped. “I found it when I was eight, in a cupboard, and put a margin on it that said surely not, and didn’t know it had killed anyone. The Headmaster told me it had killed two. I asked for their names. He sent them. I have them in the back of a pad because I thought somebody should.” He took the pad from Hermione, and turned to the back, and held it out. “I don’t know whether you knew either of them. I thought you might. I thought — if you were going to use the asking, you should know what the asking is for, and it isn’t cups.”

Poppy Pomfrey took the pad. She read the two names. She read them for a long time, for two names, and when she looked up her square face had done a thing that square faces are not built for.

“The second one,” she said. “She was a student here. 1962. I was a trainee. I was not the one who—” She stopped. “I knew her. I did not know it was the table. We were told it was the dose. We were told the Healer had—” She closed the pad. “Sixty years. The columns. And a boy of eight in a cupboard wrote surely not.”

“Yes.”

“And nobody at St Mungo’s—”

“I don’t know, Madam Pomfrey. I’ve never been to St Mungo’s. I’d like to. It’s on a list.” He held out his hand for the pad, and she gave it back, and he put it under his arm. “I’d like to teach you the asking. Not me casting it; you. I don’t know whether it can be taught; I’ve been teaching a Charms master and a girl who catches to read it for a month and they can, and nobody has yet cast a thing of mine but me. But if anyone alive could, it would be a person who has set ten thousand bones and knows what a wrist is for, because the asking is only a question and the person asking has to know what to ask. I don’t. I know sentences. You know wrists. I think those are the two halves. I’ve been saying that about everything since I was eight and I’ve never once been wrong.”

Pomfrey looked at him for a long time across her own ward with the second-year in the end bed watching with his mouth open and a very small man beside her pressing his wrist with his thumb.

“Wednesdays,” she said at last. “Eight o’clock. This ward. You will bring the girl, because somebody has to write it down who is not you, and you will bring Filius, because somebody has to sit in a chair and tell me when I am being a fool, and you will cast nothing on any patient in this room without my hand on the bed and my word first, and when I say stop you will stop in the middle of a mark.” She unfolded her arms. “And Dr Potter. The names. I should like a copy. I have a drawer.”

“You’ll have them tonight,” said Harry. “I’ll write them out myself. In the round hand. For the drawer.”

He wrote them out that night, by the window, in the blue pen E.P. had given him, two names on a sheet of good paper, for a drawer in a hospital wing, and under them, because the reader in fifty years would want to know, he wrote the date and Madam Pomfrey knew the second one; she was a trainee; they were told it was the dose, and folded it, and put it in an envelope, and gave it to Hedwig, who took it across a castle at midnight to a ward with a square woman in it who did not sleep either.

Then he wrote the day in the forty-seventh pad, for the reader, and for Hermione, who would want to see what he had made of it.

The asking works. On a cup, which I could check, and found a thing under the glaze that nobody could have known and that cracked at eleven seconds where the map said. On an old man’s wrist, and found the cord three Healers missed in forty-four years. On a second-year’s arm across twenty feet of ward, and found a hairline a Healer had not looked for because the other bone was the one that showed, and she checked it with two fingers and a word and said I did not see it in front of the boy, which is the bravest thing I have seen anyone do in this castle, and I have seen a man admit a trap.

She knew the second name. 1962. She was a trainee. They were told it was the dose.

Wednesdays. Eight. The ward. She said bring the girl because somebody has to write it down who is not me, and I have been the only one writing it down for five years, and I did not know how much I wanted someone else to be the one until a Healer said so in a voice that was not kind and was better than kind.

The disc hums. The asking answers. The table has a Healer at it now, which is the chair I wanted most and thought would take longest. Four weeks. I gave him a term.

Item four on the List was Pain. Mrs F's hands, forty years. Flitwick's wrist, forty-four. A boy in the end bed who would have broken again in three weeks and wondered why. I did not mend any of them tonight. I asked, and somebody who knows what a wrist is for is going to do the mending, with a question in her hand that she can check, and that is better than me doing it, and I have known since a kitchen floor that it would be and I have never once until tonight seen what it looks like.

It looks like a square woman with two fingers on a boy's arm saying ow in a surprised voice. I would not trade it for the fourth floor.

He closed the pad. The mountains were out there being mountains. The castle sang. The bronze disc in the biscuit tin under the bed hummed its small hum, holding a sentence a man had written on a doorstep and a boy had cut into metal with a dead duellist’s graver, and in a ward across the castle a Healer of thirty-one years sat with a drawer open and two names in a round hand and did not sleep, and in a dormitory in a tower a girl who had written forty lines of a language with one speaker into a pad in a clinical voice lay awake deciding whether she was annoyed about being brought to a ward to be a scribe or whether it was the most important thing she had ever been asked to do, and decided, somewhere towards one, that it was both, and that she would tell him so, every time.

He did not sleep. He had not expected to. He had, for once, something better than the next thing to think about, which was the last one, and he let himself.
