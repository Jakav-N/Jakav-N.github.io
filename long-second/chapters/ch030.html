Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 30

Brooms

The broom was a sentence in a stick, and it was listening, and he stood over it on the grass with twenty-five Ravenclaws and Hufflepuffs on a grey Thursday afternoon and did not say up, because he had just understood what the stick was for and wanted to sit with it for a second before he asked it anything.

Madam Hooch had hawk’s eyes and a hawk’s way of standing and had told them to put out their right hands and say up, and twenty-four children had said up in the voices of children shouting at a dog, and some of the brooms had come and some had rolled over and one — Terry Boot’s — had hit him in the shin. Harry had put out his hand and had not said anything, because the moment his hand went over the broom he had felt it, the way he had felt the hat from twenty feet: a sentence, old, carried in the wood and the twigs, holding itself, cast once by somebody long ago and casting itself ever since.

And the sentence had a listening part.

He had thought, since he was seven, that the listening part was the thing he had that nobody else did — that they told and he asked — and he had found a listening part in the roof, and in the doorstep, and had put both down to the man who wrote them and the woman who had asked once with everything she had. He had not expected to find one in a school broom with half its twigs gone. But there it was. The broom was not waiting for up. The broom was asking, over and over, in a small patient sentence that had been running since before his grandparents were born, where do you want to go? — and up was just the word most people shouted at it because nobody had ever told them it was a question.

“Potter,” said Hooch. “You’re not saying it.”

“No, Madam Hooch. Sorry. I was — I was reading it.” He looked down at the broom. “It’s asking. Isn’t it. It wants to know where. The word’s just — the word’s how people answer without knowing they’ve been asked.”

Hooch looked at him with the hawk’s eyes for a long moment, and he had the sense — he had been having it a great deal, in a month — of a professional of long standing being told something about her own instrument that she had known in her hands for thirty years and had never once heard said.

“Answer it, then,” she said.

He did not say up. He thought here, the way he would have thought it at a cat, and the broom came into his hand like a thing that had been waiting to be asked properly, and sat there, and hummed.

He was good at it. He had not expected to be, and was, and the reason was the reason for everything else.

A broom went where you wanted. That was the whole of a broom; Ron had explained it on a train for forty minutes and Quidditch Through the Ages had explained it for two hundred pages and neither had said the thing that mattered, which was that wanting was a sentence, and most people sent it ragged. They wanted up and left and faster in the shouting way, the eyes-shut way, and the broom — which was listening, which had been built to listen — got a ragged answer and did its best, and the best was a wobble, and the wobble was what people called learning to fly.

He did not wobble. He sat on a school broom twenty feet over a lawn in Scotland and held where the way he held a feather, steady, every mark of it, and the broom did exactly and only what he had asked, and he found — the first time he banked, with the castle going round him and the lake coming up gold on the left — that it was the first thing he had ever done with his body that his body was not the slow part of.

Because the broom was fast. The broom was his speed, nearly; it answered in the quarter-second, and in the quarter-second he had three minutes to decide, and so for the first time in his life the thing he was riding and the thing he was thinking with were going at something like the same rate, and the lawn went under him and the towers went past and he was not waiting for anything, and he laughed out loud over the Hufflepuffs with the wind in his face, which he had not done since a cupboard and a pencil, and Hooch, on the ground, did not tell him to come down.

“Potter,” she said, when he did. “Who taught you?”

“Nobody, Madam Hooch. It’s the first time. It’s just — it asks, and I can answer steady. That’s all. Everyone else is shouting.”

“Everyone else,” said Hooch, “has been shouting at brooms for a thousand years, and I have taught flying for twenty-six of them, and I have never once seen a first-year bank without a wobble.” She looked at him with the hawk’s eyes. “You’ll want the team. Flitwick’s lot are short a Seeker.”

He had known that was coming since the lake came up gold. He had thought about it on the way down — most of a quarter-hour — and had decided, and did not like the decision, and was going to keep it.

“No, Madam Hooch,” he said. “Thank you. I’d like to fly. I’d like to fly a great deal. I don’t want to play.”

“Why not.”

He looked at the broom in his hand, humming, asking.

“Because I can see the Snitch,” he said.

He proved it on the Sunday, because Ron would not believe him and because Hooch had told Flitwick and Flitwick had told the captain and the captain was a fourth-year called Davies who had come to find him in the sloping room with the face of a boy who had been handed a thing and could not see what it was.

“Just try,” said Davies. “One go. Hooch says you bank like a seventh-year. We lost to Hufflepuff last year by a hundred and ten because our Seeker couldn’t find the thing in a fog. One go, Potter. Humour me.”

So he went to the pitch on the Sunday morning, with Davies and Ron and Hermione — who had come, she said, to count — and Davies let the Snitch out of its box, and it went up into the grey like a thought, and Harry sat on a school broom at the centre line and did not move.

He read it.

The Snitch was a sentence too. Of course it was; everything in this world that moved by itself was a sentence somebody had put in a thing, and this one was small and gold and fast, and its sentence said, as near as he could put it, do not be caught. But do not be caught was not a plan. It was a rule. And a rule, he had learned at two from a woman at a cooker, ran the same way every time, because rules did not know when they were wrong. The Snitch ran a pattern. A good one, a thousand years sanded, with nine or ten turns in it that looked random to anyone watching at one-to-one and were not random at all to anyone who could stand still inside a quarter-second and watch the sentence choose.

He watched it choose eleven times. It took four seconds. Then he knew where it would be in two more, and went there, and it was, and he closed his hand on it, and sat on the broom at the far end of the pitch with a gold thing beating in his fist like a heart, and looked back down the length of the grass at three people who had not seen him move.

“Again,” said Davies, in a changed voice.

He let it go. It went up. He read it — nine turns this time; the pattern had a second pattern under it that came out when it had been caught once, which he thought was rather clever of whoever had built it — and went where it would be, and it was, and he had it, and the whole thing had taken six seconds.

“Again.”

“Davies,” said Harry. “It won’t be different. I can do it as many times as you like and it will take about five seconds each time and I will not once miss, because I am not catching it. I’m reading it. It has a pattern and I can see the pattern choose and I go where it’s going to be. That isn’t Seeking. That’s—” he looked for the word and found it in a geography room with a man who smelled of pipe “—that’s playing chess against someone who can’t see the board. I did that for four years every Wednesday to a man I liked and I threw the last game so he’d have one to tell the club about. I’m not going to do it to Hufflepuff.”

Davies sat on his broom with his mouth open.

“You’d win,” he said. “Every match. For seven years.”

“Yes. And nobody in the stands would have a game to watch, and the Hufflepuff Seeker would spend seven years finding out what it feels like to be hunted by someone who isn’t looking, and I know what that feels like, and it’s cold, and I don’t do it to people.” He let the Snitch go. It went up into the grey and hung there, and he watched it hang, and did not read it. “Ron. Tell him.”

Ron, who had been sitting on his broom beside Davies with the expression of a boy who has just seen his religion done something to, said slowly, “He’s right. It’d be — it wouldn’t be Quidditch. It’d be Harry, with a broom, and everyone else sort of — there.” He looked at Harry. “Mate. You could be the best Seeker in the history—”

“I know. That’s why I shouldn’t.” He turned the broom. “I’ll fly with you. Sundays. Here, if Hooch lets us. I’ll fly as hard as you like and I won’t read anything and you can try to knock me off, and when Ravenclaw play Hufflepuff I’ll be in the stands shouting for a Seeker who can’t see the board, because that’s what the game is, and I’d like to find out what it feels like to watch one.” He looked at Davies. “Find someone who wobbles. Then teach them not to. That’s a Seeker. I’m a — I don’t know what I am. I’m a boy who can read a Snitch and has decided not to.”

Davies looked at him a long moment. Then he looked at the Snitch, hanging in the grey, unread.

“Potter,” he said, “that is the most Ravenclaw thing anyone has ever said to me, and I am a Ravenclaw, and I hate it, and you’re right.” He went to get the box. “Sundays. I’ll square it with Hooch. And if you ever change your mind—”

“I won’t. But I’ll tell you first if I do. I tell people first. It’s a rule.”

Hermione, on the grass, had been counting. She came over when Davies had gone and looked up at him on the broom and said, “Five seconds and six. You could have done it in three. You waited.”

“I wanted him to see it twice. Once could have been luck. I don’t do luck; I did it once at four about a star crossing a gap and I’ve never trusted it since.” He got off the broom. “Was that right? Saying no?”

She thought about it honestly, which she had learned from somebody.

“Yes,” she said. “And you hated it. I counted that too.”

He flew with Ron on the Sunday afternoon, and did not read anything, and it was the best two hours he had spent in the open air since a bench in the Alley with an old woman holding his hand.

Ron flew the way Ron did everything, which was all at once and with his whole heart and no plan, and he was good, in the way that Dudley had been good at football — not clever, not careful, but there, in the thing, every second of it, shouting at a broom that had been built to be asked and getting, somehow, by sheer wanting, most of what he shouted for. Harry flew beside him and did not hold anything steady and let the broom wobble under him for the first time in his life and found out what learning to fly was, which was a thing he had never once had the chance to find out about anything, because he had always been able to see the answer before he had the question.

“You’re wobbling,” Ron shouted, over the wind, delighted.

“I know! I’m doing it on purpose! I wanted to know what it was like!”

“What what’s like?”

“Being bad at a thing because I don’t know how to do it! I’ve never — Ron, I’ve never once in my life not known how to do a thing I was doing! This is—” he wobbled, hard, and corrected it the shouting way, the way everyone did, and the broom did its best, and the best was a lurch that put the lake briefly where the sky should have been “—this is brilliant!”

Ron laughed so hard he nearly came off. They flew until Hooch came out and shouted at them, and then a bit after, and when they came down Ron said, with the face of a boy who had worked something out and was not sure he liked it, “You could read it, though. The whole time. You could have stopped wobbling any second.”

“Yes.”

“But you didn’t.”

“No. I wanted to be in the game. Not watching it. I’ve watched everything my whole life, Ron. I wanted one afternoon of not knowing where the Snitch was going to be.” He looked at the broom. “It was the best afternoon I’ve had here. Don’t tell Hermione; she counts.”

“She’ll know anyway,” said Ron. “She catches. You said.”

“I did say,” said Harry, and found he was grinning, and did not stop for some time.

Draco Malfoy found him on the Monday, in the entrance hall, between Charms and lunch, with two large boys in the attitudes of furniture and the face from a stool in a shop that had been practising for a month.

“Potter. I hear you turned down the Ravenclaw team.” He said it to the furniture, not to Harry. “Davies was telling anyone who’d listen. Reads the Snitch, apparently. Catches it in five seconds and won’t play.” He looked at Harry at last. “Afraid of losing to a Slytherin, are we? Or is it that a doctor doesn’t stoop to—”

“I ate a biscuit on a train,” said Harry, “and I didn’t see who took the one on the seat by the door. It went at Peterborough. It’s the first thing in a year I didn’t see, and I’ve been thinking about it since, and I’d like to ask you what you thought when you ate it, because I think you did, and I think nobody has ever asked you what you thought about anything.”

The entrance hall was not quiet; it was lunchtime. But the six feet of it round the two of them went quiet, and the furniture shifted, and Draco Malfoy stood with a line ready and the line did not go anywhere, because nobody in the six feet had done anything he had been told to expect.

“I didn’t—” he said.

“It’s all right. You don’t have to say. I didn’t see, so I don’t know, so you can tell me it wasn’t you and I’ll have to take your word, which is the first time in a year anyone’s had that over me and I think it should be you, because I think you took it, and I think you took it when nobody was looking because you didn’t want to be seen taking a thing from someone your father has thought about what to do with.” He kept his voice level, because the sentence had been built on a Sunday afternoon with a broom wobbling under him and he wanted it to carry. “I turned down the team because I can see the Snitch and it wouldn’t be a game. Not because of you. I don’t do things because of people, Malfoy. I do them because of what they are. I’d have turned it down if Ravenclaw were playing nobody.” He paused. “And I’d still like to know what you thought about the biscuit. The offer’s open. It was open on the train. It’s open now.”

Draco looked at him for a long moment with his father’s face on and something under it that was not his father’s at all, and Harry watched the two of them fight it out behind the pale eyes the way two Vernons had fought behind a purple one, and this time it was not no contest, and this time he could not tell who had won.

“It was dry,” said Draco Malfoy, at last, to the floor, very fast, as if the words had got out before he could stop them. “The biscuit. It was a bit dry.”

“They were from a tin from Edinburgh. They’d been in it since July. My — a woman who looked after me packed them.” Harry did not smile. He wanted to and did not, because a boy who had just said a true thing to the floor did not want to be smiled at. “Next time I’ll give you one from the new tin. It’s got the Humber Bridge on it. They’re better.”

“I don’t want your—”

“I know. The offer’s open anyway. Everyone’s is.” He shouldered his bag. “Lunch. If you want to know what I thought about the Snitch, I’m in the sloping room on the fourth floor on Thursdays. Nobody’s going to ask you what a bezoar is. Neville already knows.”

He went to lunch. He did not look back; he had learned at the bottom of some steps in Egham what it did to a person to be looked back at while they were deciding something. But Hermione, who had been standing in the doorway of the Great Hall for the whole of it with her chin up, said, when he reached her, very quietly:

“It was dry.”

“Yes.”

“That’s the first true thing he’s said in this building.”

“I know. It’s a start. I counted.”

The broom came apart on the Saturday, in the sloping room, on a bench, with Flitwick in the chair and the Workshop round it, and it was the first thing Harry had ever taken apart that had been built by somebody who could listen.

It was a school broom, the oldest Hooch had, with half its twigs gone and a crack in the handle that she had been meaning to have mended since 1979, and she had given it to Flitwick with the hawk’s eyes narrowed and said if the boy can tell me why it pulls left I shall want to know. Harry had not taken it apart with his hands. He had sat in front of it with the holly and sent the asking into it, the way he had into a cup, and the map had come back, and the map had been — he sat very still with it for a long time — the map had been beautiful.

“It’s got three,” he said. “Three sentences. Not one. I thought a broom was one thing that listened and it isn’t. There’s one that holds it up — that’s the old one, that’s the one that’s been running since before anybody in this room was born, it’s enormous, it’s like the wall in the yard — and there’s one that steers, that’s the listening one, the one that asks where you want to go, and that one’s newer and somebody good wrote it, and there’s a third one—” He stopped. “There’s a third one that cushions. That makes it not hurt to sit on. And that one’s the newest and it’s bad. It’s written by somebody who was shouting. It’s ragged. And it’s rubbing against the steering one, the way two people hold a face, and that’s why it pulls left. The cushioning charm’s pulling the steering charm’s sleeve.”

Flitwick had got out of his chair.

“Three,” he said. “Three separate—”

“Yes, sir. Layered. Somebody wrote the lift a thousand years ago and somebody wrote the steering four hundred years ago and somebody wrote the cushioning about sixty and they never once met and the three sentences are sharing a stick and nobody has ever read all three at once to see whether they get on.” He looked at the broom. “They nearly do. The old two are fine; they were written by people who could hold. The third one wasn’t. I could — sir, I could rewrite the third one. Clean. In my marks. Cut it into the handle under the crack where it wouldn’t show. It would stop pulling left and it would be the first broom in the world with a cushioning charm that didn’t argue with the steering.”

“And then,” said Hermione, from the bench, with the pad open, in the voice of a girl who had been following him for a month and had just got somewhere first, “you could do it to all of them.”

The room went quiet.

“A press,” said Harry slowly. “A press for brooms. Not blankets. A seal with a clean cushioning sentence in it that you press into the handle and the broom stops pulling left, every broom, every time, and nobody has to shout at it.” He looked at Hermione. “That’s — that’s not the blankets. That’s not a hospital. That’s a shop. That’s a thing you could sell to Davies’s father.”

“Yes,” said Hermione. “And the money would go to the ward. And the Wizengamot wouldn’t have to amend anything, because it’s a broom, and brooms are allowed.” Her chin was up. “You said the table needed more chairs. I’m saying some of the chairs have to be paid for. I grew up with two dentists. I know what a ward costs.”

Harry sat on the bench in a room that sloped and looked at a girl who had grown up with two dentists and had just, in one sentence, turned a press for blankets into a press for everything, and understood that he had been right on the platform and right on the landing and right in the entrance hall, and that the thing of which all of them had been the small dose was this: somebody else getting there first, in the room, out loud, where he could count it.

“Hermione Granger,” he said, “that is the most useful thing anyone has said in this room since Neville said seeds, and I am going to write it down with your name on it in a pad that a person is going to read in fifty years, and the person is going to know who thought of selling the press.”

“I didn’t think of selling it. I thought of paying for the ward.”

“I know. That’s why it goes in with your name.” He turned to Flitwick, who was standing very small in the middle of the room with his hand on a broom that had three sentences in it and looking at it the way he had looked at page one of a biscuit tin. “Sir. Can I have the broom?”

“Dr Potter,” said Filius Flitwick, “you may have every broom in this castle, and I shall tell Rolanda why, and she will not believe me, and then she will fly one, and then she will.” He took his hand off it. “Three sentences. In a stick. Four hundred years and nobody read all three.” He went back to the chair and sat down in it and looked at nothing for a while. “I burned the wrong letter,” he said, eventually, to the room. “I should have burned the Compendium.”

He wrote it in the forty-seventh pad that night, by the window, with the mountains, and he gave it the title it had earned.

Brooms.

A broom is three sentences sharing a stick, written by three people who never met, four hundred years apart, and the newest one was written by somebody shouting and it argues with the steering and that is why Hooch's oldest broom pulls left. I can read all three at once. Nobody has. I am going to rewrite the third one clean and cut it under the crack and give it back to Hooch and not tell her which one I fixed and see if she can tell.

I can see the Snitch. I caught it twice in eleven seconds and said no to the team, because it would not have been a game, and Ron said it'd be Harry with a broom and everyone else sort of there, and Davies said it was the most Ravenclaw thing anyone had said to him and he hated it and I was right. I flew with Ron on Sunday and wobbled on purpose and did not read anything for two hours and it was the best afternoon I have had in the open air since a bench, and I did not know until I was doing it that I had never once in my life been bad at a thing I did not know how to do.

Draco Malfoy said the biscuit was dry. To the floor. Very fast. It is the first true thing he has said in this building and Hermione counted it before I did.

Hermione said: do it to all of them. And then: the money goes to the ward. And then: brooms are allowed. Three sentences, in a room, out loud, in the time it took me to see one, and the one I saw was the smallest. I have been the only one in the room for ten years and I did not know the room would be faster than me. Not at thinking. At arriving.

Item two on the List is the table. The table has a Healer and a Charms master and a prefect and a boy who said seeds and a boy who came for the biscuits and a girl who said do it to all of them. And a pale boy who said dry to the floor, nearly. Not yet.

Hooch has twenty-six brooms. Davies's father has a shop. A ward in London has a line in a budget. I had a term. I have nine weeks of it left and a press that does not yet press and a disc that hums and a girl who got there first.

Faster. But not alone. Those are not the same thing any more. I did not know that until Sunday, wobbling.

He closed the pad. The broom was in the corner of the dormitory where he had put it, humming its three hums, two old and clean and one ragged and sixty years old, and he looked at it a while, and at the mountains, and thought about a Snitch hanging in the grey, unread, because he had decided to let it, and found that of all the things he had done in six weeks in a castle, that was the one he was proudest of, and that he could not have explained why to anyone, in any of seven languages, except perhaps to a man who smelled of pipe and had once watched a knight thrown with love.

He did not sleep. He had not expected to. He wrote a letter to Mr Pole instead, about a Snitch, and gave it to Hedwig, and she went out over the lake into the dark, south, with a post from everywhere, and he watched her go until he could not, which was a long time, and did not mind.
