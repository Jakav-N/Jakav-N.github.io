Harry Potter and the Long Second
Chapter 32

Hallowe’en

He had decided not to think about Godric’s Hollow at a feast, and he failed at it before the pumpkins had finished coming in, and he had known he would, and he had decided to let himself, and he did.

Ten years. He sat at the blue table under a ceiling that was the sky with a thousand live bats going round in it and a thousand pumpkins floating with candles in them, and he had read the paragraph four times on a bench at seven and knew the date the way he knew the hall clock, and he found — the way he had found on the step with the milk going cold — that knowing a thing and sitting in a room full of people celebrating the other half of it were two different things, and that the second was colder than he had budgeted.

Not the people. He did not mind the people. They were not celebrating his parents; they were celebrating pumpkins, and a thousand years of pumpkins, and most of them did not know what day it was for one boy at the blue table, and the ones who did were kind enough not to look. It was the shape of it. Ten years ago tonight a woman had asked, once, with everything she had, and the world had said yes, and the yes was sitting at a table eating treacle tart, and the price of the yes was in a churchyard in the West Country with a white stone that a woman in tartan had promised to take him to one day, and he had never been, and the bats went round.

He did not say anything. He ate the treacle tart, because it was good and because his aunt had taught him that you ate what was put in front of you, and he looked at the staff table with a quarter of his mind out of habit — Quirrell at the end by Sprout, where the candles did not reach, with the first face pale and the second one busy, which was new, and which he noted — and with the other three-quarters he sat with the shape, and let it be the shape it was.

A biscuit appeared by his plate.

He had not seen it arrive, which was the second thing in a year he had not seen. It was from the Humber Bridge tin, one of the good ones from the left, and Hermione, beside him, was looking straight ahead at the Hufflepuff table with her chin up and her hands in her lap and an expression of complete and total innocence that she had plainly been practising, and did not look at him, and did not say anything, and he understood that she had counted the date too.

He ate the biscuit. It was not dry.

“Thank you,” he said, to the Hufflepuff table.

“I don’t know what you mean,” said Hermione, to the same table, and that was the whole of it, and it was a great deal, and he put it on the shelf next to a cap on a station concourse and found that the shelf had got, in two months, very full indeed of things that people had done for him quietly and would have denied.

Then the doors of the Great Hall banged open, and Quirinus Quirrell came through them at a run with his turban askew and his face the colour of the tablecloths, and the second face was not busy any more. The second face was gone. He had the first one all to himself, for once, and the first one was terrified, and that — Harry sat very still — that was not what Harry had expected, and he was reading it before the man had reached the staff table.

“Troll — in the dungeons — thought you ought to know—”

And Quirrell fell down.

He read the faint in the time it took the man to hit the flagstones, which was most of a minute, and it was false, and it was false in a way that was interesting.

Not the first face. The first face had been terrified; the stutter had been real; the man had seen a troll and had run and was afraid, and the fear had every mark in it that fear had. But a frightened man did not faint. A frightened man stood there shaking, or sat down, or was sick. Fainting was a thing the body did when it had decided to stop, and the first face had not decided anything; it had been running on fright, and fright did not stop. Something else had stopped it. Something had reached in, from under, and said down, and the first face had gone down like a puppet with the string cut, and lay on the flagstones with the eyes shut and the turban askew, and the second face was not in it, because the second face did not need to be. The second face had done what it came to do, which was to empty the Hall.

The Hall emptied. Screaming, mostly, and then the Headmaster’s voice over it like a door shutting on a wind — prefects, lead your houses to the dormitories — and the four tables going up like flocks off a field, and Penelope Clearwater at the Ravenclaw end with her prefect’s badge and her face set saying first-years, with me, now, stay together, and Harry stood up with the rest and found that he was counting.

He always counted. He had counted his uncle’s shouting and the thing in Quirrell’s face and a hall full of first-years at a Sorting, and he counted the Ravenclaw first-years now as they went past Penelope towards the door, out of habit, with a quarter of his mind, and he got to twenty-five and stopped.

There were twenty-six.

He counted again. Twenty-five. He went back along the shelf to the Hall ten minutes ago, before the pumpkins, and found the twenty-sixth: a small girl called Lisa Turpin with a bag she had been fussing with all through the feast, and he found her at the moment the doors banged, which was the moment he had been looking at Quirrell and not at the table, and she had been bending to the floor for the bag, and when the Hall went up like a flock she had not gone with it. She had gone back. Under the table, for the bag, and then — he could not see; he had not been looking; it was the third thing in a year — out, somewhere, in the wrong direction, in a crowd of four hundred people going the right one.

“Penelope. There are twenty-five.”

“What?”

“First-years. There should be twenty-six. Lisa Turpin went back for her bag when the doors opened and she isn’t with us. I think she went out the side door by the staff table because it was nearer and there was nobody at it.” He kept his voice level. “The side door goes to the first-floor corridor. The one with the girls’ lavatory.”

Penelope Clearwater stood at the door of the Great Hall with twenty-five first-years behind her and a fifth-year’s badge on her chest and a face that had been told by a boy of eleven exactly where a child was and what to do about it, and did the arithmetic that prefects do, which was twenty-five children going up a staircase against one child going the wrong way, and he watched her arrive at the answer and hate it.

“I can’t leave them,” she said. “Potter, I can’t—”

“I know. Take them up. I’ll get her.” He had decided before he had finished the count; the deciding had taken a quarter-second, which was most of a minute, and the minute had been spent on a thing he had promised a man in a round room with a hundred eyes and had kept. “The troll’s in the dungeons. He said so. The first floor is the other way from the dungeons. I’m going to walk to the first-floor corridor and find a girl with a bag and bring her up the back stairs and I’ll be in the tower before you’ve done the register. That isn’t the third floor. I gave my word about the third floor and I’m not breaking it. I’m going to the first.”

“Harry—”

“Go, Penelope. Count them again at the top.”

She went. She did not want to and she went, because she was a prefect and the sum came out the way it came out, and she took twenty-five first-years up the marble staircase at a pace that had them running, and Harry turned, in a hall that was emptying round him like water out of a bath, and went the other way, towards the side door by the staff table, past a man lying on the flagstones with his eyes shut and his turban askew and his face doing nothing at all, which Harry noted, and filed, and did not stop for.

Hermione was beside him before he reached the door.

“I told you to—”

“You didn’t tell me anything. You told Penelope. I’m not in Penelope’s count; I was already past her.” Her chin was up and her wand was out and her face was white and perfectly determined. “Lisa sits next to me in Herbology. She left her bag on the train too. I know what she looks like when she’s gone back for something. I’m coming.”

He looked at her for a quarter-second, which was long enough to decide that he would spend the rest of his life losing this argument, and that he did not mind.

“Then stay behind me,” he said. “Not because you’re — because I can hear things you can’t and I need you where I know you are. Yes?”

“Yes.”

They went through the side door.

The first-floor corridor was long and stone and lit by torches, and empty, and quiet, and he stopped three steps into it and listened, and in the quiet he heard the troll.

Not in the dungeons.

He had known a thing was wrong since the faint, and he knew now what, and it went through him like the morning of the ice. The troll was not in the dungeons. A troll was a large slow heavy thing that breathed like a bellows and dragged a club and did not, as far as any book he had read on either side of the curtain had ever suggested, know where the dungeons were or care, and the sound of one — he had never heard one and knew the sound at once, the way he had known the hand on the back of his head — was coming from the left. Along this corridor. Towards the girls’ lavatory. And under the bellows-sound, small and sharp and wrong, was a thing dragging on stone, and under that, so faint that nobody at one-to-one could have heard it over their own heart, was a girl.

Crying. In the lavatory. With a bag.

“It’s not in the dungeons,” he said, very quietly. “It’s here. Along there. It’s between us and her and it hasn’t found her yet and it’s going to, because she’s making a noise and it’s following noise.” He did not turn round. “Hermione. I am going to ask you to do a thing and I need you to do exactly it and not the thing after.”

“Yes.”

“When I say, go back to the side door and open it and shout. For a teacher. Any teacher. Don’t come after me. The shout is the thing. Yes?”

“Yes. Harry, what are you—”

“I’m going to go and stand where it can see me instead of her.”

He went.

He did not run. He had thought about running on the stairs and rejected it, because a thing that followed noise would follow a running boy and a running boy was a boy who could not hold a sentence. He walked, down the middle of the first-floor corridor at Hogwarts on Hallowe’en night with the torches going past him one by one, and he held the holly in his right hand with the tip down, and he built, as he walked, the thing he was going to say to the troll, because a troll was a large slow heavy thing and he had, by his own clock, all the time in the world to compose for it.

It came round the corner at the end of the corridor as he reached the halfway point, and it was twelve feet tall and grey and smelled like a thing that had died in a drain, and it had a club the length of a man, and it was slow.

That was the thing. That was the whole of the thing, and he had known it would be and had not known how much. He had lived his life at two hundred to one among people who moved like glaciers, and this was slower than people. This was a glacier’s idea of a glacier. It came round the corner and saw him — he watched it see him; it took the troll most of a second and him most of three minutes to watch — and its small eyes did the slow thing that small eyes do when they have found a noise, and the club came up, and it came at him, and the coming took so long that he had time to be afraid, and to notice being afraid, and to put the fear where it went, and to think, quite clearly, this is the first thing I have ever met that is slower than the Dursleys, and nearly to laugh.

“Hermione. Now.”

Behind him, a door banged, and a voice he had never heard her use went up the stairwell like a thing on fire: “PROFESSOR — ANYONE — FIRST FLOOR — TROLL — HELP—”

The troll heard it. Its head came round. The club paused.

And Harry Potter, standing in the middle of a corridor with a twelve-foot troll deciding between two noises, held Wingardium Leviosa in his head the way he would have held a note in singing — not his version; theirs, every mark of it, the one he had held on a feather in a cupboard three years ago and on a feather in a sloping room every Thursday since — and sent it down the holly at a club the length of a man, and held.

The club went up.

Not far. A troll’s club weighed what it weighed, and he was sending down a wand at a tenth of what a grown wizard sent, and he had never once lifted anything heavier than a book. But it went up — a foot; two; out of the troll’s hand, which had not been gripping it because the troll had been deciding — and stopped, two feet above the troll’s head, and held, perfectly still, not wandering, not wobbling, not going into the ceiling the way Waffling said feathers did, because he was holding the sentence steady through a moment nobody else alive could see and so there was nothing in it to wander.

The troll looked up.

That was what he had been counting on. Not that the club would hurt it; he had no intention of hurting it; it was a large slow thing in a corridor following a noise and he did not do that to things. That it would look up. A thing that had just lost its club to the air would look at the air, and a thing looking at the air was not looking at a boy, or at a lavatory door twenty feet behind it, and he had, while it looked, a slow eternity to walk.

He walked. Past the troll, on the right, with the club held steady over its head and his eyes on the club and three-quarters of his mind on the sentence and a quarter on his feet, and the troll turned its head to follow the club and not the boy, because the club was the thing that had moved, and he reached the lavatory door and said, through it, in the flat Surrey vowels, at the speed of his mouth, which had never once been fast enough and was fast enough now:

“Lisa. It’s Harry Potter. Come out now. Don’t look at it. Look at me. Walk to me and keep walking and don’t run.”

The door opened. Lisa Turpin came out, white, with a bag, and looked at him and not at it, because he had said to, and walked, and he put his free hand on her shoulder and turned her, and they walked, together, back down the corridor on the right-hand side, past twelve feet of grey thing with its head tipped back looking at its own club hanging in the torchlight, and he held the club the whole way, and his arm had begun to shake with the cost, the hill of it, and he did not let it slip.

They reached the side door. Hermione was in it, with her wand out and her face white and her mouth still open from the shout, and she took Lisa by the other shoulder and pulled her through, and Harry stepped through after, and turned in the doorway, and looked back down the corridor at the troll, and let the club go.

He let it go beside the troll. Not on it. He turned the sentence, the way he had turned a dimmer, and moved the club a yard to the left before he let the holding stop, and it fell, and hit the flagstones with a sound like the end of the world, and the troll — which had been looking up — looked down, at the club, at the noise, and made a sound of its own, deep and confused and aggrieved, and turned, and went the other way, towards the far end of the corridor and the stairs to the dungeons where it had been supposed to be all along, dragging nothing.

He shut the door.

He stood with his back against it in the little passage by the staff table with his arm shaking and his heart going and a girl with a bag sobbing into Hermione’s shoulder, and he found that he could not, for a moment, say anything at all, and that the moment was not the long quiet. It was something else. It was the thing after the ice. He let it be that.

“You walked,” said Hermione. Her voice had gone very strange. “You walked past it. You held its club over its head and you walked past it and you talked to Lisa through a door—”

“It was slow,” said Harry. “It was so slow, Hermione. It was the slowest thing I’ve ever met. I had — I had all the time in the world.” He slid down the door and sat on the flagstones with the holly across his knees. “Did you get anyone? When you shouted?”

Running feet, on the marble staircase, answered him. Many. Fast.

“Yes,” said Hermione. “I think I got everyone.”

Everyone was McGonagall, and Snape, and Quirrell, in that order, and they came round the corner into the little passage at a run and stopped, and the three faces did three things, and he read all three, sitting on the floor with a wand across his knees and a sobbing first-year beside him, and the reading was the most important thing he did that night and he did not know it yet.

McGonagall’s face was fury, and under the fury, so far under that only he could have seen it, was the thing from a lounge in Surrey when she had looked at the frames and the green line and not resolved them: terror, resolved. She had come round a corner expecting to find something she could not stand and had found a boy on a floor, and the fury was what the terror did when it had nowhere else to go.

Snape’s face was the shut door, and the door was limping. He had come round the corner a half-step behind McGonagall and the half-step was not deference; it was a leg. The left. He was holding it, in the way a man holds a thing he does not want seen, with his weight off it and his robe arranged, and the shut door was shut all the way, and his eyes went — not to Harry; not to the eyes, not this time — to Quirrell.

And Quirrell’s face had two people in it again. The first one was pale and shaking and had plainly been woken, somewhere, and had run; the second one was back, behind it, cold and still and checking — the way the small silver instrument checked; the way the roof checked — and the thing it was checking was Snape’s leg. It looked at the leg for a quarter-second, which was most of a minute, and then it looked at Harry, and the look was the one from a classroom, the is-he-still-there look, except that under it now there was a thing Harry had never seen in it before and did not have a word for in seven languages, and the nearest he could get, afterwards, for the reader, was recalculating.

Behind his door, the grain moved.

Once. The way it had in a classroom. A sleeper hearing its name. He felt it turn over and felt the door hold and did not let anything show on his face, because there were three teachers in a passage and one of them had a thing in him that was watching for exactly that, and he sat on the floor and was a boy who had just been frightened by a troll, which was also true.

“Explain yourself,” said McGonagall. “All of you. Miss Granger. Mr Potter. Miss Turpin—” she saw the bag, and the sobbing, and something in the fury shifted “—Miss Turpin, are you hurt?”

“N-no, Professor. I went back for my bag and everyone had gone and I went the wrong way and it was there, in the corridor, and I hid in the — and he came and he talked to me through the door and said walk and don’t look and I—” She stopped, because she had run out of breath and sentence at the same time.

“I counted,” said Harry, from the floor. “Professor. There were twenty-five first-years and there should have been twenty-six. I told Penelope. She couldn’t leave twenty-five to find one and she was right not to, so I said I’d get her, because the troll was in the dungeons and the first floor is the other way.” He looked up at her. “It wasn’t in the dungeons. It was here. I don’t know why. I walked down the corridor and it came round the corner and I lifted its club and held it over its head so it would look up and not at the lavatory door, and I got Lisa, and I walked her back, and I dropped the club beside it so it would go the other way, and it did. I didn’t touch it. I didn’t hurt it. It was slow and I had time.” He paused. “I didn’t go to the third floor. I want that on record. I gave my word about the third floor and this was the first.”

The passage was very quiet.

McGonagall looked down at him for a long moment with the square spectacles catching the torchlight, and he watched the fury try to find somewhere to go and find that every door was shut, because he had counted, and told the prefect, and gone the other way from the dungeons, and lifted a club and held it and walked a child out and hurt nothing and kept his word, and there was no part of it that a Deputy Headmistress could stand in a passage and call wrong without calling the counting wrong, and she had read the receipts.

“You held it,” she said at last. “Over its head. A troll’s club. A first-year, with—”

“With a Levitation Charm, Professor. The one from chapter four. Theirs, not mine. I’ve been holding a feather with it every Thursday since September. A club is a heavy feather. It cost—” his arm was still shaking; he let her see it “—it cost a great deal. I’ll sleep till the boiler.”

Something in McGonagall’s face gave.

“Five points to Ravenclaw,” she said. “For Miss Granger, for the loudest shout I have heard in this castle in thirty-four years, which brought three teachers to a corridor at a run. Five to Ravenclaw for Mr Potter, for counting, which nobody else in a hall of four hundred did. And twenty from Ravenclaw, from both of you, for being in a corridor with a troll in it when you had been told to go to your dormitory, because I do not care how slow it was, and I do not care that you had time, and I will not have first-years walking past trolls on the grounds that they can hold a sentence steady, and if you ever do it again I shall find a thing to take from you that is not points.” She drew a breath. “Miss Turpin. Hospital wing. Now. Miss Granger, take her. Mr Potter—”

“Yes, Professor.”

“Stay where you are. I want to look at you.”

Hermione took Lisa. She looked back once, from the stairs, at Harry on the floor, and her face did the thing from the compartment on the train, and then she was gone, and McGonagall stood over him, and Snape stood with his weight off his left leg, and Quirrell stood with two people in his face, and nobody said anything.

Then Snape said, softly, to Quirrell, without looking at him: “You fainted, Quirinus.”

“I — yes. The — the shock. I had only just — I came round and there was nobody, and I heard the girl shout, and I—”

“You heard the girl shout from the Great Hall. On the floor. In a faint.”

“I had come round, Severus, I—”

“Hm,” said Snape. It was Mrs Prentice’s hm exactly, and Harry nearly looked up at it, and did not. “And the troll was in the dungeons.”

“I saw it in the dungeons. I don’t — I don’t know how it—”

“No,” said Snape. “No. Nor do I.” He shifted his weight. The left leg took it and did not like it and the shut door did not move. “Minerva. The troll is loose on the first floor and has, I gather, gone towards the dungeons unaccompanied. I shall see to it. Quirinus will help me, since he knows where it was.” He turned, and limped, and did not hide the limp this time, and Quirrell went after him with the two faces doing two things, and the second one looked back, once, at a boy on a floor, for a quarter-second that was most of a minute, and then they were gone.

McGonagall looked down at him.

“Professor Snape is limping,” said Harry.

“Yes.”

“He wasn’t at the feast when Quirrell came in. He came in a minute after. And he went out the staff door before the Hall was empty. I saw him go.” He kept his voice level. “The third floor is up the staff stairs from that door. I didn’t go there. I want it on record that I’m saying I didn’t, because I think somebody did, and I think somebody else did, and one of them is limping, and the other one fainted on a floor and then wasn’t on it, and the troll wasn’t in the dungeons, and I am sitting on a floor with my arm shaking telling the Deputy Headmistress what I saw because the Headmaster told me on the second Thursday to tell him what I saw and he is not here and you are.”

Minerva McGonagall looked at the Boy Who Lived on the floor of a passage by the staff table for a long time, with the fury gone entirely out of her face and the thing under it, the terror resolved, turned into something else that he had seen on her once, on a pavement in the Charing Cross Road, with a carrier bag.

“Dr Potter,” she said quietly. “Get up. Go to the hospital wing and let Poppy look at that arm, which you have strained, and then go to bed, and do not come down for breakfast; I shall have something sent. And on Thursday, at four, you will tell the Headmaster every word of what you have just told me, in that order, and I shall be in the room, because I have decided that I had better be.” She held out a hand.

He took it. She pulled him up. She did not let go at once.

“And Dr Potter. The club. Beside it. Not on it.”

“Yes, Professor. It was following a noise. It wasn’t — it didn’t mean anything. I don’t hurt things that don’t mean anything. It’s—” he searched, and found it in a courtyard with a round boy and a bezoar “—it’s a rule.”

“Hm,” said McGonagall, and let go of his hand, and he went to the hospital wing, and Poppy Pomfrey looked at his arm with her square face and said strained, nothing torn, you held something too heavy for too long, what was it, and he said a club, and she said whose, and he said a troll’s, and she did not say anything for a moment and then said Wednesday, eight o’clock, and you will tell me how, because I should like to write it down, and gave him a thing to drink that tasted of winter, and he slept till the boiler, which was a thing that had not happened to him since a pencil.

Hermione and Neville were on the stairs outside Ravenclaw Tower the next morning when he came down, which they should not have been, because Neville was a Gryffindor and had no business on those stairs and had plainly been there some time, and they stood up when they saw him, both of them, and he stopped.

“You walked past it,” said Neville. It was the first thing he said. His round face had something in it that had not been there over a bezoar. “Hermione told me. You held its club over its head and you walked past it and you talked to Lisa Turpin through a door.”

“It was slow, Neville.”

“I don’t care how slow it was.” Neville’s voice had gone up and he did not seem to notice. “You went back for her. You counted and there were twenty-five and you went back. Nobody — Harry, nobody goes back. Everyone always goes forward and the one who went the wrong way is just—” He stopped. He had gone red. He did not stop. “I’ve been the one who went the wrong way. My whole life. And nobody ever counted.”

Harry did not say anything, because there was nothing in seven languages for it.

“So we’re coming,” said Hermione. Her chin was up. “Next time. Whatever it is. You said on the train that everyone whose parents stood on that side of the barrier should sit together, and I said yes, and I meant yes, and I shouted for a teacher last night because you told me to and it was the right thing and I would do it again, and I am also going to be behind you, where you know where I am, every time, because you can hear things I can’t and I can count things you miss, and I counted that you didn’t eat after the biscuit.”

“There won’t be a—”

“Harry,” said Hermione.

He looked at the two of them on the stairs. A girl who chased and caught and counted, and a boy who had been the one who went the wrong way and had just found out that somebody counted, and he thought about a hat in the dark saying do not open it alone, and about a man who had decided to be sure, and about a thing behind a door that had moved twice now, and he found that he had, somewhere between a corridor and a stair, stopped being the only one in the room in a way that could not be undone, and that he had not asked for it, and that he would not have given it back for the fourth floor.

“All right,” he said. “All right. Yes. Next time. Behind me. Where I know where you are.” He paused. “Both of you. That’s — that’s sworn, then. I don’t know the word they use here. Carrow would have called it a collaboration and Mr Pole would have called it a club.”

“It’s called friends, Harry,” said Neville, very red, to the stairs. “That’s what it’s called. You’ve got a doctorate. I thought you’d know.”

Harry stood on the stairs outside Ravenclaw Tower with his arm aching from a troll’s club and a Gryffindor telling him the word for a thing, and found, for the fourth time in two months, that he had nothing ready, and that he was getting used to it.

“I’ll write it down,” he said. “In the pad. For the person in fifty years. Friends. Neville told me.”

“Good,” said Neville, and went to breakfast, and Hermione went with him, and Harry stood a moment longer on the stairs, and then followed, and did not count anything the whole way down, which was the first time, and he noticed, and let it be.

He wrote the night in the forty-seventh pad that evening, by the window, with the mountains, and he wrote it in order, the way he would say it on Thursday to a man with a hundred eyes with a woman in tartan in the room.

Quirrell came in with the second face gone and the first one terrified, and fainted, and the faint was false — not the fear; the stopping. Something reached in from under and said down. The second face emptied the Hall and did not need to be present to do it.

Snape was not at the feast when Quirrell came in. He came in a minute after and went out the staff door before the Hall was empty. The staff door goes up to the third floor. He came back limping. Left leg. He did not hide it the second time.

The troll was not in the dungeons. It was on the first floor. Quirrell said dungeons. Quirrell was the only one who had seen it.

Quirrell was on the floor in a faint and then was not on the floor and arrived in a passage behind Snape having "heard the girl shout from the Great Hall." Snape said hm. The second face looked at Snape's leg and then at me and it was recalculating. The door moved. Second time. Thirty-two days since the first.

I lifted a club and held it and walked past a troll and talked to a girl through a door. I did not hurt it. I dropped the club beside it. McGonagall said beside, not on, in a voice that was not fury. I said it's a rule.

Twenty-five. I counted. Nobody else did. Neville said nobody ever counted and he had been the one who went the wrong way his whole life, and then he said the word, and the word is friends, and I have a doctorate and did not know it.

Hermione put a biscuit by my plate on the tenth anniversary of the night my mother asked and did not look at me and said I don't know what you mean. I have put that on the shelf with the cap and I think the shelf is going to need a second shelf.

Thursday. All of it. In order. She will be in the room. I have decided that I am glad.

He closed the pad. He looked at the inside of his own arm, where there was nothing to see and a thing that still ached, and thought about a troll looking up at its own club in the torchlight with an expression of slow aggrieved wonder, and found, to his surprise, that of all the faces he had read that night, that was the one he liked best, because it was the only one that had meant exactly what it looked like.

He slept. He had not expected to. He did.
