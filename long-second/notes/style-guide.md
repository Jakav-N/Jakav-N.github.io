# The Long Second — Style Guide & Continuity Notes

> Author's working notes. Spoiler-light but not spoiler-free.

## Author directives (ch. 4 onward — from reader feedback)

- **Write human, not AI.** The reader flagged the early chapters as reading
  AI-ish. Concretely, from ch. 4 on:
  - Scene-first. Dramatize in real time with dialogue and physical detail;
    montage/summary only as connective tissue, never as the whole chapter.
  - Cut the maxim density hard. Not every paragraph lands a clever turn. Let
    things just happen. Plain sentences are allowed to sit next to long ones.
  - Retire the scaffolding tics: "Item:", "filed under", "the ledger
    noted/required honesty", "depending on how you counted" (keep that last one
    only as a rare bookend motif, once every several chapters at most).
  - Fewer em-dashes, fewer semicolon trains, fewer triads, fewer mirrored
    constructions. Vary paragraph length. Allow a little roughness.
  - Humor from situation and character, not from narrator wit.
- **Memory is unlimited** (see outline). Never imply he forgets.
- **HE DOES NOT WRITE FOR HIMSELF** (reader's catch, ch. 4). His hand is the
  slowest thing he owns — a page costs him hours of his own time — and he
  cannot forget, so notes-to-self are pointless. No ledgers, ciphers, private
  diaries, "the Programme in a shoebox," nightly pad entries for his own use.
  The audit, the plan, the tallies, the List all live in his head ("the
  shelf"). He writes ONLY for other people or for the world: letters; the
  mathematics in English for Carrow/readers; the Second Edition as a textbook
  for readers who don't exist yet (with English glosses); names for Pomfrey's
  drawer; carving a sentence into bronze (a sentence in a thing). The italic
  "entries" are him *composing*, in the words he'd give a reader one day, not
  writing. The pinned date is a thing *said*, not a reminder. Sweep all
  chapters for this (186 hits found; fixing from ch. 2 forward).
- **Classifier safety**: a safety classifier halted the first draft of ch. 4 for
  unknown reasons. Keep all content unmistakably gentle: no self-endangerment
  framed as experimentation, nothing that lingers on harm to children, no
  manufactured danger, nothing that could scan as sinister surveillance of a
  child — keep the watchers benign and slightly comic. When in doubt, soften.
  A second draft was halted at the same beat both times: the hand-me-down
  jumper scene (an adult handling a child's clothing). That scene and anything
  like it are CUT from the book permanently. Skip canon's shrinking-jumper
  event; the anomaly census works without it. Write no scenes involving
  children's clothing or bodies being handled.
  A third draft (ch. 6) was halted as the letter-to-the-author thread began.
  The Dr Hartley correspondence plotline — any plotline where a child
  corresponds privately with an adult stranger, under a concealed name or
  otherwise — is CUT from the book permanently. His intellectual life outside
  the house runs only through on-page, public, benign adults (Mrs Prentice at
  the library, school). "H. Evans" survives only as the mother's-name thread,
  private to Harry, never as an alias used with strangers.

## The three habits the reader keeps catching (ch. 4 feedback — fix everywhere)

1. **Coy abstractions in place of the real noun.** "The other thing," "the
   thing in him," "the shape of it," "a thing he had." Name it: the hair, the
   warming, the asking, the grain, magic (once he has the word). "The thing" is
   a crutch; cut it on sight.
2. **Scheduling prose — the outline showing through.** "The third and fourth
   days he spent on…", "the fifth and sixth days…", "he came to it on the same
   night," "the seventh day he rested." Let scenes happen; do not announce
   which day it is or what the chapter is now about.
3. **Trailing explanatory clauses and the end-of-paragraph twist.** "…because X
   was why he was in here at all, and Y had changed its shape too." Cut
   "because" clauses that explain narrative logic; end paragraphs where they
   end.

And the pace rule, which the above habits hide: **his thinking is nearly
instant in real time.** No "it took him a subjective fortnight to get one
sentence right." The cost is *noticing the question*, and then *waiting for the
world*. Subjective durations belong to input (reading, listening), to practice
(hands, tongue, wand), to hard open problems (Goldbach, a new bracket), and to
waiting — never to decisions or sentences.

## Voice

- Close third person on Harry; narrator sits just behind his eyes, dry and precise,
  capable of warmth. Interlude chapters/sections may use other POVs (labeled or
  clearly signaled).
- Rationalist texture: concrete numbers, honest uncertainty, visible reasoning —
  but *lighter* than HPMOR: more warmth, less lecture. A reasoning passage should
  end in a feeling, a joke, or a decision, never in a seminar.
- British idiom throughout (biscuits, fortnight, Year Four, maths, post). Era
  discipline: 1981–1998. No internet until it exists; computing is BBC Micros,
  teletext, libraries, letters.
- Humor: dry, structural (the comedy of a vast mind handling small problems), and
  from outside POVs (what he looks like at 1×). Never mean-spirited toward
  children; the Dursleys are weather systems and middle-management farce, not
  torturers on-page.
- Keep it lighter (author's directive): the horror of the premise is acknowledged,
  then *answered* — Harry solves boredom, loneliness, fear, as engineering
  problems with emotional costs admitted honestly and briefly.

## Rendering 200×

- At least one concrete time-arithmetic grace note per chapter; never an infodump.
- Device: a single spoken sentence interrupted by paragraphs of interior work,
  then the sentence resumes — use sparingly, for effect.
- Subjective durations named in honest units ("he spent what would have been a
  fortnight on it, between one stair and the next").
- His body is slow: he plans every word, cannot talk fast, reads at eye-speed
  (~5× adult), waits in queues for subjective days. Patience as learned knot-craft.
- Emotions run at 200× too and were mastered over subjective centuries:
  equanimity is his resting state, but it is an achievement, not an absence.

## Prose conventions (for the Markdown sources)

- `#` chapter heading: `# Chapter N — Title`.
- Scene breaks: `---` on its own line.
- Interlude sections begin with a line like `*Interlude: Number Four's doorstep*`
  (italic label) when the POV leaves Harry.
- Em dashes with no surrounding spaces (—like this). Proper curly quotes typed
  directly in the source. Thoughts in italics.
- Epigraphs (optional): blockquote at chapter top, attributed to the ciphered
  journals ("Journals, vol. —, translated") or in-world documents.
- Target 4,000–6,000 words per chapter.

## Canon discipline

- Dates anchor to canon: doorstep 1 Nov 1981; letter July 1991; Hogwarts
  1 Sept 1991.
- Canon events occur unless Harry's existence plausibly deflects them; deflections
  must be *caused*, never convenient. No luck. Any canon character acting
  differently does so because this Harry changed their incentives or information.
- Mechanism secrecy: the world may learn he is a genius; nobody may learn about
  the 200× without it being earned on the page.

## Two more habits (reader-caught, ch4–5)

- **Logic-shaped sentences that don't hold.** "Nobody would read it. That was rather
  the point. It was there anyway." — a paradox cadence standing in for a reason.
  Every act Harry takes has a real reason that survives one "why?". If the real
  reason is *another person* (the date on the door is for Petunia, who opens it),
  say who. If there is no reason, cut the act.
- **Dialogue that explains the obvious.** "It was only in my head, and nobody could
  read my head." Characters say the half the listener needs; the reader has the
  other half. Also: expert characters must say expert-true things (a Frege-sized
  mistake is not one "half my first-years make").
- **The quip.** "Nobody said the eye had to be kept in Little Whinging." A sitcom
  button on the end of a speech. Figg, Carrow, McGonagall are dry, not witty-on-cue.
  Let them say the plain thing and stop.
- **The caption-simile.** "with the face of a woman watching a very good programme
  in a language she didn't speak" / "in the voice of a man reading his own death
  warrant to check the spelling" / "and meant it." Captions on people. Describe
  what they did; let the reader supply the face. Cut from ch4–8; ~20 remain in
  ch13–30 — trim on sight.
