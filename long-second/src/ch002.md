# Chapter 2 — The Operating Manual

> Every household keeps two sets of rules: the ones it announces and the ones it runs on. Nobody writes the second set down. Somebody should. It took me years to accept that I had been given the only copy.
> —the journals, vol. I

The cupboard under the stairs became his in the spring after his fourth birthday, by a process that no one in the house would have been able to reconstruct afterwards, because no one in the house had noticed it was a process.

The official sequence of events ran as follows. Dudley’s toys, breeding steadily in the second bedroom, reached the density at which Aunt Petunia could no longer open the door without preparing herself first, and began to annex the box room where Harry slept. Uncle Vernon observed, over several Sundays, that the boy hardly had anything *in* there anyway. Aunt Petunia observed that the camp bed would fit perfectly well under the stairs, where she had been meaning to clear out the hoover attachments for years. It was all extremely sensible, and the Dursleys congratulated themselves on their sense, and if either of them felt a flicker of something less comfortable about billeting a four-year-old in a cupboard, the flicker was smothered under the warm agreement that it was only practical, and that he was a strange, quiet child who probably preferred it.

The unofficial sequence of events had taken Harry five months.

It had begun the day he understood—watching Dudley receive, inhabit, and lay waste to the second bedroom—that in this house, rooms were status, and status would never flow his way, but that *space* and *status* were different currencies, and he only needed one of them. What he needed was a door that shut. A place where no eyes went. Somewhere the estate—the whole folded-down country of him—could be unfolded, inventoried, and worked, without first checking the hall for weather.

The second bedroom could never be that; it had traffic. But the cupboard under the stairs had a door, a latch, a light with its own switch, and a social meaning so low that no Dursley would ever open it except to shove something in. It was the one space in the house that suspicion had no reason to visit. He had conducted the campaign with the patience of a man moving a garden wall six inches by leaning on it: toys he was alleged to share migrating to Dudley’s room a piece a week; the hoover attachments developing a tendency to be in Petunia’s way; the virtues of the arrangement arriving in Vernon’s mind, by way of small staged inconveniences, as his own shrewd idea. When the camp bed finally scraped in under the sloping ceiling and the door clicked shut on him for the first time with his things inside it—one bed, one blanket, one shoebox—he lay in the dark of the smallest office in England and felt the nearest thing to proprietorship he had ever been permitted.

The spiders held a general meeting and decided to tolerate him. He decided to learn their names.

It would be put about, much later, by people writing about his childhood with more indignation than information, that he had been *kept* in a cupboard, and the word did an honest day’s work for the indignant, and he never bothered to correct it. But the truth, set the same night on the growing shelf he kept for the house, where no one could read it because it was in his head and nowhere else, was this: he had surveyed Number Four, Privet Drive, identified the one defensible position it contained, and taken it.

---

He had been in possession of the marks—the whole lovely standing army of them—for a year by then, and this is how the campaign had gone.

The problem of reading, approached from zero, with no teacher, by someone who must not be seen learning, decomposes into three problems. First: which marks exist? (Manageable. The ranks of marks that mattered came in two parades, big and small, of twenty-six each, plus a rabble of numbers and dots; he had the full set collected within a fortnight of the newsagent’s, harvested from cereal packets at breakfast while appearing to mash his cereal.) Second: what sounds do the marks make? (Hard. The marks of English, he would eventually conclude, had the loosest professional discipline of any system he ever studied; the letter combinations drank, moonlighted, and lied about their hours.) Third: how do you get a corpus—marks and speech, certified to mean the same thing, delivered side by side where a small boy can study them without anyone once saying, *what’s he looking at?*

It was the third problem that should have been impossible, and the television solved it for him, twice over, because the television was the one member of the household that answered questions tirelessly and never looked down.

The first gift was the adverts. Adverts repeated—the same twenty seconds, dozens of times a week—and adverts loved to print their slogans on the screen while a warm voice spoke them aloud. Marks, sound, meaning: certified, synchronised, and repeated until even an ordinary child half-learned them, which was the camouflage. Nobody marvels at a four-year-old chanting adverts.

The second gift he found by accident on a wet Saturday, when Dudley—mashing the remote control in pursuit of something that had stopped pleasing him—struck the sequence that summoned page 888, and words appeared at the bottom of the film, white on black bars, and *changed when the people spoke*. Subtitles. For the deaf, said the continuity announcer. Harry sat very still on the lounge carpet, in the blind spot behind the armchair where he was tolerated when quiet, and inside him the whole country of one came to a halt and stared.

A machine that wrote down speech as it happened. In the house. Available, on quiet afternoons, to a boy who had learned the remote control’s logic through the back of an armchair by sound alone.

After that it was not a question of whether but of how long, and the answer was five months—five months of real time, which was, in the only hours he truly lived in, the length of an honours degree. He worked the way water works, every allowed afternoon, matching the moving speech to the standing marks, prising the system open combination by combination: the hard C and the soft one, the silent K (filed under *ambush*), the behaviour of GH, which he came to regard the way Petunia regarded next door’s dog. The spelled-out secrets of the kitchen—P, A, R, K; S, W, E, E, T, S—converted overnight from a code he had cracked by frequency into plain text, which was a strange small loss; he found he missed the puzzle.

And then one evening in late spring—he was not quite four—the local news put up a caption under a man standing in front of a half-built road, and Harry read it. Not assembled it, not solved it: *read* it, the way the man at the newsagent’s had read, the marks going silently in and arriving as speech, no border, no customs, the whole sentence simply present in his head in the half-second his eyes crossed it. BYPASS DELAYED AGAIN, COUNCIL BLAMES COSTS. Fixed speech, flowing free.

He had expected satisfaction; he had logged, in advance, the slot where the satisfaction would go. What arrived instead frightened him for a second with its size. It was the doorstep feeling—the warmth rising through the blankets, somebody somewhere disagreeing with the cold—except that this time he had built the disagreement himself, plank by plank, in five months, under the weather’s eye, and nobody knew, and nobody could ever take it out of him. They could lock every door in the house. The marks were everywhere, and all of the marks were his now, and each one would stay read for the rest of his life.

That night in the box room (this was before the cupboard) he lay planning in long luxurious detail the reading of everything—*everything*, the word had a taste—and ran, almost at once, into the fact that would govern the next year of his life.

Number Four, Privet Drive, contained almost nothing to read.

He conducted the census over the following week, mostly while dusting; Petunia had begun delegating him small chores, which suited them both for different reasons. The haul, in full: one daily newspaper (excellent, but confiscated to Vernon’s chair by evening and binned by morning); the Radio Times; Petunia’s magazines (a rich seam of the language of insinuation, if thin on facts); a phone book; the instruction manuals for every appliance Vernon had ever bought, pristine in a kitchen drawer; a Bible with a wedding inscription and an unbroken spine; one shelf of hardbacks in the lounge, bought by the yard for their bindings; and, in Dudley’s room, forty-one picture books and annuals in various states of structural failure, none opened voluntarily in living memory.

He read all of it. Of course he read all of it. He read the phone book—it taught him the shape of names, the geography of Surrey, and the pleasing fact that the world contained an A. Aardvark, Aquatics, of Byfleet. He read the manuals and became, silently, the only person in the house who knew what the washing machine’s third dial did. He read the hardbacks in burgled instalments, a chapter per dusting. The famine was not of marks but of *new* marks; at his speeds, the house was a water glass and he needed a sea, and the sea—he knew exactly where the sea was, he had known since before he could read it. The word was painted on a building he had passed on the shopping run, gold on green, and it was the most beautiful word in Little Whinging:

LIBRARY.

The problem was that the library was a quarter-mile beyond the end of the reins, in territory he was never taken to, and that between him and it stood the whole architecture of being four: no key, no rights of movement, no account of his own, and a guardian whose policy on Harry-related requests was refusal as reflex. A frontal ask would not just fail; it would *flag* the library as something he wanted, and everything he wanted was, by the operating logic of the house, suspect.

So he did not ask. He opened a file, and gave the file a year, and the file’s first entry was an observation that would have surprised everyone who ever met the Dursleys: the household, examined coldly, was not a fortress. It was a machine with exactly three moving parts, and all three parts ran on schedules, and schedules were the native terrain of a boy who had once watched a star cross a gap between rooftops just to see it done.

---

The machine’s parts, as he had them that spring:

**The Purple One** ran on esteem. Vernon Dursley needed the world to confirm, hourly, that he was a man of substance and sense, and would trade almost anything for the confirmation without ever noticing a trade had occurred. He was dangerous the way a sash window is dangerous: not malicious, but heavy, and inclined to come down hard on whatever was under it when the cord slipped. Management protocol: stay out from under. Supply, in homeopathic doses, the pleasure of being right. Never be interesting.

**The Sharp One** ran on fear, and the fear ran on two fuels. The surface fuel was the neighbours—what might be seen, what might be said at a fence. But under it, in the nailed-shut drawer, was the other thing: the sister-shaped hole, the *whom*, the old fear that the freakishness would bloom again in her own house and swallow a second family. He understood perhaps a tenth of this at four—the tenth that could be read in a face at flood stage—and he handled her accordingly, as one handles a cracked vessel: no pressure, no sudden temperatures. Boring, boring, boring, eleven months and counting of boring. It was working. She had begun leaving him unsupervised with the chores.

**The Small Loud One** ran on appetite, and was therefore—Harry recorded this with the dispassion of a naturalist—the easiest instrument in the house. Dudley wanted; whatever stood between Dudley and the want was demolished; the want, obtained, died instantly and was replaced. You could not reason with the loop, but you could *steer* it, the way you steer a shopping trolley with a mad wheel: small inputs, early. A word about what was on the other channel. A toy left visible at the right moment. Dudley’s rampages, which had once cost Harry his few possessions on a monthly basis, had not touched the shoebox in half a year, because the shoebox was boring and the alternatives never were. The books Harry was quietly accumulating—rescued annuals, a one-eyed *Ladybird Book of London*—lived their public lives as “Dudley’s old things Harry tidies,” a category Dudley defended as property when challenged and forgot otherwise, which made Dudley, functionally, their armed guard.

It was not kindness, any of it; he did not pretend to himself that it was. He kept a place on the shelf where he was honest about the whole arrangement. These were the people he had, and none of them could be trusted with a single true thing about him, and he was going to run their house from a cupboard because the alternative was to be run. But he noted—because the place required honesty and the honesty went both ways—that the machine, well-steered, was *calmer*. Vernon shouted less, finding less to shout at. Petunia’s headaches thinned. Dudley, nudged away from his worst boredoms, broke fewer things, including, lately, fewer parts of other children. A better-run household was its own camouflage, and it was also—the thought arrived unbidden one evening, watching Petunia almost smile at a tidy hall—simply better, in some ledger larger than his, which he had not yet drawn up but could feel, sometimes, like weather beyond a hill.

He was four. He filed it under *later*.

---

Twice that year, the world showed him its second set of rules.

The first time was at Mrs Figg’s.

Mrs Figg lived two streets over in a house that smelled of cabbage and was upholstered in cats, and she was what the Dursleys did with Harry when even the cupboard would not do—weddings, hospital visits, Dudley’s birthday outings. The Dursleys considered her a batty old woman convenient precisely because she was the sort of person who would take ten pence an hour to mind a boy nobody wanted. Harry, by four, had a different entry for her, flagged and growing.

Item: Mrs Figg was the only adult in his life who asked him questions and then waited, actually waited, for the answers. Item: the questions had a pattern. Sprinkled through the chatter about Tibbles and Snowy and Mr Paws came, regular as posts in a fence, the other kind—was he sleeping, was he eating, had anything odd happened lately, anything *funny*, any accidents?—delivered in the same batty singsong, but landing, every visit, in the same places. Grown-ups repeated themselves; that was baseline. These repeated like a form being filled in.

Item—and this was the day it stopped being a curiosity and became a file—the photograph.

It was a dim afternoon in March, rain on the windows, Dudley at the zoo without him. Mrs Figg had gone to make tea, and Harry was conducting his standing survey of the mantelpiece, which held nine framed photographs of cats, because at two hundred to one even a mantelpiece of cat photographs is worth a periodic audit. Eight of the frames held what they always held. In the ninth, behind glass, a grey cat with peculiar square markings around its eyes sat on a garden wall—and as his eyes crossed it, the cat turned its head.

Fast, for the world. Lazy, for him. A long feline swivel, the head coming around through forty degrees of arc to look—he would have sworn it, did swear it, to the only court he answered to—directly at him, with an expression no cat on any tin of cat food had ever worn: appraising. Interested. *Professional.* Then the kettle shrieked its slow shriek in the kitchen, and the cat was a photograph again, and Mrs Figg came in with the tray and followed his eyes, and he watched her face do a thing that Petunia’s face could not have done to save its life: go perfectly, instantly, boringly blank. She set down the tray, picked up the ninth frame as if straightening it, and carried it out of the room in the same motion that offered him a biscuit.

He took the biscuit. He ate it slowly, with the full honours of toddler absorption, eyes on the middle distance, while inside, the country of one declared a state holiday and every office of the government turned to the same page.

The warmth in the blankets, that first night, that answered need and could not be accounted for. Filed three years, pending, flagged *memory of an infant; unreliable; do not build on*. And now: a photograph that moved, in the house of the one adult who asked, every visit, on a schedule, whether anything *funny* had happened to him—a photograph she removed, with tradecraft, the moment she saw it seen.

Two anomalies did not make a theory. He set the theory on the shelf anyway, that night, and put a box round it, which was his own mark for *expensive if true*, and the shelf was the only place it went, because he had no need of paper and a page would have cost him an hour of his hand for a thing he could not have forgotten if he tried:

*There is more than one set of rules. Somebody runs on the second set. Somebody is watching to see if I do.*

---

The second time, the world used a gentler instrument: a nursery school teacher named Miss Henmore, twenty-four years old, kind as bread, who very nearly saw him whole.

St Gregory’s Pre-School had entered his life that September, three mornings a week, and he had walked in through its gate with a complete, rehearsed, written policy. Dossier name: *the Bright Little Boy*. Specification: top quartile, never top. Errors: two per ten, varied, plausible—reversed letters, miscounts of one. Speech: full sentences permitted (school had different physics from home; here, articulate was safe, even rewarded) but vocabulary capped at a ceiling he revised weekly by observing the cleverest ordinary children. Questions: asked often, because silence was conspicuous, but calibrated to things a bright four-year-old wouldn't know. It was, he believed, a conservative design, and he shipped it with confidence.

It survived eleven days.

The failure analysis, conducted in the cupboard the same evening, was instructive, and he kept its conclusions for the rest of his life. The policy had failed not through a breach but through *accumulation*. He had made no single error. That was the error. Miss Henmore, dispensing nothing more sophisticated than attention, had noticed that Harry’s mistakes never repeated. Ordinary children's errors had *signatures*—the same letters flipped, the same sums overshot, week after week. His were perfect strangers to one another, a different plausible slip every time, because he had been generating them from a model of “plausible slip” instead of from a hand that genuinely slipped. Randomness, it turned out, was a tell. True noise had structure, and his noise was too clean, and nobody consciously sees that, but bread-kind young women who watch children all day see it without knowing what they’ve seen.

She had kept him back at tidy-up time, friendly as anything, and shown him a word no four-year-old in the catchment should have been able to read—*yesterday*, on a staffroom notice about the boiler—and asked him, smiling, no trap in her anywhere, to tell her what it said. And he had looked at it, and in the two seconds he took to decide (a comfortable six minutes, in the only hours he truly lived in, and he needed all of them) the courtroom of the country of one heard the following:

That a lie direct—*I can’t read it, Miss*—would hold today and corrode tomorrow; she would watch now, and the policy could not survive watching, as the last eleven days had proved. That the bell, once rung, could not be unrung; her belief had already updated, and the only live question was what shape it settled into. That there existed, between the Bright Little Boy and the truth, a defensible middle fortification: the *odd, clever child*—reads early, self-taught, solemn, a bit alone; a known type, a *filed* type; remarkable enough to explain everything she had seen, mundane enough to explain nothing that mattered. The world had a drawer for that child. Drawers were safety. The whole art, he decided that night, was never to be drawerless.

“Yesterday,” said Harry, and then, laying the first brick of the fortification, with the solemnity the type specified: “The boiler’s going to be mended on Thursday. What’s a flue?”

Miss Henmore’s delight was total, warm, and—he noted it with something that was not entirely the ledger talking—rather nice to stand in, nicer than he had budgeted for. He let the policy die gracefully. In its place rose the odd clever child of St Gregory’s, who read to the hamster at break, who was allowed—the single negotiated treasure of the whole retreat—to take books home from the classroom shelf, two at a time, officially because Miss Henmore believed in meeting children where they were.

There was a price, and the price arrived by telephone, as all the worst things did. *A gifted child, Mrs Dursley. You must be so proud.* He listened from the cupboard to Petunia’s half of it, the strangled civilities, the receiver going down like a lid going on, and he sat in the dark among his named spiders and did the sums on the fear that would now be loose in the house. The sums came out survivable. School-clever was not the freakishness; even Petunia’s taxonomy kept those shelved apart. Lily Evans, he would learn in time, had been school-clever too, and the school-cleverness had not been the thing that took her through the drawer in the nailed-shut dresser and out of the ordinary world; but Petunia could not nail shut a parents’ evening, and Vernon found, upon reflection and two helpings of esteem, that a nephew being called gifted reflected, when you thought about it, rather well on the household that was bringing him up. Right-thinking people’s children did well at school. It was the sort of thing one might mention, lightly, at the Rotary.

Harry, listening through the cupboard door to his uncle rehearse the sentence, closed the file on the incident and opened, with great care, the one it had been blocking for a year.

If the school could praise him and the house absorb it—if *gifted* could be laundered into Vernon’s esteem and come out safe—then the library was no longer a quarter-mile away. It was one well-built sentence away, and the sentence would not even have to be his. Miss Henmore, who believed in meeting children where they were, would write it for him by October, in a voice no Dursley could refuse without looking, at a fence, at the Rotary, like the wrong sort of family.

He lay back on the camp bed in the smallest office in England, hands behind his head, and listened to the house tick, and let himself, for one whole glorious unbudgeted minute—three hours and twenty subjective minutes, the longest holiday of his life to date—simply gloat.

The sea. Shelves and shelves of it, gold on green. All the voices that could not be used up.

Four years old, or eight hundred, depending on how you counted, and the second set of rules was out there waiting for him, and now, at last, so was the first.
