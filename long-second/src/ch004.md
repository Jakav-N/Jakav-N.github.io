# Chapter 4 — The Week

The week in the cupboard was the best thing Aunt Petunia ever did for him, and she never knew it, and he never told her.

Seven days. The door shut, meals passed through, no school — she had rung Miss Weald and said *a chill*, in the fence voice — and nothing to do but lie on the camp bed under the slant of the stairs with the Programme where it lived, which was in his head, and the house ticking round him. Seven days, which by the hall clock was a week and by his own was the better part of four years, and he had planned to spend all four of them on the hair.

He kept no notes. He never had. A page took him an hour to fill at the speed of his hand, and he could not have forgotten a line of it if he had tried; the shelves in him had never once dropped anything since a doorstep in November. A boy who cannot forget does not keep a diary. He keeps a shelf, and walks along it, and that was what the Programme was: a shelf, in a cupboard in his head, inside a cupboard under the stairs, with every anomaly since the blanket laid out on it in order where he could see them all at once.

He spent the first day on the hair. It was good work and he kept it: the casefile, the hypothesis, the problem of an effect that ran only when the watcher stood aside. By the end of the first day he had the experiments laid out for a year ahead and the dream log redesigned and every anomaly since the doorstep in a table that pleased him, and he lay back with the light off and felt the particular satisfaction of a department that has got its filing straight.

Then, in the dark, with nothing left to do, he asked himself a question he had never once asked in five years, and the question was so simple that he was ashamed of it before he had finished thinking it.

*Why am I hiding?*

Not from Petunia. He knew why he hid from Petunia; he had the whole of it on the shelf, the face at the cooker, *just like—*, the drawer that would not open. That was a real fear with a real address and the rule he had passed at two to deal with it had been right. He had never doubted the rule.

He doubted it now. Not the rule. Its *reach*.

Because the rule said *be small*, and he had been small — at nursery, at the branch, at St Gregory’s, in the odd-clever-child fortification with its careful ceiling of two years ahead and its two errors in ten. He had drilled sentences for weeks to seem less than he was. He had built a *persona*, with specifications, and shipped it, and when it failed he had built another, lower. And none of those rooms had a drawer with his mother in it. Miss Henmore had wanted to give him more. Mrs Prentice had held a door he could have walked through at four if he had only *asked*. Miss Weald had put him on a register and his aunt had sat with her handbag on her knees and said *no fuss*, and he had let her, because the rule said small, and the rule had been written for one woman in one kitchen and he had been paying its premium in every room in Surrey.

He lay in the dark and did the sum, and the sum was obscene.

Three years since he could read. Six hundred of his own. Three books a week, by rule, when the whole building had been his for the asking. A ceiling of two years ahead, by rule, when the county had machinery for children it could *see* and he had made himself invisible to it on purpose. He had the whole of the branch library in his head and he had never once told anyone, and he had rebuilt arithmetic from one textbook and gone on from there into country he had no names for, alone, under these stairs, and he had never shown a page of it to a living soul, because a woman had been frightened of a shopping list when he was two.

He had been running the cuckoo’s rule. The parents who cannot stop feeding the thing in the nest because the rule says feed. He had *said that*, at five, at a returns desk, about birds, to a woman with a stamp, and had been pleased with himself for saying it.

“Oh,” said Harry Potter, aloud, in the dark, under the stairs, five and a half years old. “Oh, I’ve been *stupid*.”

It was the first time he had ever said it about himself and meant it, and it went through him clean, and he found — lying there with the boiler starting up for the evening — that it did not hurt at all. It felt like a window going up.

---

He spent the second day being honest about the costs, because an audit that skipped the costs was just a feeling in a coat.

He made himself go through every room. Nursery: a year of calibrated errors, a persona that had failed in eleven days and been replaced with a smaller one, and a kind young woman who would have given him the moon and had been given, instead, a boy who read to the hamster. The branch: two years of three tickets a week before Mrs Prentice solved it for him, unasked, in eleven seconds, because he had never once walked up to the desk and said *I have read all of these*. School: Miss Weald, who could see he was deciding to fit, and had said so in a staff room — he could not have known that, but he knew Miss Weald, and he could do the sum — and whom he had rewarded with a reading age *politely off the charts* and mathematics *two years ahead instead of the truth*. The county, with its register and its lady who would come and do puzzles: hidden from, by policy, for a year.

He put the costs in a column and added them. He did not like the total and he kept it anyway, where he kept everything, which was where it could not be lost and could not be softened.

Then he did the other side, because he had learned at the returns desk that a thing with only costs was not an audit, it was a confession. The rule had bought him the cupboard. It had bought him the library run, and Tuesdays — no, that was Miss Henmore, that was a gift he had nearly refused. It had kept him out from under the sash window. It had kept Petunia’s fear at a temperature he could live in, and that was real, that was worth something, and he would keep that part of the rule for exactly as long as he lived in her house.

But outside the house the rule had bought him nothing, for three years, at the price of everything, and the only reason he had never noticed was that he had never once put it up for review, because it had been passed by a two-year-old in an emergency and two-year-olds do not schedule reviews.

He scheduled one. He did it that night, in the place where he kept his rules, and the new rule said: *Every rule I have, I made in a hurry. Every year — my year — I will take them all down and ask whether the person who made them is still here. Mostly he will not be.*

Then he began on what he was going to do instead.

---

The third and fourth days he spent on the plan, and the plan was the first thing he had ever built for the real size of himself, and the real size of himself turned out to be the problem.

Because the obvious thing — *stop hiding* — was not a plan. It was a direction. The plan was *how*, and the how was hard, and he was glad of the two years he had to spend on it, because a child of five who walked into a library and announced that he had read it would be patted on the head, and a child of five who could prove it would frighten people, and he had learned from a shopping list what frightened people did.

So he worked out who could bear it, and in what order, and how much.

Mrs Prentice could bear all of it. He was sure of that the way he was sure of the hall clock. She had tested him with a cuckoo and given him six tickets and the run of the adult shelves without blinking, and she had a face that filed things rather than fled them, and she was the one adult in Little Whinging who had already seen the shape of what he was and had simply, calmly, stood to one side of it and held the door. He would go to her first, and he would not go small.

Mrs Bell, the head, could bear most of it, if it came through Mrs Prentice. The county could bear what the county was built to bear, which was *a great deal*, if it was written down by someone with letters after their name. The newspapers could bear anything and should be given nothing, yet. Uncle Vernon could bear exactly as much as made him look good at the Rotary, and that was a lever, not a limit; he had been pulling it for a year without admitting what it was for.

And Petunia.

He spent most of the fourth day on Petunia, and arrived at the end of it somewhere he had not expected to be. He had been going to keep the rule for her entire. *Small at home, large everywhere else*. But he sat with it, in the dark, with the house ticking, and found that the rule for her had a hole in it the size of a kitchen, and the hole was this: she was going to find out anyway. The school would write. The county would telephone. Vernon would boast. She would hear, in her own hall, that the boy was being tested by a lady from Kingston and talked about at a university, and she would hear it *without him having told her*, and the not-telling would be the thing that frightened her, far more than the fact.

He had been managing her like weather for five years. He had never once tried the front door. He built, that night, the thing he would say to her, and it was one sentence, and it was true, and it took him a subjective fortnight to get it to be both.

---

The fifth and sixth days he spent on the other thing, because the other thing was why he was in here at all, and the audit had changed its shape too.

He had been treating it like a hobby. He saw that now. A Programme on a shelf in his head, worked at in the gaps between library books, as if it were a private puzzle — when it was, by any honest accounting, the single most important fact about his life. Something in him could regrow hair overnight and warm a blanket on a doorstep and set a jam jar down soft, and there were people in cloaks who knew what it was, and one of them bowed to him in the street and one of them watched him from two streets away and asked every month whether anything out of the ordinary had happened, and he had been *filing* all of that, under *pending*, for *years*, and going to school.

“Stupid,” he said again, more gently, because he was getting used to it.

So he put it where it belonged, which was first. Not before the library — the library was the shelves and he could not live without the shelves — but *beside* it, with the same hours and the same seriousness, every day, not when a cat was poorly. He did not yet know how to make it come. He had the hypothesis — it ran when the watcher stood aside — and the hypothesis was good and he distrusted it, because it had been made by a boy who had just slept through his own experiment and was annoyed. He set the questions out properly, the way the Survey had taught him. *What is it for? When does it come? Who else has it? How do they make it come?* And under the last question, after a long time, he put the plainest answer, the one he had been avoiding for two years because the rule said small:

*Ask Mrs Figg.*

She knew. She had known since the cat with the square markings. She had a report to make every month and somebody to make it to, and he had decided, at six — at *six*; a quarter of his life ago; two hundred years — not to poke the kind thing, and had called that wisdom. It had not been wisdom. It had been the rule, in a coat.

He did not know what she would say. He did not know whether she was allowed to say anything. But he had spent two days learning what *not asking* had cost him in every other room in Surrey, and he was not going to spend another six hundred years finding out what it cost him in hers.

---

The seventh day he rested, because the books said that was what you did, and because he had earned it, and because he wanted to look at the plan once more with the whole of it finished and see whether he still believed it.

He did. He lay under the stairs with the light off and went through it — all of it, the costs and the other side, the rooms in order, the sentence for Petunia, *ask Mrs Figg* — and it held, the way a good proof holds, with no soft place anywhere he could lean on and feel give.

Then he said the rule that was going to replace the one from the kitchen. Out loud, in the dark, in a whisper, because the first one had been passed in silence by a boy of two and he wanted this one to have been *said*, once, in that house, in his own voice, so that the house had heard it even if nobody in the house had.

“Be small in one room. Everywhere else, be the size you are, and let them deal with it. That’s their job. Mine is to stop making it hard for them to do.”

He listened to it for a long time by his own clock, the way you listen to a thing you have said to see whether it still sounds true with the air round it. It did. Then he listened to the house, and found that for the first time since the doorstep he was not waiting for anything at all. He was simply going to go and get it, on Monday, when the door was unlocked.

He slept the whole night through. He did not dream of anything. It was, he would say later, the best week of his life, and he had said that about several, and this time he meant it.

---

“Mrs Prentice,” he said, at four on the Monday, with the stepladder between them, “I need to tell you some things. All at once, I think. I’ve been not telling you them for two years and it was a mistake and I’d like to stop.”

She was shelving. She did not stop, but she slowed, and he had learned in two years that the slowing was the thing to watch.

“Go on, then.”

He went on. He had rehearsed none of it, because he had worked out on the third day that rehearsing was what you did to be small *well*, and he was done with that. So he simply told her. That he had read every book in the building, both sides of the room, including the ones on the top shelf behind the stepladder, and could tell her what was in any of them. That the Survey, which she knew about, had been a *map*, and that he had been walking the map for a year looking for the edges, and had found some. That he had rebuilt arithmetic from Dudley’s old textbook under the stairs when he was four, and gone on, and that he thought some of what he had gone on into was in the fat books on the adult side with the German names, and some of it he had not found in any book yet and did not know whether that meant it was new or that the branch did not have the book. That he had been *pretending*, at school, for two years, by a rule he had made when he was two, and that he had spent last week in a cupboard working out that the rule was wrong, and that he was not going to pretend any more, anywhere, except at home, where there was a reason.

That he needed a bigger library. Not the county’s reserve stock. A *university*.

It took him nine minutes at the speed of his mouth, which was the longest he had ever spoken to anyone about anything, and partway through he realised she had stopped shelving and was sitting on the second step of the ladder with her hands in her cardigan pockets, listening the way she listened to nothing else in that building.

When he finished she didn’t say anything for a while.

“Well,” said Mrs Prentice at last. “What took you so long?”

He had expected a dozen things and not that, and it went into him clean and sat there, and he found that he was smiling.

“I was two,” he said. “When I made the rule.”

“Yes. I did gather it was something of that order.” She got up off the step and dusted her skirt, which didn’t need it. “I knew about the top shelf, dear. I knew about the top shelf in October. I moved the Hilbert to the bottom one so you’d stop risking your neck for it, and you noticed, and you never said, and I never said, and I have been waiting since October for you to walk up to this desk and *say*.” She went to the desk. “I shall ring Mrs Bell. I know her from the churchyard committee. She has a drawer with your name on it that she has never liked, and I am going to tell her she may open it.” She wrote on a date-due slip. “And I shall ring somebody else.”

“Who?”

“My husband’s brother lectures at the university in Egham. Chemistry. He is a tiresome man and I have not spoken to him since the funeral, and I am going to telephone him tonight and tell him that there is a child of five in the Little Whinging branch who has read Hilbert off the top shelf, and ask him what the university does about that, and he will say *nothing, Edith, universities do not take children*, and I shall say *then find me the person who decides that*, and he will, because he has always been afraid of me.” She squared the slip. “It may take a fortnight.”

Harry stood by the stepladder with his hands at his sides and found, for the second time in his life, that he had nothing ready.

“Thank you,” he said. “For the door. For — for *October*.”

“Hm,” said Mrs Prentice. “Go and do the crossword. There’s one in the bottom corner I can’t get and I shall be very annoyed if you can.”

---

The lady from the county came on the second Thursday in March, and she brought the big folder, and he did it straight, and she lasted forty minutes before she had to go and stand in the corridor.

Her name was Dr Prosser. She had glasses on a chain and a cardigan the colour of oatmeal and a stopwatch she worked with her thumb under the table where children weren’t supposed to notice it, and he noticed it, and did not pretend not to, and that was the first thing she wrote down.

He was gentle with her. He had decided to be, on the walk down the corridor behind Mrs Bell, with the smell of poster paint getting stronger: he would answer at the speed of his mouth, not his mind; he would not finish anything before she had finished asking it; when the number strings went past the end of her sheet he would say them back carefully and politely as if they were a shopping list. But he would not stop. Not this time. Not at fourteen, where the folder stopped, nor at twenty, nor at thirty, where Dr Prosser stopped reading them out and simply looked at him across the little table with the blocks on it for a long moment and then said, “Right,” and squared the cards, and said “Right” again, and said, “Would you excuse me one moment,” and went and stood in the corridor, and he sat with the orange squash and listened to her breathe out there, slow and even, like a woman doing an exercise from a leaflet.

She came back and did the rest with great steadiness. He thought she was very good at her job; she simply had not known there was a far side to it. At the end she closed the folder and sat back and took the chain glasses off and looked at him with the face he had seen on Mrs Prentice over a cuckoo and nowhere else: a professional, who had been given cause.

“When I was training,” she said, “a long time ago, there was a girl in one of the studies. Up in the valleys. Read the back of the medicine packets in the surgery for something to do, and came out ordinary on every test we gave her — dead ordinary, test after test, until one of us noticed she was ordinary the way a swan is clumsy. On land, you follow me. Because it suited her to be, where she lived.” She clipped the rubber band back round her second pile. “I have wondered on and off for thirty years what became of her. I rather think she’s still in the valleys.”

Harry looked at the blocks box.

“I was being a swan,” he said. “Until last month. On purpose. It was a rule I had, and it was wrong, and I’ve stopped.” He looked up. “Dr Prosser, I don’t know what you can write. I know the forms stop. But I’d like you to write the true thing, whatever the true thing is, and not the kind one, because the kind one is what I’ve been getting for two years and it’s cost me.”

Dr Prosser was quiet for a moment. Then she opened the folder again, and turned to the last page, where there was a box for *Recommendations*, and he watched her, upside down across the little table, cross out something she had already written — he could read it; it said *very able; enrichment* — and write underneath it, in a hand that did not hurry:

*Ceiling not reached. In my professional judgement not reachable with any instrument available to this authority. Recommend immediate referral to university-level provision, with whatever precedent has to be invented to do it. I will put my name to that.*

She signed it. She turned it round so he could see it properly.

“That,” said Dr Prosser, “is the true thing. Heaven help us all.” And then, at the door, holding it open for him, in the voice people use for remarks about the weather: “It gets easier to find them, you know. The swans. Once they decide to be found.”

---

He said the sentence to Petunia on the Friday, at the sink, when the letter from the school had been read and Vernon had taken it into the hall to ring Marge.

He had watched the side of her face while Vernon read it aloud — *exceptional*, *the county’s most senior educational psychologist*, *the university at Egham has expressed an interest* — and the white had come up, as he had known it would, and the hand had gone towards the collarbone, and he had put down the tea towel and said, quietly, so that only she would hear it under Vernon’s voice in the hall:

“It’s only school, Aunt Petunia. Being good at school isn’t the other thing. I promise you it isn’t. I’ll never let it be, in this house.”

She didn’t answer. Her hand came down. She stood with it flat on the letter on the counter and read it again, and what was in her face when she finished was still fear, but under the fear was a thing that had been there the whole time, he saw now — five years — and that he had never once given her a reason to show.

She was *tired*. She had been frightened for five years of a thing she could not say, in a house where nobody would let her say it, and here was the boy at the sink telling her in a sentence that he knew exactly what it was and would keep it out of her kitchen.

“Well,” said Petunia, to the letter. And then, in the fence voice, to the hall: “Vernon. Tell Marge the *university*.”

Vernon told Marge the university. Harry picked up the tea towel and finished the plate, and the whole thing had taken forty seconds, which for him was a working day, and he spent the rest of the washing-up quietly amazed at how little it had cost. One true sentence. Five years of being a convenient nothing to avoid one woman’s fear, and when he finally looked the fear in the face and told it the truth, it had smoothed a letter flat and asked Vernon to say *university* down the telephone.

*The front door*, he thought. *Even with her. The whole time.*

---

He asked Mrs Figg on the last Saturday in March, in the garden, with the cats, because it was the last line of the plan and he had saved it for last on purpose, the way you save the thing you most want.

“Mrs Figg. What are you?”

She was deadheading the geraniums. She did not stop, but she slowed — he had learned the slowing from Mrs Prentice and it meant the same thing in every adult he had met — and he saw her shoulders settle in the way he had seen Petunia’s settle at the sink: the look of a woman putting down something she had been carrying a long way.

“Well,” she said. “There it is.” She snipped a stem and dropped it in the trug. “I wondered when. I had money on next year. Tibbles said this spring.”

“I know there’s a second set of rules,” said Harry. “I’ve known since I was three. I know the man who bowed to me runs on it, and the photograph on your mantelpiece that moved. I know my parents were part of it and didn’t die in a car. I know you watch me for someone, and ask me every month whether anything out of the ordinary has happened, and I know you write it down after. I know my hair grew back in a night and something warmed my blanket on the doorstep when I was a baby, and I know that whatever does that isn’t in any of nine thousand books at the branch, because I looked.” He stopped. “I don’t know the *word*. I’ve never had the word. And I’ve spent two years not asking you for it because I thought asking would make you choose between me and whoever you write to, and I’ve just spent a week in a cupboard working out that not asking has cost me more than anything in my life. So I’m asking.”

Mrs Figg put the secateurs down on the wall. She did it slowly. Then she sat on the garden bench and patted the slats beside her, and he sat, and Mr Tibbles came down off the wall and sat at their feet with the lecture-hall face, as if the examiner had finally turned up.

“I can’t tell you,” she said. “Not today. Not because I don’t want to — Harry, I’ve wanted to since the cat with the markings, and you were three. Because it isn’t mine to tell. There’s a man I write to, and he’s a good man, and he asked me five years ago to watch and to report and not to interfere, and telling a child what he is counts, and I’ve kept to it, and I’ve told him he was wrong to my face once already and he listened and did it anyway.” She looked at her hands. “But I’ll write to him tonight. I’ll tell him you asked. I’ll tell him *how* you asked — all that, the two sets of rules and the nine thousand books — and I’ll tell him that if he says no I shall think a great deal less of him, and I’ll say it in those words.”

“How long?”

“A fortnight. He’s a busy man. Sometimes less.”

Harry sat on the bench in the thin March sun with the cat at his feet and found that he was not disappointed, which surprised him, and he examined the not-being-disappointed and found it was made of a thing he had never had before and had not known he wanted.

She had *said yes to asking*. That was all. She had not told him the word. But she had sat down beside him and told him, plainly, that there was a word, and a man, and a letter going tonight, and that she was on his side in it and would say so in writing to whoever it was. For five years he had been the only person in the world who knew there was a question. Now there were two, and one of them had a stamp.

“Thank you,” he said.

“Oh, get on,” said Arabella Figg, and picked up the secateurs, and her voice had gone odd.

---

The reply came in eleven days. He knew because she told him, at the gate, on the Wednesday, in passing, with Tibbles under her arm: “Saturday, dear. Come at two. Bring an appetite; I’ve opened the good tin.”

He came at two. The good tin was on the table — chocolate biscuits, the Edinburgh one, the one he had seen on the top shelf for three years and never once been offered from — and she poured out with the left hand, as she always did, and sat, and looked at him across the little table for a long moment, and said:

“He said yes. He said—” she took a folded paper from her apron pocket and read from it, in a different voice, a reading voice, “—*if he asks you what you are, tell him the truth. I suspect he already knows. I suspect that is the least of what he knows.*” She folded it away. “He had the right of that, I think.”

“What’s the word?”

“The word is *magic*, dear. Just that. You’ll think it’s a silly word. Everybody does, the first time. It isn’t.”

He had expected to feel something, and he did, but not what he had planned for. It wasn’t revelation; he had known the thing for years, and a word was only a label on a drawer. What he felt, sitting at a table in Wisteria Walk with a chocolate biscuit he had not touched, was smaller and older than revelation, and it took him a second to place it, and the second was a long time.

It was *company*. Someone had said it to him. Out loud, across a table, as a plain fact, the way you’d tell a child the name of a bird.

“Tell me,” he said. “Please. Everything you’re allowed.”

She told him. Not everything — he could feel the gates she kept shut, and respected them, because she kept them with the face of a woman under orders she thought were right. But a great deal. That there were people who could do what had been done to his hair, thousands of them, a whole hidden world of them with their own streets and shops and newspapers, living in the same country behind a kind of curtain, which was why nobody at Number Four had ever met one except by accident. That they were called witches and wizards and the words were not insults where they came from. That she was what they called a *squib*: born to a family of them — two brothers who could, and she who couldn’t — and so she lived on this side and kept cats and did what she could, and what she could, for five years, had been to watch over one small boy for a man who had asked her to.

That there was a school, in Scotland, where children like him went at eleven to learn to do properly what his hair had done by itself. That a letter would come. On his eleventh birthday; it always came on the birthday. That his mother and father had both gone to that school and had been very good — “your mum especially, dear; everyone said” — and that what had happened to them had happened in that world and not this one, and was not hers to tell, and that he would learn it, she promised, and soon enough.

“Eleven,” said Harry.

“The thirty-first of July, 1991. I’ve had it in my diary five years.”

Five years and four months. He sat with that, with the biscuit untouched and the cat on the sugar bowl where he was not allowed. Five years and four months, by the hall clock, in which a world with schools and streets and newspapers would send for him — a world that had sent a man in a violet hat to bow to him and a kind squib to watch him and had taken his parents in some way that was not his to know yet. Five years and four months, which by the only clock that counted was a thousand years and more, and nobody on the far side of the curtain, by the sound of it, had the least idea that the boy on this side had just spent a week in a cupboard deciding to stop hiding, and had rebuilt arithmetic at four, and was about to be given the run of a university.

A thousand years to get ready. He had wasted six hundred being small. He was not going to waste one more.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “Thank you. For all of it. For writing.”

“Eat your biscuit,” said Arabella Figg, and turned to look out of the window so that he wouldn’t see her face, which he had already seen, and would keep.

---

That night the plan got its last line, and the line was a date: *31 July 1991.*

Under it he put what he had and what he meant to do with it, and it was short, because the long version was the rest of his life.

*Five years and four months. Over a thousand of mine.*

*A university, soon, if Mrs Prentice’s brother-in-law is as frightened of her as she says. Everything in it, as fast as my eyes will go. All of it. Every subject, to the edge, and past the edge where I can.*

*The other thing. Every day. Find out what it is for, when it comes, how they make it come. Ask Mrs Figg everything she is allowed to say and work out the rest from the shape of what she isn’t.*

*Be the size I am. Let them deal with it.*

Then he did a thing he had never done, and knew while he was doing it that it was not for remembering.

He took a square of paper from Dudley’s old exercise book, and a pencil, and wrote the date on it, alone, large, in the round careful hand that was the only hand he had, and it took him most of a minute, which was most of three hours of his own, and he did not need it. He would not have forgotten that date if he lived to be a thousand, and he was already, by the only clock that counted, well on the way. He pinned it to the inside of the cupboard door anyway, with a drawing pin he had been saving for something worth pinning, at exactly the height of his eyes when he sat up in bed — because a thing on a door was a thing *said*, in a house where he had never once said anything, and he wanted there to be one sentence in Number Four that was his and that anyone who opened that door could read.

Nobody would open the door. That was rather the point. It was there anyway.

Then he lay down in the smallest office in England, five years and eight months old by the hall clock and well over a thousand by his own, and for the first time since a doorstep in November he was not waiting for anything.

He had a date. He had a thousand years. He was going to go and get ready.
