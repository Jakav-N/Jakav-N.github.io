# Chapter 5 — The Shelves

Mrs Prentice’s brother-in-law was called Dr Gerald Prentice, and he was exactly as tiresome as advertised, and he was so frightened of her that he had the meeting arranged inside nine days.

It was held in a room at the university in Egham that had a long table and a view of trees, and there were seven people at it, and Harry was the only one who had not been told what it was for, because nobody had thought a child of five needed telling. Dr Gerald Prentice sat at one end with the expression of a man who had been made to do something and wanted it known. A woman from the county called Mrs Achebe sat with a folder and a pen she clicked when she was thinking. Dr Prosser sat beside her with her chain glasses and said nothing for the first twenty minutes and watched Harry the whole time. Mrs Bell had come, in her good coat. Uncle Vernon had worn the funeral suit and sat in it with his hands on his knees and the look of a man at his own investiture. Petunia sat beside him in her gloves and did not take them off.

And at the far end, with her feet on a stack of paper under the table where she thought nobody could see, sat a grey-haired woman in a jumper with a hole at the elbow, who had been introduced as Professor Carrow, who ran the mathematics, and who had the face of a good sharp knife kept for best and had not yet used it on anyone.

They talked about him for twenty minutes. He let them. He had set this table; the food on it was his; he could wait for the plates. Dr Gerald Prentice said that universities did not take children. Mrs Achebe said that the county had a referral from its most senior psychologist and clicked her pen. Dr Prosser said, when asked, that she stood by every word of it and would say so to anyone, and clicked nothing. Vernon said the family had always encouraged him. Petunia said nothing. Mrs Bell said that she had a drawer.

Professor Carrow said nothing at all until the twenty-first minute, and then she took her feet off the paper and said, to Harry, directly, across the whole length of the table, in a Yorkshire voice that gave nothing away:

“You. What have you got.”

It was not a question. He had been waiting for it since Mrs Prentice had said *mathematics* on a date-due slip.

“Forty pages,” he said. “In English. I wrote them in the last fortnight; before that it was only in my head. It’s the beginning of what I worked out under the stairs from my cousin’s old textbook, done properly so that somebody slow could follow it. I’m sorry it’s only forty. My hand is slow and I didn’t know how long I’d have.”

He took them out of his bag and walked the length of the table and put them in front of her, and walked back, and sat down, and Vernon made a small noise that was the beginning of *now then, boy* and stopped, because Professor Carrow had opened the first page and begun to read, and something about the way she read made even Vernon Dursley fall silent.

She read for eleven minutes.

Nobody at the table spoke. Mrs Achebe clicked her pen twice and then, catching Dr Prosser’s eye, put it down. Dr Gerald Prentice looked at his watch and then at Carrow and then at the trees. And Harry sat with his hands in his lap and watched a grown woman read his handwriting with a pencil, turning pages at the speed of someone who was *checking*, and it was, he thought, the longest he had ever gone without being bored in a room where nothing was being said.

At the ninth page she stopped, and went back, and forward again, and wrote something in the margin.

At the end of the eleventh minute she closed the pages and took her glasses off and looked down the table at him, and the knife-face had not softened at all, and he was glad of it.

“Page nine is wrong,” she said.

The room went quite still. Harry felt it go through him like the morning of the ice — not the fall; the *gear*, the world going vast and slow and bright.

“Where?”

“Fourth line from the bottom. You’ve got the idea — it’s the right idea, it’s a famous idea, a German had it a hundred years ago and it caused a great deal of trouble — but you’ve let the thing you build have two names in your notation and you haven’t noticed, and so you’ve proved slightly more than you meant to, which here means you’ve proved nothing. It is a famous mistake. One man made it, in print, in 1893, and it took thirty years to dig the subject out from under him.” She put the glasses back on. “The other thirty-nine pages are correct as far as I can see at speed, and page thirty-one is not in any textbook I know, and I should like to think about it. But page nine is wrong, and you walked up this table and gave it me yourself, so I am telling you to your face. How does that feel?”

He had been waiting for someone to do that to him on purpose since before he could say *purpose*, and he sat at the end of a long table in Egham with seven adults looking at him and found he had to take a moment before he could answer, because the honest answer was absurd.

“It feels wonderful,” he said.

Professor Carrow looked at him for a long time. Then one corner of the knife-face did something it had plainly not done in a while.

“Yes,” she said. “It does, doesn’t it. Nobody ever believes me about that.” She turned to the rest of the table, and the knife came out at last, and it was for them. “Gerald. Mrs Achebe. Whoever else is deciding things. I don’t care what this university does or does not take. A child of five has handed me forty pages that I would be pleased to get from a second-year, with one mistake in them that I would be pleased to find in a *third*-year, and he thanked me for finding it. I want him in my library. I want him on Thursdays in my office. I want to know what is on page thirty-one. Write down whatever you have to write down to make that happen and I will sign it, and if there is no form, Gerald, *make one*.”

Dr Gerald Prentice opened his mouth.

“Edith said you would say universities do not take children,” said Carrow. “Edith was right. They don’t. They are about to.”

The card was ready in a week, and Harry walked across the park in the rain with it in his pocket to a building with a copper roof gone green, and a woman at a desk read the note that came with it and looked at him and read it again and said, “Well, I never,” and let him in.

He stopped on the threshold. He always did. This was the one all the others had been practice for.

Six floors. A hundred and twenty thousand volumes. And the journals — rows of them, bound by the year, going back to before his grandparents were born, the whole slow conversation of a century shelved in order and waiting. He stood on the ground floor with the card in his hand and the rain on the copper roof and felt a mistake he had been making his whole life come loose and fall away.

He had thought the hunger was *him*. Two hundred to one, and no engine runs that fast on a water glass; he had taken the hunger for a fact about his own machinery, like the slow hands, like the glasses, a thing he would carry to the end.

It wasn’t. It had only ever been the *shelves*. He stood in a building with enough on its shelves to feed even him, and the hunger that had sounded like the hall clock for four years went quiet, all at once, as if someone had reached into the hall and stopped the pendulum with a finger, and in the silence where it had been he could hear the rain, and the lift, and his own breath, and nothing else at all.

Then he went and found the mathematics, because he had promised Carrow page thirty-one by Thursday, and he was five, and he had the afternoon.

---

Getting there was the problem.

Egham was eleven miles up the line. A child of five could not take a train alone, whatever the child could read; Petunia had said so at the long table in a voice that had surprised the whole room by existing, and had been right. Vernon had the car and worked. Mrs Prentice had the branch. The county had a form for everything except this. And so for the first fortnight it was Petunia herself, on the train, twenty-two minutes each way, sitting opposite him in her good coat not speaking, walking him up through the park to the building with the copper roof and standing at the bottom of the steps and saying, “Well. Go on, then,” and not coming in, and going back down to the station to wait, with a magazine, for four hours, every day — and on the ninth day he came out at five and found her on the bench by the lake with the magazine shut on her knee, looking at the water, and understood that she had not read a page of it in nine days, and that this could not go on.

He said so to Mrs Figg, in the garden, on the Saturday.

“She sits by the lake. Four hours. She doesn’t read. I think she’s—” he had the word and did not like it “—I think she’s *waiting*, Mrs Figg. The way you wait outside a place you can’t go into.”

Mrs Figg snipped a stem.

“Well,” she said. “That won’t do.” And then, after another stem: “I’ve a bus pass, dear. And a great many cats who can mind themselves till teatime. And I was asked, a long time ago, by a man I write to, to keep an eye on you, and I don’t see that it matters which town I do it in.” She dropped the stem in the trug. “Monday. Half past eight. I shall bring a book. I shan’t come in; I can’t see the half of what’s in there, I don’t suppose, and I shouldn’t want to. But I can sit in a café with a pot of tea as well as anyone in Surrey, and I’ve been meaning to catch up on my reading.”

So it was Mrs Figg. Monday to Friday, from the second week of May, on the eight thirty-one, in a mackintosh with a library romance in the pocket, sitting in the university café from nine until five with a pot of tea and a scone and a succession of novels with castles on the covers, while two floors up a boy of five read a hundred and twenty thousand books at the speed of his eyes. She never asked what he had read. He told her anyway, on the train home, some days, and she listened to all of it, and said “Well I never” at the end.

Petunia did not say thank you to Mrs Figg. It was not a thing the house permitted. But on the first Monday that Harry went with Mrs Figg instead, there was a tin of biscuits on Mrs Figg’s doorstep when she got home at six — the good kind, from the shop in town, not the Edinburgh ones — with no note, and Mrs Figg looked at it for a long moment and took it in and said, to Tibbles, “Well. There’s hope for us all.”

---

He read the university that summer the way he had read the branch, in order, at eye-speed, with a map.

Not all of it. Even he could not read a hundred and twenty thousand books in a summer; the eyes were the eyes, and a thick book was still an hour, and a summer was still a summer. But he read the *shape* of it in a week — every floor, every bay, every run of spines — and then he went at it the way he went at everything, which was by refusing to gorge. Mathematics first, because he owed Carrow and because it was the one subject where he could carry his own water: three thousand years of it, shelved, and he had rebuilt perhaps the first two from one textbook and found, walking the shelves, where he had been right, and where the Germans had got there first with better names, and where — this happened four times that summer, and each time he stood between the stacks and simply breathed — he had got somewhere nobody on the shelves had been, and went back to the cupboard that night and put it into English for Carrow, slowly, at the speed of his hand, because a result nobody else could read was not yet a result.

Then the physics, because it was next door and because he had been wondering since the pendulum. Then chemistry, which he had dismissed at five as cookery and discovered was a language. Then — because Carrow had said, on the second Thursday, *you cannot be a mathematician and nothing else; it is a disease; I have it* — history, and the beginning of the philosophy, and a shelf of linguistics that explained in two afternoons a thing about English he had been working at since he was two.

He kept the hours of the building, nine till five, and the hours were, by the only clock that counted, about two years a week. He did not fall behind. He did not know how to fall behind. He sat in a carrel on the fourth floor with a stack beside him and the rain or the sun on the copper roof and read, and understood, and kept — every page, every word, for good; the shelves inside him had never once failed to hold — and at five he went down to the café and found a woman in a mackintosh with a castle on her lap, and they caught the five fourteen, and he told her about the Persians, or the Krebs cycle, or a man called Gödel who had proved that you could not prove everything, and she said *well I never*, and they were home for tea.

He was five years old and ten months. By midsummer he had read, by his own count, the whole of the mathematics on the shelves that he could follow without a teacher, which was most of it, and the whole of the undergraduate physics, and had started on the journals, and had written two hundred and eleven pages in English for a reader who was slow, and had been wrong, by Carrow’s count, nine times, and had loved every one.

“You are not,” said Carrow, on a Thursday in July, with page thirty-one in front of her for the sixth time and the window open on the lake, “going to tell me how you do this.”

“No, Professor.”

“Not the mathematics. Any fool with a mind like yours could do the mathematics. The *reading*. I had the library check. You have taken out four hundred and six books since May. Six a day. You cannot read six books a day. Nobody can read six books a day.”

“I read quickly,” said Harry. “My eyes are the slow part.”

Carrow looked at him over the glasses for a long moment, and he watched her decide — the way he had watched Dr Prosser decide over the blocks, the way he would watch a great many adults decide over the years — to take the answer she had been given and stand a little to one side of the door she had found behind it.

“Your eyes are the slow part,” she repeated. “Hm.” She pulled a pad towards her. “Then let’s see what the fast part makes of this. I’ve been stuck on it since 1983 and I’m sick of the sight of it. It’s about colouring. Don’t let that fool you.”

---

The boxes came from the spare room in June, and the margins in them were a dead man’s, and he read the margins before the text, and kept doing it for years.

Mrs Prentice brought them to the branch on a Thursday — four cardboard boxes gone soft at the corners, and she let him do the string, which he always suspected her of knowing was the best part. Inside were the Open University course units: slim pale books, dozens of them, with course codes on the spines and the dates of the broadcasts pencilled inside each cover in a hand that was not hers. Mathematics. Science. The arts foundation, which turned out to have the Renaissance in it and a unit on Beethoven he read on the train. And in the margins, in the same pencil hand, Mr Prentice.

*Surely not* — on page forty of a unit about infinite sets. He had been right; the book had been careless. *Lovely* — beside a proof Harry had found himself at four and never had anyone to show. *Ask R.* — he never found out who R was. *Enough for one night*, at the end of a hard chapter, with the date, and the next date three days later, and Harry sat in the returns cupboard with the pale book open and understood that he was reading a man learning, slowly, at one-to-one, in the evenings, after work, for the pleasure of it, nine years dead, and that the man had been good at it, and had stopped at the hard bits and come back to them, and had never once written *of course*.

“He watched them on the box,” said Mrs Prentice, from the desk, not looking round. “Nights. BBC2, after one. He said the maths ones were the only thing on the television worth losing sleep for.” A pause, and the stamp came down on a book. “The listings are in the Radio Times, dear. If you were ever awake at that hour. I shouldn’t think a child would be.”

He was awake at that hour. He had been awake at that hour, most nights, since he was three, in the smallest office in England, with nothing to do but the country of one; and from the second week of June he was awake at that hour on the lounge carpet at Number Four, eighteen inches from the screen, with the sound at two and the Radio Times open on his knee, watching men in brown jackets write on boards with squeaking pens in studios that looked like the waiting rooms of provincial dentists.

He loved them past all reason. For four years every voice he had learned from had been on a page — fixed, finished, certain. Here were people *teaching*. Thinking aloud, going back, saying *in fact let’s do that again more carefully*, hesitating before a hard step in a way that told you where the hard steps were. The first time a man in a brown jacket got to a place Harry had got to alone under the stairs and *went a different way* — a shorter way, through a wall Harry had spent a subjective month going round — he sat on the carpet and laughed out loud in the dark, and clapped his hand over his mouth, and listened to the house for a full minute, and laughed again more quietly, because it had been such a good wall.

He was wrong twice that summer, against the men in brown jackets. Twice they taught a thing he had derived alone and taught it differently, and he sat there certain in the dark that they had slipped, and worked it through, and found they hadn’t, and that he had, years ago, in a place he had never gone back to check because nobody had ever been there to make him. He went back and fixed everything downstream of both, and kept the two mistakes where he could see them, with something close to affection, and thought, lying on the carpet after the second one: *Three times in one summer. Carrow once, and now these two. For four years there was nobody to catch me, and I begin to think that was a kind of poverty.*

Mrs Prentice never asked whether he watched them. But in July she brought in a new Radio Times, unasked, every Thursday, with the Open University slots ringed in pencil in the hand he knew from the margins, which she had plainly been practising, and left it on the returns desk where he would find it, and said nothing, and that was Mrs Prentice.

---

Mr Tibbles stopped doing the stairs in June, and Harry noticed before Mrs Figg said anything, because he had been watching that cat with professional attention for two and a half years.

Tibbles had a spot on the landing sill — first sun in the morning — and all through the first week of the month the spot stayed empty, and the old cat lay instead on the folded blanket by the kitchen radiator and watched the doorway as if the stairs had personally let him down.

“He’s nine, is Tibbles,” said Mrs Figg, on the Saturday, setting down the tea. “Ten in March. It’s his hips, the vet says. There’s a tablet, but he won’t have it in sardines, he won’t have it in butter, he knows it’s there, don’t you, you old fool.” She said it to the cat, fondly, and the cat ignored her with the whole of his back. “Old cats get stiff. There’s nothing for it really. You just keep them warm and don’t move the furniture.”

She went out to see to the kettle. The rain ticked on the window — it was a wet June, the kind the roses hated — and Harry sat down on the floor by the radiator, which he was allowed to do, and Mr Tibbles looked at him with his lecture-hall face, and Harry looked back, and what he felt, watching the old cat decide whether getting comfortable again was worth the trouble of moving, was not curiosity.

He had been trying, since the week in the cupboard, to make the warming come on purpose. Three months, every day, with the hypothesis — *it runs when I am not looking* — and a dozen experiments built round it. He had tried not looking. He had gone lucid in his own sleep, night after night, and waited there for whatever had grown his hair to show itself, and it had shown him nothing but dreams, which were liars. He had wanted, hard, with his whole mind, at cold tea and wilting cress and the latch of the cupboard door, and nothing had come, not once, not a degree of warmth. Three months, which was fifty years of trying, and the hypothesis was wrong in some way he could not see.

He was not trying now. That was the whole of it. He had three years of this cat — three years of being watched from the armchair like a colleague, of Tibbles sitting on his feet for a whole visit the winter he was five on a day that had been bad — and the cat was old in his hips and would not take the tablet, and there was nothing for it really, and Harry sat on the kitchen floor in the rain and was *sorry*, properly, the way you are sorry about a thing you cannot fix.

He put his hand on the cat’s side, in the deep fur over the ribs, which was permitted on quiet days.

And it came.

Quietly. No fear, no falling, none of the bell-struck hugeness of the doorstep. It rose up through him the way the warmth had risen through the blankets that first night, patient and friendly and completely unhurried, down his arm and out through his palm into the old cat’s side as if it had been waiting three months — three years — for somebody to ask it *properly*. He felt it go looking. That was the only way he could ever say it afterwards: it went looking, the way Mrs Prentice went looking along a shelf, hand out, sure of the general area. It found the hips. Something there was the wrong shape of warm, and it made it the right shape, gently, taking its time, and then it sat back, so to speak, and was done, and the rain went on ticking.

Mr Tibbles raised his head. He looked at his own back legs for a moment with an expression of deep suspicion. Then he stood up, stretched himself the whole length of the blanket, back feet trembling with the luxury of it, stepped off, crossed the kitchen, and went up the stairs like a kitten, straight to the landing sill, where there was no sun at all because it was raining, and sat in the grey light looking extremely pleased with himself about nothing.

Mrs Figg came in with the pot and stopped.

The kitchen was very quiet. Harry stayed where he was, on the floor by the radiator, hand still warm, heart going, and for once he did not arrange his face at all, because there was no longer anyone in that house he needed to arrange it for.

“He just got up,” said Harry.

Mrs Figg looked at the cat on the landing for a long time. Then she looked at Harry, over the top of her glasses, and there passed over her face a thing he would come to know well in the years after and had no file for yet: surprise, and arithmetic, and underneath the two of them, unmistakably, like the radiator under the blanket, gladness.

“So he did,” she said. “Well. There’s hope for us all.” She poured the tea. Her hand had a small shake in it that was gone by the second cup. “You’ll want to remember that, dear. I expect it’s important.”

“I don’t forget things, Mrs Figg,” said Harry. “But yes. I think it’s the most important thing I’ve got.”

---

On the train home, with Mrs Figg beside him reading a castle, he put it into words. Not on paper; there was nobody yet to hand paper to. In English, in his head, in the shape he would use when there was somebody, because it was the most important thing he had and he wanted it in a form that could be given away.

*It does not come for wanting. I wanted for fifty years and it never came once. It does not come for watching or not watching; the hypothesis was wrong, or it was the wrong hypothesis. It came today for a thing that was genuinely wrong, that I genuinely minded about, with my hand already out and nothing in my head but sorry. It answers* caring. *Not the word. The thing.*

*The hair was my need. The doorstep was my need. The cat was his. It does not seem to mind whose. It only seems to mind that it’s real.*

*So the question of my life is not “how do I make it come.” That is a two-year-old’s question, like the rule. The question is: what, in the world, is genuinely wrong, that I can genuinely mind about, with my hand out — and how many of those things are there, and how fast can I find them.*

*I think there are a great many. I think I have a thousand years. I think I had better start.*

Mrs Figg turned a page of her castle. Outside the window, Surrey went by at the speed it always went by, which was slowly, and he sat with his hands in his lap and the shelves full and the hunger quiet and a cat on a landing sill eleven miles behind him, and was five years and eleven months old, and well over a thousand, and happy.

“Well I never,” said Mrs Figg, to her book.

“Yes,” said Harry. “That’s about right.”
