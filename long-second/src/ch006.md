# Chapter 6 — Page 141

He got his mother’s name off Aunt Marge, down the telephone, on Boxing Day.

He hadn’t been listening for it. Marge rang every Boxing Day at four, after the Queen and before her dinner, and the calls ran forty minutes, of which Vernon contributed perhaps six words a minute, so the content came through the hall quite clearly for anyone drying up with the kitchen door open. Mostly it was dogs. Then it was neighbours, and the state of the country, and then — Vernon must have mentioned the school letter, the enrichment, because his contribution changed to a defensive rumble — the volume on the other end went up a register, and the word came down the line and through the door and into Harry’s year like a coin dropped in a well.

Clever, is he. Well, there you are. The Evans girl was clever. Our mother said it from the start, Vernon, you can’t breed it out, it’s in them like the hip trouble in spaniels. You watch that boy.

The Evans girl.

He stood with the tea towel in his hands and a serving dish half dried, and the hall clock ticked on, and inside, in the long quiet, he took the two words and set them where he could see them.

Evans. His mother had been Evans before she was Potter. Petunia had been Evans too, then — the sisters Evans, of wherever it was, before one of them went to a world with a different set of rules and one of them went to Privet Drive. All this time the name had been sitting four feet from his cupboard in Marge’s address book, and in the end nobody had told him; it had arrived as livestock commentary, between the dogs and the dinner, from a woman who had never once got his own first name right.

The Evans girl was clever. It wasn’t much. From Marge, it was practically an heirloom. He dried the dish, and put it away, and said it over to himself a few times, in the private alphabet: *Lily Evans was clever. People said so where she came from.*

What the name was for, he couldn’t have said. It wasn’t for anything. He took it out and looked at it, some nights, the way you’d look at the one photograph, if you had one.

The blue book came three weeks later, off the bottom shelf of the 510s, with the lettering worn off the spine, and it had nothing to do with any of this, until it did.

The book was called *The Pleasures of Number*, by E. M. Hartley, and it had been written to be read, which put it ahead of most of the shelf. Whoever E. M. Hartley was, he wrote like a man showing you round a town he’d lived in all his life — here’s a good corner, stand just here, now look. The chapters went at walking pace with their hands in their pockets. It was the first maths book Harry had found that was in no hurry at all, and he read it twice in an afternoon, which for him was the respectful speed, and then a third time slowly, which was the affectionate one.

The trouble was on page 141.

Chapter nine was about the primes, and partway along, discussing how they thin out but never run dry, Hartley remarked — in passing, the way you’d mention the time of the last bus — that of course every even number past two can be written as two primes added together, and went on his way to the thing he was actually saying.

Harry stopped at *of course*.

He spent the walk home on it, and then the evening, and then, in the slow hours, longer than he liked to add up. Sixteen, that was easy, three and thirteen, five and eleven. Ninety-eight. Every even number he tried would split; that was the maddening thing, they split every time, like conkers. But trying numbers wasn’t a proof, every book on that shelf said so and he believed them, and when he went looking for the *reason* — the thing underneath that would make it have to be true, the way things in the other chapters had to be true — there was nothing to stand on. He built and discarded through most of a weekend, in the bus-shelter sort of weather where the house left you alone. Nothing held. Every road in went grand and came out marsh.

Which meant one of two things. Either there was a proof, known, presumably wonderful, sitting in some book the branch didn’t have — or E. M. Hartley’s *of course* was carrying more than it could lift.

On Tuesday he took the problem to the returns desk, because that was the procedure now for things the shelf couldn’t answer.

“Hartley,” said Mrs Prentice, turning the blue book over. “We’ve had him out four times in ten years, and twice was you.” She read page 141 with the expression she kept for people returning books damp. “Mm. Well. If his of course has a bottom to it, the county will have it on a shelf somewhere. Leave it with me.”

The county took eleven days. The parcel, when it came, was wrapped in brown paper and string like something off the ration, and Mrs Prentice put it on the reading table at the quiet end of a Thursday and let him do the string himself, which he always suspected her of knowing was the best part.

Two books and a pamphlet. He went through them the way he went through everything, fast first and then slow, and the answer was in the second book, in a chapter on problems, and the answer was this.

Nobody knew.

It had a name. *Goldbach’s conjecture*, after a man who had wondered about it in seventeen forty-two, in a letter to Euler — Euler! — and Euler, the walking lighthouse of that whole century, the man who had answers the way other men had opinions, had written back, in effect: it must surely be true, and I cannot prove it either. And then the really enormous thing, the thing Harry read three times to be sure the sentence said what it said: *neither had anyone since.* Two hundred and forty-six years. Every mathematician alive had grown up knowing about it the way you know about a mountain in the next county. The greatest of them had walked out into that same marsh he’d spent his weekend in, with better boots and better maps, generations of them, and every road in still went grand and came out wet, and the conjecture sat there, checked true for every even number anyone had the patience to try, proven for none of them, patient as weather.

E. M. Hartley’s *of course* wasn’t hiding a wonderful proof after all. It was a man waving cheerfully across the marsh at a mountain nobody had climbed, saying isn’t it lovely, and forgetting to mention that fact, which, in a book as honest as his, Harry chose to read as affection rather than carelessness. You could love a thing so well you forgot it wasn’t finished.

He sat at the reading table with the county’s books open in front of him and the radiator ticking, and something rearranged itself in him, quietly, for good.

He had spent four years thinking of knowledge as a city already built, and himself as a boy learning the streets of it. The Survey had been a map of a finished place; the game had been to walk all of it. That picture was wrong, and he’d just seen where it broke. The city had an edge. Not an edge like a wall — an edge like the edge of a town, where the pavement gives out and the last lamp post stands at the start of open country, and some of the lanes just stop in fields. Sixteen can be made of three and thirteen; any child can check it; no one on earth knows *why it keeps working.* The field began that close. You could reach the last lamp post of all human knowing from a branch library in Surrey, between the end of school and the shepherd’s-pie smell of teatime, at the age of seven and a half.

And out there — this was the part that kept him awake that night, lying under the stairs with his hands behind his head — out there, people were working. Right now, this year, in ordinary buildings, grown men and women were out past the lamp posts in the wet grass with their trouser legs rolled, pushing the pavement a flagstone at a time. The books had names in them of people who were *alive*. Knowledge wasn’t a city he’d inherited. It was a town still going up, short-handed, with the scaffolding out and the edges all raw.

Short-handed. He put that in the notebook, and underlined nothing, because he was seven and a half, and even in the private alphabet some things were better left looking casual: *there is room*.

The times tables saved Dudley in March, which is a sentence nobody at St Gregory’s would have believed, whichever way round you read it.

Year Three did tables tests on Friday mornings, out loud, down the rows, and Dudley — who could do things with a football that Harry privately rated far above anything on page 141, but for whom seven eights sat in the mind like a wasp in a jar — had come home three Fridays running the colour of putty. There was to be a grand one at the end of term, up to twelves, in front of the whole class, and Dudley had got it into his head that he was going to be made to stand up and fail it out loud, and the dread of it had taken the stuffing out of him so thoroughly that he had gone off his food, which at Number Four was treated as a national emergency.

Harry watched it build for a fortnight and found, to his mild surprise, that he minded.

He examined the minding, that being the habit of his life, and it held up. It wasn’t strategy. There was nothing in it for him; a frightened Dudley was, if anything, a quieter Dudley, better for business all round. He just didn’t like watching it. Dudley wasn’t clever and never would be, and had spent years making Harry’s life a low grey misery over exactly that gap between them, and here he was all the same, nine years old, sick with fear over seven eights, and the fear was real, and Harry had two hundred times as long as anyone else to sit and feel how real it was. Knowing a thing is small doesn’t make it light to the one carrying it. That went in the notebook too.

So he fixed it, and the fixing taught him more than the sum did.

He couldn’t teach Dudley. Teaching Dudley was like Petunia and the tablet in the sardines — the moment the lesson showed its face, Dudley clamped shut and bolted. The front door was barred. But Harry had spent his whole life getting into rooms by other doors, and he knew by now that Dudley didn’t learn through the front of his head at all; Dudley learned the way he learned goal nets and the batting order and the names of all forty-odd men who had ever played for his team — through the side, through chant and rhythm and not-noticing-you-were-doing-it.

It took him three weeks of small, deniable, sideways work. A football rhyme, invented and left lying about, that happened to carry the sixes in its bounce. The eight-times as a thing you banged out on the stairs with a tennis ball, two steps to a line, which Harry started doing himself, pointedly, until Dudley took it up to prove he could do it louder. A long car journey to Marge’s turned, by Harry asking Vernon innocent questions about mileage and petrol, into forty minutes of Vernon doing sums aloud and getting them wrong and Dudley, delighted, correcting him. None of it looked like teaching. None of it had Harry’s fingerprints anywhere near it. By the end Dudley thought he’d simply stopped being frightened of his own accord, the way the weather clears, and that was exactly the shape Harry had built it to have, because a confidence you think you found yourself is worth ten that somebody handed you.

Dudley stood up at the end of term and got to twelve twelves with only one wobble, and came home two feet taller than he’d left, and told the whole table that tables were easy, really, you just had to not be thick about it, and Vernon beamed, and Petunia did a steak-and-kidney to mark the occasion.

Harry ate his share and said nothing and kept his eyes on his plate, and underneath the table, in the country of one, he turned the whole operation over and found it good, and found also the thing it had taught him, which was bigger than Dudley and bigger than tables and which he would spend the rest of his life proving out:

You almost never move a person by the front door. You find the door they already use. Dudley through football. Petunia through the garden and the neighbours. Vernon through being right. Mrs Prentice through a held-open door, and Mrs Figg through a mended cat. Everyone he had ever managed, he had managed sideways, through the thing they already loved, and it had never once failed, and the one time in his life he’d gone through the front door — *behind the cooker, Aunt Petunia, Dudley posted it there this morning* — he’d frightened a woman into years of cold.

The thing in him answered caring, and people answered the same way. It was the same law twice, he thought, lying under the stairs that night with the house ticking and a good dinner in him for once. You couldn’t make either one come by force. You went to where it already lived, and you asked properly, and you were patient, and then — cat’s hips, boy’s tables, warmth in a blanket on a cold step — it came.

He was seven and a half, or fifteen hundred, depending on how you counted, and he had reached the edge of the map and found people still working there, and taught his cousin the twelve-times through a tennis ball, and neither thing felt, that night, like the more important one. They felt like the same thing. He wasn’t sure yet what the thing was called. He had a feeling the word for it was out there past the last lamp post with everything else worth finding, and that he had, as usual, plenty of time to walk out and get it.
