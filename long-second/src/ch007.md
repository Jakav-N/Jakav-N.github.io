# Chapter 7 — The Audit

The newspaper that changed his life was Vernon’s, and Vernon was using it to hold up the kitchen wall, and the article was on the back of the page he was reading, facing out, so that for the length of a Sunday breakfast Harry had nothing to look at but it.

It was a feature. A photograph of a girl, thirteen or so, in a gown too big for her, on some college steps, with a man who must be her father standing a little behind with his hand on her shoulder. She had gone up to Oxford at twelve. She had a degree now, a first, the paper said, the youngest ever. She had been taught at home. Her father had given up his job to do it. Tutors had been found, exam boards had bent, a whole university had opened its doors, and the paper had sent a photographer.

Harry ate his toast and read it four times, which took him a very long while, and the kitchen clock went on ticking over his head.

Then Vernon turned the page, and the girl was gone, and Harry sat looking at the back of a report about the Chancellor and felt something he had not felt in years. Not since the playpen. Not since the long afternoons at two hundred to one with nothing to count but his own heart.

He was angry.

It was not at the girl. He was glad for the girl; he hoped she was happy; he hoped the gown fitted better now. It was not even, exactly, at the Dursleys, who were what they were and could no more have given up a job to teach him than the hall clock could have. It was at himself, and it came up through him slow and hot, like the warmth did, except that this answered no one and mended nothing.

A child had sat at a kitchen table somewhere in the north of England and been *seen*. And the world — the whole ordinary muggle world, the same world that stopped at the garden gate of Number Four — had done what it does for children it can see: it had moved. Tutors. Exams. Oxford. A photographer. All of that was available. It had been available the entire time. It was sitting out there like a library with the lights on, and he had spent six years and twelve hundred of his own kind of years in a cupboard, being *small*, by choice, by policy, by a rule he had passed at the age of two and never once put up for review.

“More tea,” said Uncle Vernon, to the room.

Harry got up and poured it. His hands were perfectly steady. Inside, in the long quiet, a government that had not sat in emergency session since the night of the doorstep was being called to order.

---

He did it that night, properly, and it took him the whole night, which was most of a year.

He didn’t sleep. He hadn’t needed to decide that; he simply lay down under the stairs with the light off and the house ticking and found he wasn’t going to. He got the notebooks out instead, all of them, the whole run back to the shoebox, and laid them open along the camp bed and the floor in the order they’d been written, and then he lay on his side in the dark with his eyes shut, because he didn’t need to read them, he only needed them *there*, and he went back through his own life the way you’d go back through a set of accounts you had kept honestly and never once added up.

The rule had been right. He made himself say that first, because it was true and because he was going to be hard on himself in a minute and wanted the hardness to land on the right place. *Be small.* A boy of two, in that kitchen, in front of that face at the cooker: there had been nothing else to do. The rule had kept him out from under the sash window for six years. It had got him the cupboard, the library, Tuesdays and Thursdays. He didn’t regret a day of it up to about the age of five.

And then the rule had gone on running, like the cuckoo’s parents, long after it was wrong.

He went through the costs, line by line, because he had never allowed himself to. Two years of three books a week when the whole building was his for the asking and Mrs Prentice would have held the door from the start if he’d only *walked up to it*. The Bright Little Boy, the odd clever child, the calibrated errors — all that engineering, all those sentences drilled for weeks, spent on being *less*, when the school had wanted, had been begging in its clumsy way, to give him more. Dr Prosser, who had looked at him across a table of wooden blocks and all but said it out loud: *the swans, you know, whatever water it is they’re waiting for* — and he had thanked her, politely, and gone back to his pond. The county had a whole machinery for children like the girl in the gown. He had hidden from it for two years on purpose. He had hidden from the one thing in Little Whinging built to help him, out of a habit he’d picked up in a playpen.

Six years. Twelve hundred of his own. The lost input alone — he tried to count it and stopped, because the number was obscene and the counting was a way of not deciding.

So he decided.

Leaving first, because it had to be looked at squarely before it could be put down. He looked at it. He knew what happened to children who left; the nine o’clock news had taught him about children’s homes, years ago, in that grave voice. Fewer books than here. Less time alone than here. Grown-ups whose whole job was to watch you, and no door that shut. A boy who ran from Privet Drive would land in a place that was Privet Drive with the library taken out and the watching turned up, and he would be *right*, morally, and also an idiot. He put it down. Not because he couldn’t bear the house. Because the house, for all its weather, was the best position he could hold this year, and a good general does not abandon a defensible position for a worse one to make a point.

But he was not going to *stay* either. Not the way he had been staying. He was going to do the thing the girl’s father had done for her, except that nobody was going to do it for him, so he would do it for himself: he was going to make the world *see*, and then stand back and let it move.

Everything in him that had run on the old rule flinched at that, and he let it flinch, and then he reasoned with it, the way he’d reasoned with Dudley’s tables, through the side door.

The fear in this house had one owner and one address. Petunia, in the kitchen, in front of the drawer that would not open. That was real, and it would stay real, and he would honour it: he would never again do in this house what he had done at two. But school was not this house. The county was not this house. The library, the exam boards, the newspapers, the universities with their lights on — none of them had a drawer with his mother in it. In every room but one, *small* bought him nothing. He had been paying for a whole town’s worth of insurance against one woman’s fear.

He would be small in one room. Everywhere else, he would be exactly as large as he was, and let them deal with it.

It took him the rest of the night to build the plan, and the plan was the first thing he had ever built for the real size of himself, and it had five parts, and he wrote them in English, not the private alphabet, at the back of the newest notebook, because for the first time in his life he was writing something that other people were going to have to read.

*One. Stop hiding at school. Ask to be tested, properly, by the county. Say yes to everything they offer. The girl in the gown had tutors and a university; those exist; they are for children they can see; be seen.*

*Two. Make it Uncle Vernon’s idea. He wants a nephew to boast about more than he wants a nephew to be ordinary, if the boasting is loud enough. Feed the boasting. The school and Mrs Prentice do the talking; I say nothing at home but yes, Uncle Vernon.*

*Three. Aunt Petunia gets the truth, once, in one sentence, and then I never frighten her again. “Being good at school isn’t the other thing.” She knows what the other thing is. She will not quite be able to argue.*

*Four. The other thing. Every day. Not once a week when a cat is poorly — every day, like a scale on a piano. There is always something that needs mending if you look. Look. It gets easier each time; the hinge is loosening; find out how far it goes. Write down the shape of it. I am the only one who can see the shape and I have been treating that like a hobby.*

*Five. Stop pretending the library is enough. It never was. The Radio Times lists the Open University on BBC2 at night. Those are university lectures. For free. In the lounge. I can be awake at one in the morning more easily than anyone alive.*

Under that he wrote one more line and then sat looking at it a long time, because it was the real one, and the five above it were only the ways of getting there.

*There is room. Mrs P said so and the county’s books said so and I have twelve hundred years of nothing to show for knowing it. People are out past the lamp posts working, short-handed, and I have been in here counting spiders. Enough.*

He closed the notebook. The light under the stairs door had gone from black to grey. The boiler whooshed at seven, as it always did, and he lay and listened to it with the strange, light, scoured feeling of a man who has just discovered he was a good deal richer than he thought and a good deal more foolish, both at once, and has decided to forgive the second for the sake of the first.

He got up and put the notebooks away and went to lay the breakfast table, and when Petunia came down she found the cutlery straight and the kettle on and the boy already dressed, looking out the kitchen window at the garden with an expression she couldn’t read and didn’t try to.

“You’re up,” she said.

“Yes, Aunt Petunia,” said Harry.

He had never in his life meant two words more.

---

Mrs Prentice heard the plan on the Monday, after four, with the stepladder between them like a witness.

He had rehearsed none of it. That was itself part of the plan — he had worked out, somewhere around three in the morning, that rehearsing was a thing you did to be small *well*, and he was done being small well. So he simply told her: the girl in the gown, the county, the lamp posts, the five parts. It took him eleven minutes at the speed his mouth went, which was the longest he had ever spoken to anyone about anything, and partway through he realised she had stopped shelving and was sitting on the second step of the ladder with her hands in her cardigan pockets, listening the way she listened to nothing else in that building.

When he finished she didn’t say anything for a while.

“Well,” she said at last. “What took you so long?”

He had expected a dozen things from her and not that, and it went into him clean and sat there, and he found, to his surprise, that he was smiling.

“I was two,” said Harry. “When I made the rule.”

“Yes. I did gather it was something of that order.” She got up off the step and dusted her skirt, which didn’t need it. “I’ll ring Mrs Bell. I know her from the churchyard committee; she’ll take it better from me than from a seven-year-old with a plan, no offence intended. The county will want to test you again — properly this time, I expect, with the big folder — and you will not, this time, forget the next bit halfway through. Will you.”

“No,” said Harry.

“Good.” She went to the desk and wrote something on a date-due slip, which was how she remembered things she intended to do that week. Then she looked up, over the glasses. “And the Open University.”

“I saw it in the Radio Times.”

“My husband watched them. Nights. He said the maths ones were the only thing on the box worth losing sleep for.” She said it in the filing voice, and then, in her own: “I’ve the course books still. Boxes of them. Nobody in this town’s asked for an Open University maths text in nine years, and I never could bring myself to send them to the sale.” A pause. “They’re in my spare room. They can be in the returns cupboard by Thursday.”

Nine years of boxes in a spare room, for no one, and now for him. He didn’t know what to say, which was rare, and said the thing closest to hand.

“Thank you. For the door.”

“Hm,” said Mrs Prentice, and squared the slip on the desk, and went back to the shelving as if nothing had happened, which was how he knew that something had.

---

Mrs Bell, the head, had a face like a closed library and the kindest heart in the building, and she had been keeping a drawer marked *Potter — no fuss* for two and a half years without once liking it.

She had him in on the Wednesday, which was fast, and she did it properly: door shut, tea for herself, orange squash for him, and the drawer open on the desk between them with Dr Prosser’s folder on top. He could read Prosser’s handwriting upside down and did, in the time it took Mrs Bell to sit, and it said more than it had said to him across the table of blocks. *Ceiling not reached. Possibly not reachable with available instruments.* And underneath, in a different ink, as if she’d come back to it: *He chose to stop. Watch for the day he chooses not to.*

“Mrs Prentice tells me,” said Mrs Bell, “that you’d like to be assessed again. By the county. Of your own accord.”

“Yes, Mrs Bell.”

“Last time, your aunt was quite clear that the family wanted no—”

“Fuss,” said Harry. “Yes. I know. I think I can help with that, if you’ll let me do it my way. My aunt is frightened of one particular thing, and this isn’t it, and I can tell her so in a way she’ll believe. And my uncle—” he chose the next words with care, because they were going to have to be true by Friday, “—my uncle will be delighted. He likes it when the family does well in public. If the school writes to him, not to her, and if the letter says something he can repeat to people, he’ll sign anything you put in front of him.”

Mrs Bell looked at him over her tea for a long moment, with the particular expression of a head teacher watching a seven-year-old explain the handling of his own parents.

“You’ve thought about this,” she said.

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

He considered lying, from old habit, and let the habit go past like a bus.

“Since I was two,” said Harry. “But I only decided on Sunday.”

Something happened at the corner of Mrs Bell’s mouth that would have been a smile on a less well-defended face. She closed Dr Prosser’s folder, and put it back in the drawer, and shut the drawer, and he understood that the thing marked *no fuss* had just been filed somewhere else.

“I shall write to your uncle,” she said. “Mr Dursley. On the good paper.”

---

The letter came on the Friday, and Harry made sure he was drying up when it was opened.

It was a very good letter. He never saw it, then or later, but he heard the whole of it in Vernon’s breathing. There was the first bit, where the breathing went suspicious, as at anything from the school. Then a long slow bit where it went careful, like a man reading small print. And then the part Mrs Bell had been asked to put in — the part about *exceptional*, and *a credit to the family*, and *the county’s most senior educational psychologist* — and the breathing stopped altogether for three whole seconds, which gave Harry the better part of a morning to stand at the sink and listen to his uncle discover a new idea in his own head.

“Petunia,” said Uncle Vernon.

She came in from the hall. He handed her the letter without a word, and she read it, and Harry watched the side of her face do the thing — the first white flicker, the hand going towards the collarbone — and he put down the tea towel, and said, to the plate he was holding, quietly, so that only she would hear it over Vernon’s breathing:

“It’s only school, Aunt Petunia. Being good at school isn’t the other thing. I promise you it isn’t. I won’t ever let it be.”

She didn’t answer. Her hand came down from her collarbone and smoothed the letter flat on the counter, twice, and she read it again, and what was in her face when she’d finished was still fear, but underneath the fear was something that had been under it all along, he saw now, the whole six years, and that he had never once given her a reason to show. She was *tired*. She had been frightened for six years of a thing she had never been able to say, in a house where nobody would have let her say it, and here was the boy at the sink telling her, in a sentence, that he knew exactly what it was and would keep it out of her kitchen.

“Well,” said Petunia, to the letter. And then, to Vernon, in her fence voice, the one for Mrs Next Door: “Marge will want to hear about this.”

Vernon had the telephone in his hand before she’d finished the sentence.

Harry picked up the tea towel and finished the plate. The whole exchange had taken forty seconds, which for him was a working day, and he spent the remainder of the washing-up quietly amazed, as he would be amazed at intervals for the rest of his life, at how little it had cost. One true sentence. Six years he’d spent being a convenient nothing to avoid one woman’s fear, and when he finally looked the fear in the face and told it the truth, it had smoothed a letter flat and asked Vernon to ring Marge.

The front door, he thought. Even with her. It had been there the whole time. He’d just never once tried the handle.

---

On the Saturday he went to Mrs Figg’s, and did the thing he had been putting off all week, because it was the first part of the plan that was for nobody but himself, and he had wanted to earn it.

It was raining again. Snowy and Mr Paws and Tufty lay about in the attitudes of cats who had never had a bad day, and Mr Tibbles sat on the arm of Mrs Figg’s chair, where he had taken to sitting since the stairs, and watched Harry with his lecture face, and Mrs Figg poured out, and he saw her hands.

He had seen them before. He had seen everything before; that was the whole trouble with him. But he had never *looked*, not the way he looked now, with the hinge loose and the plan open and the new rule running — *every day; find the thing; look*. Her knuckles were swollen. The joints of two fingers on the right hand went off at an angle, and she poured with the left, and he went back along six years of Saturdays in a quarter of a second and found she had always poured with the left, and he had never once asked himself why.

“Your hands look sore, Mrs Figg,” he said.

The teapot paused. “They’re old, dear. Old hands get sore. It’s the damp.”

“May I hold them? Just for a minute.”

He had never asked her for anything. He had never, he realised, asked anyone in that town for anything, except Mrs Prentice for books, and the asking stood in the warm cabbage-smelling room like a fourth cat.

Mrs Figg looked at him over her glasses for a long moment. Then she put the pot down, and sat, and held out both her hands across the little table, palms down, the way you’d show a child a trick, and he took them.

They were cold, and dry, and the knuckles under his thumbs were hot in the wrong way, the way the old cat’s hips had been. He didn’t try anything. He had stopped trying months ago. He only sat there in the rain-light holding an old woman’s hands and let himself mind — not about *a thing*, this time, a rose or a crack, but about *her*, the whole of her, six years of ten pence an hour and biscuits off her own plate and *nothing out of the ordinary, dear*, every month, in the same place, for someone who had never thanked her because he had been too busy being clever to notice there was anything to thank her for.

It came up through him like a tide.

Not the slow trickle of the roses. A tide — warm and sure and enormous, as if whatever it was had been waiting for him to mind about a *person* and had rather a lot to say on the subject. It went down his arms and into her hands and he felt it go looking, the way it did, hand out along a shelf, and find the knuckles, and find the two bent fingers, and consider them with great patience, and *undo* something, slowly, like a knot being worked loose by someone who had all day.

Mrs Figg made a small sound. Not pain. The other thing.

He let go. Her hands lay on the table between them. The knuckles were knuckles. The two fingers on the right hand lay straight beside the others, and she looked at them, and turned the hand over, and made a fist, and opened it, and made a fist again.

The rain ticked on the window. Mr Tibbles had stopped looking like a man at a lecture and started looking like a man who had seen the examiner handed the answers.

“Well,” said Mrs Figg, very quietly, to her own right hand.

“I’m sorry I didn’t notice before,” said Harry. “I should have. I notice everything. I just wasn’t looking.”

She looked up at him then, and it was the face from the kitchen the day of the cat — surprise, and arithmetic, and gladness — but with something added, something careful, like a woman closing a gate behind her to keep a thing in.

“Harry,” she said. It was the first time in six years she had used his name as the start of a sentence rather than the end of one. “You be careful who you show that to. You hear me? There are people who’d — well. You be careful. Not everybody who’d want to see it would be kind about it.”

“I know,” he said. “That’s why I showed you.”

Mrs Figg looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up the teapot, with her right hand, and poured, and her hand did not shake at all.

“Chocolate biscuit,” she said. “The good tin.”

---

That night, when the house was asleep, he went down to the lounge in his socks and turned the television on with the sound at two and sat on the carpet eighteen inches from the screen with the Radio Times open on his knee.

*1.05 a.m., BBC2. Open University: M203, Introduction to Pure Mathematics. Unit 7: Groups.*

A man in a brown jacket, in a studio that looked like a dentist’s waiting room, wrote on a board, and talked, and did not once look down at anyone, and Harry sat in the dark and listened to a lecturer explain a thing he had worked out for himself two winters ago under the stairs, and found that the man did it a different way, a better way, a way that opened a door he hadn’t known was in the wall, and sat perfectly still for twenty-five minutes with his heart going like a bird.

Then the programme ended, and there was a card saying what came next, and he wrote it down.

He turned the television off and went back under the stairs. Before he lay down he took the page he had torn from the Radio Times — the whole week’s listings, every Open University slot ringed in pencil, twenty-two of them — and pinned it to the inside of the cupboard door with a drawing pin he had been saving for something worth pinning, at exactly the height of his eyes when he sat up in bed.

Then he lay down, in the dark, with the house ticking, seven years and eight months old by the hall clock and fifteen hundred and some by his own, and for the first time in his life he was not waiting for anything.

He was going to go and get it.
