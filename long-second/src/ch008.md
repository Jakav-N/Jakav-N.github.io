# Chapter 8 — Fifteen Papers

The examination boards said no, and then they said no again in writing, and then Mrs Achebe clicked her pen in a particular way and they said that they would need to consult, and in April a man came down from London to consult, and Harry did the consulting in a room at Royal Holloway with a view of the lake while Professor Carrow sat in the corner with her feet on a stack of paper and did not help at all.

The man from London was called Mr Bellamy and he represented four boards at once, which Harry gathered was unusual, and he had a face that had been built for saying no to parents and had never before been asked to say it to a child of six and three-quarters who was reading his own regulations upside down across the table.

“The thing is,” said Mr Bellamy, for the third time, “there is no *lower* age limit, as such, because it has never been necessary to have one. The youngest candidate any of the boards has on record was eleven. We have no procedure for—”

“Then you don’t need one,” said Harry. “You need a candidate number and a centre, and the centre needs to agree to invigilate, and Mrs Bell has already said Stonewall will. The regulations say *any person*. I’ve read them. I’m a person.” He said it without any edge at all, because he had none; he liked Mr Bellamy, who was plainly trying. “Mr Bellamy, I’m not asking you to decide whether I can do them. The papers will decide that. I’m only asking you to let the papers try.”

Mr Bellamy looked at Mrs Achebe. Mrs Achebe clicked her pen twice.

“Fifteen,” said Mr Bellamy. “Ten at one level and five at the other. In one summer. At *six*.”

“Seven, by the time of the second week,” said Harry. “My birthday’s the thirty-first of July. And I’ve read the syllabuses. All of them. They’re not very long.”

From the corner, Professor Carrow made a small sound into her coffee that Harry had learned, over a year of Thursdays, was the nearest she came in company to a laugh.

Mr Bellamy went back to London. In May, a letter came to Mrs Bell on four different letterheads, and the letters all said the same thing in slightly different ways, and the thing they said was that the boards had no objection in principle, that this was without precedent, and that they would be obliged if the centre could confirm that the candidate was *able to hold a pen for the duration*.

Mrs Bell confirmed it. She did so, Harry was later told, in a single sentence, and the sentence was *He has been holding one longer than most of your examiners.*

---

The sports hall at Stonewall High smelled of floor polish and fear, and on the first Monday in June he walked into it with his two pens and his ruler and a hundred and forty sixteen-year-olds turned round in their chairs to look at him.

He had thought about this moment more than it deserved and had concluded there was nothing to be done about it but to walk in. So he walked in. The invigilator — a tall stooped man in a cardigan the colour of a wet pavement, with chalk on both elbows, who had plainly been told and had plainly not believed it until now — checked his candidate number three times, and then went and fetched the deputy head to check it a fourth, and Harry sat at the front desk, desk one, and laid his pens parallel, and waited to be allowed to sit a mathematics examination.

“It’s right,” said the deputy head, who had a list. “Potter, H. Front row, desk one. Boards’ special arrangement.” She looked down at him over the list. “Good luck, Harry.”

“Thank you, Mrs Okafor.”

The man in the cardigan looked at him a moment longer than the number needed.

“Haines,” he said. “Physics. I’ll be doing most of yours. If you need anything—” he paused, visibly discarding *the toilet* as inadequate to the occasion “—put your hand up.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You may begin,” said Mr Haines, to the room, and a hundred and forty papers turned over with a sound like a flock lifting off a field.

Harry turned his, and read it, and that took nine seconds, and then he had the problem he had been thinking about for a month and had never quite solved, and the problem was this: an hour and a half.

Ninety minutes, at two hundred to one, was three hundred hours. Twelve and a half days at a desk in a room where he was not permitted to read, or move, or do anything whatever but the paper in front of him — a paper he could finish, at the speed of his hand, in perhaps twenty-five minutes. He had sat tests before. He had never sat one with *rules*, where the end of the paper was not the end of the sitting, and he had spent some of the spring genuinely worried about what twelve days in a sports hall would do to a mind that had very nearly been killed by a playpen.

He had decided, in the end, to treat it as a gift. Nobody else in his life had ever given him twelve days with nothing to do but think, and an obligation to look as if he were doing nothing.

So he did the paper. Carefully, at the speed of his hand, showing all working, because the mark scheme would want it and because a dead man’s margins had taught him that writing for someone slower was a courtesy, not a chore. It took twenty-six minutes. He checked it twice. Then he laid the pen down parallel to the ruler, and folded his hands, and looked at a point on the far wall just above the badminton net, and went inside.

He spent the first day on the colouring problem, because he owed Carrow and because there was a thing he had seen on the Thursday that he had not had a chance to turn over — a place where two pieces of the construction could slide past each other that they had both been assuming could not. He turned it over. He turned it a long way. By the time Mr Haines walked past his desk for the third time, glanced down at the folded hands and the finished paper and the small boy gazing peacefully at the badminton net, and walked on, Harry had the rough shape of what he would say to Carrow on Thursday, and it was going to make her swear.

The second day he spent on the people. A hundred and forty of them, breathing. He had never been in a room with so many minds working so hard at once and he could hear them — not the thoughts; the *weather*: the pens that stopped and started, the sighs, the boy at the back who was going to fail and knew it and kept turning to the last page as if a different question might have appeared there. He minded about that boy, properly, for most of an hour of his own time, and nothing came, because there was nothing to mend; the boy simply hadn’t done the work, and the magic had no sentence for that, and he filed it under *limits* and moved on.

The third day he thought about his mother. He let himself. He had very little of her — a word in a dream, a sentence from Marge, *your mum especially; everyone said* from a woman on a garden bench — and he had learned to be careful how often he took it out, because it did not wear well. But he was sitting in an examination she had never sat, in a world she had left, being the clever one, and he found he wanted to tell her. *I’m doing the papers, Mum. Fifteen of them. The lady from the county says it’s never been done. I’m letting them see.* The badminton net did not reply. He hadn’t expected it to.

“Pens down,” said Mr Haines. “Stop writing now, please.”

He had been sitting with his pen down for sixty-four minutes, which was most of a fortnight, and had not been bored for one second of it, and he thought, standing up into the flock-noise of a hundred and forty people exhaling, that he had learned something that morning no board had a grade for. He could do this. He could sit in a room where the rules said *nothing* and fill the nothing himself, for twelve days, out of his own head, and come out having done good work and thought about a boy at the back and said a thing to his mother. The sports hall had been the playpen. He had been afraid of the playpen since he was two.

He wasn’t any more.

---

Mr Haines caught him in the corridor after the fourth paper, which was physics, and which Harry had finished in nineteen minutes and then spent eleven days thinking about a thing in the sixth question that the paper had got, very slightly, wrong.

“Potter.”

“Sir.”

“Question six.” Mr Haines had the paper in his hand — the question paper, not Harry’s; invigilators were not supposed to read the scripts and Mr Haines, Harry had already decided, was a man who did not do things he was not supposed to. “You wrote something in the margin of your script. I didn’t read it. I saw that there was something, and I saw you underline it, and I have been teaching this syllabus for twenty-nine years and I know what is on page four of that paper, and I should like to know, as one professional to another, whether you found what I think you found.”

“The moments one, sir,” said Harry. “The diagram and the words don’t agree about which way the force acts. The words are right. If you use the diagram you get the wrong sign.”

Mr Haines looked at the ceiling of the sixth-form corridor for a moment.

“I have written to that board about that diagram,” he said. “Four times.” He looked at Harry. “You put it in the margin.”

“I didn’t know who else to tell.”

“Hm.” Mr Haines put his hands in his cardigan pockets, and seemed to come to a decision. “I shall be invigilating the chemistry on Thursday. And the further mathematics, which I don’t understand, and the biology, which I understand less. If you find anything else wrong with the papers, Potter, don’t put it in the margin. Put your hand up. I’ll come over. I should like to see it for myself.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Potter.” He had turned away and turned back. “The moments one. Apart from the diagram. It was straightforward?”

“Yes, sir. Once you know which way it goes.”

“Good,” said Mr Haines, and went off to find a cup of tea, and Harry stood a moment longer in the corridor with the thing in his throat that was not for question six, and then went to catch the train, where Mrs Figg was waiting with a castle.

He put his hand up twice more that fortnight. Both times Mr Haines came over and bent down and looked, and said “Hm,” and once he said “*Yes*,” quietly, with real pleasure, and wrote something on his own pad, and Harry gathered that the board would be receiving a letter.

---

“Say it,” said Carrow.

It was the Thursday after the mathematics paper, in her office, at twenty past four, with the lake going grey outside and the board so covered in chalk that she had started writing on the back of the door. The colouring problem — stuck since 1983; a corner of it found by Harry in his second week at the library a year ago; and since then, every Thursday, the two of them going at the general case from every side either of them could think of and a great many sides that neither of them would have thought of alone.

That was the thing he had not expected about working with someone. He had thought it would be faster. It wasn’t. He could think a year’s worth in a week; she couldn’t; the pace of the thing was set by the slowest mind in the room, and the slowest mind in the room was a professor of mathematics, and it was *still* two hundred times slower than him, and he had spent the first month quietly frantic about it and the second month understanding why it didn’t matter.

It didn’t matter because she could see things he couldn’t. Not faster. *Differently*. She had thirty years of knowing where the bodies were buried in six fields he had read but never lived in, and when he came at the problem down a road that looked clear to him she could say, without looking up, “That’s the Hungarian trap, Potter, three people have died in it, go round,” and he would go round, and the road he hadn’t seen would be the one. He had a thousand years of thought. She had a *map*. And a map, he had decided that spring, was worth most of a thousand years, if the years had all been spent in one cupboard.

“It doesn’t hold,” he said.

“Where.”

“Here. The step from the third case to the general one. We’ve been assuming the pieces don’t overlap. They can. I saw it on Monday, in the sports hall. I had twelve days.” He drew it on the back of the door. “They slide past each other. Like that.”

Carrow came to the door and looked at it for a long moment, and said something under her breath that he had not heard her say before and that he filed, with interest, for later.

“Yes,” she said. “Yes, *damn* it. They do.” She took the chalk out of his hand. “All right. Then we don’t go through the third case. We go—” She stopped. The chalk stopped. He watched her face do a thing he had been waiting a year to see it do, and the thing was the ice — the world going vast and slow and bright — except that it was happening to someone else, at one-to-one, and he could see every frame of it. “We go *under*. If they can slide, they can be *made* to. All of them. The whole way down. Potter — if we force the overlap—”

“—then the overlap is the thing we count,” said Harry, “not the pieces,” and he had it, and she had it, at the same moment, two people standing in front of a door in the rain with their mouths slightly open, and neither of them said anything for three full seconds, which for him was most of a morning, and he spent it looking at Helen Carrow’s face and thinking that he had never in his life seen anything so entirely worth the price of admission.

It took them till seven to write it down so that it held. She rang the Dursleys herself, and spoke to Vernon in the voice she used for the head of faculty, and Vernon said *of course, Professor*, and she put the phone down and said, “That man would sign anything,” and went back to the door. When it held she stood back and looked at it — nine feet of chalk and most of a door — and took her glasses off, and said quite quietly, “Four years,” and then, in the ordinary voice: “Right. We write it up. Properly. Both names.”

“Carrow and Potter.”

“Alphabetical. It’s the convention and I don’t hold with fiddling it.” She looked at him over the glasses. “You understand what this is. It’s a paper. In a journal. With your name on it. Not a certificate. The *conversation* — the one I told you about, the one that goes back a hundred years. You’ll be in it. At six.” A pause. “They won’t believe it, you know. The referees. They’ll think I’m having a joke.”

“Then we’d better make the proof very good,” said Harry.

Carrow laughed — the first time he had heard it properly, out loud; he started a tally — and threw him his coat.

---

Results came on a Thursday in the third week of August, both sets on the same morning, because the boards had decided, in a spasm of administrative mercy, that if they were going to do this they might as well do it once.

Stonewall gave them out in the hall, in envelopes, from a table with a tablecloth on it, and the corridor was full of sixteen-year-olds screaming or going very quiet, and eighteen-year-olds doing the same more loudly, and Harry stood in the middle of it, up to most people’s waists, with his hands in his pockets, and waited his turn at the P’s.

Mr Haines was behind the table. He had taken the P’s on purpose; Harry could see it in the way he was sitting. He found the two envelopes without looking, and held them, and did not hand them over.

“I’ve known since Monday,” he said. “They telephone the centre first. I have been teaching for twenty-nine years, Potter, and I have never once wanted to open someone else’s results, and I have had these in my desk since Monday, and I have not opened them, and I should like that noted.”

“Noted, sir.”

Mr Haines gave him the envelopes. Harry opened the first standing up, with the hall roaring round him, and read it, which took a second, and then the second, which took another, and then stood holding both, and the two seconds went on for a very long time.

Ten A’s. Five A’s. Fifteen letters down two sheets of paper, all the same letter, and his name at the top of each, and it was the first piece of paper in his life from the outside world that said, in a form the outside world could not argue with, what he was.

He had expected to feel something large. He felt something small and precise, like a key turning. *They can see.* That was all. Seven years of being the only one who knew, and now there was a certificate, and the certificate was in the handwriting of four examination boards who had said no twice.

Mr Haines was watching his face.

“Well?”

Harry turned the sheets round and held them up.

Mr Haines looked at them. He took his glasses off and looked again. Then he put them back on and sat back in his chair behind the tablecloth and said, quite quietly, so that only Harry heard it under the roar, “Twenty-nine years,” and then, a little louder: “Yes. Well. I did say the moments one was straightforward.”

“You did, sir.”

“Go and telephone your uncle, Potter. Before the newspapers do.”

---

The newspapers did it anyway.

The *Surrey Advertiser* was Thursday evening, because Mrs Achebe clicked her pen. The nationals were Saturday, because the *Advertiser* had a stringer, and because a boy of seven with fifteen top grades was the sort of thing that filled the whole of page three in the dead week of August when nothing else was happening. BOY, 7, SITS FIFTEEN EXAMS — AND GETS TOP GRADE IN EVERY ONE. Four of them ran it. Two of them ran the photograph the *Advertiser* had taken on the Thursday — a small boy in a school jumper holding two sheets of paper, carefully, with both hands, as if he were carrying them for somebody else — and all of them used the word *prodigy*, and the word sat on the kitchen table at Number Four for a whole weekend being read aloud by Vernon in a voice that got slightly deeper each time.

Vernon, that weekend, was a man transfigured. Harry had seen him pleased before — the university letter, the Rotary, the funeral suit at the long table — but this was a different order of thing, and he watched it with the attention of a naturalist who has finally seen the rare bird at the top of the tree. His uncle moved differently. He answered the telephone before the second ring. He said *my nephew* to the man from the garage, to Mrs Next Door, to Marge — twice to Marge, who had rung back to make sure she had heard right, and had been told, in a voice Harry would remember for the rest of his life with something close to affection, that she had heard perfectly well the first time, and that the Dursleys had *always* encouraged him, and that if she had anything further to say about bad blood she could say it to the *Daily Mail*.

Petunia said nothing, which was what Petunia said. But on the Saturday morning, at the till at the greengrocer’s, she opened her purse to pay, and Harry, standing with the basket at knee height as he had stood at that till since he was three, saw inside it, folded small behind the stamps, a square of newsprint with a photograph on it, and looked away before she could see him seeing.

He never mentioned it. He never would. He put it on the shelf with the straight fingers and the garden bench and the margins, in the place where he kept the things that had been done for him quietly by people who would have denied it, and the shelf was getting, he noticed, rather full.

---

He gave one interview, by design, and it was the most boring interview of 1987, and he was prouder of it than of the fifteen A’s.

The nationals wanted television. He said no, through Mrs Bell, who was delighted to say it for him. He had no intention of being a thing that people watched in their lounges. But a newspaper interview could be *managed*, if you were patient and dull and had rehearsed every answer for a fortnight, and it bought something he wanted, which was to be a *known thing*. A filed thing. A boy in a drawer marked *prodigy, Surrey, polite* that the world could stop wondering about and leave alone.

So he sat in Mrs Bell’s office with the orange squash, with Mrs Bell behind her desk and Vernon in the funeral suit in the good chair, and a young man from the *Telegraph* with a notebook and a tape recorder asked him what it was like, and he told him, in sentences a seven-year-old might plausibly produce, that he liked reading, and that the school and the university had been very kind, and that his uncle and aunt had always encouraged him. He said he wanted to go on learning things. He said his favourite subject was mathematics because you could always check whether you were right. The young man from the *Telegraph* wrote it all down and visibly despaired of the whole business, and Harry watched the despair with deep satisfaction.

He had planted exactly one line, and he planted it at the end, where the young man asked — because they always asked — who he would like to thank.

“Mrs Prentice at the library,” said Harry, “who held the door. And Mrs Figg, who takes me on the train, and has kept an eye on me for a very long time. I don’t think I’d have done any of it without them.”

It went in. He checked, the following Tuesday, on page nine of the *Telegraph*, under a headline about a *modest prodigy*. It was a perfectly ordinary thing for a child to say. It would mean nothing to anyone who read it on this side of the curtain. And if anyone on the far side read Muggle newspapers — he did not know whether they did; he suspected the man Mrs Figg wrote to did; he suspected the man read everything — then somewhere in Scotland, or wherever such men sat, a person who had asked a squib to watch over a small boy had just been thanked for it, in the *Telegraph*, by name, by the boy, who had known about the watching for years and had decided to say so in print where everyone could see and no one could understand.

It was the nearest thing to a letter he could send. He hoped it had arrived.

---

The money came last, and came strangely, and taught him what the ordinary world did when it decided to be kind to a child.

A man called Pargeter telephoned Mrs Bell in September. He was a solicitor, in Guildford, and he represented a client who had read page three and wished to do something about it, and the something, when it had been explained to Vernon across Mrs Bell’s desk at a meeting Harry was permitted to attend in silence, turned out to be a trust. An educational trust, in Harry’s name, funded to a sum that made Vernon sit very still in the funeral suit, administered by Mr Pargeter’s firm, to pay for books, tuition, examination fees, travel to and from any place of learning, and *such other expenses as the trustees shall in their discretion deem conducive to the beneficiary’s education*, until the beneficiary attained the age of twenty-one.

“The trustees,” said Vernon, after a moment, “being—”

“Myself,” said Mr Pargeter, “and my partner, Mrs Hale. Our client was specific on that point. He felt — forgive me, Mr Dursley — that the funds should be kept quite separate from the household, so that the family should never be put to the awkwardness of seeming to benefit. It’s a standard provision.” He said this in a voice as smooth as a bowling green, and Harry, watching his uncle discover that a large sum of money had arrived in the family and could not be *touched*, understood that it was not a standard provision at all, that Mrs Achebe had clicked her pen somewhere behind this, that Mr Pargeter had met Vernon Dursley before he had met Vernon Dursley, and that kind people, when they chose to be, could be quite as careful as he was.

Vernon signed. He had to; the alternative was explaining to the Rotary why he had refused a scholarship. He signed.

“Who is he?” Harry asked Mr Pargeter at the door. “The client.”

“I’m afraid I can’t say.”

“Can you tell him thank you?”

Mr Pargeter looked down at him from a great height with a face that gave away nothing at all, and then it gave away a very small amount at one corner.

“I can tell him,” he said, “that you asked. I rather think that will do.”

---

That night he took stock, the way he did at the end of a thing, in the words he would have used if there had been anyone to use them to, and it was short, because the long version was going to be the rest of his life.

*Fifteen papers. All of them. Nobody has done it and the boards said so twice. A paper in a journal going off in October with my name on it. A trust that pays for travel to any place of learning, and Mrs F says the far side has a bookshop.*

*Four years. Eight hundred of mine, roughly.*

*I have read everything on the fourth floor that I can follow and most of the fifth. I have the grammar to eleven parts and a bracket and I can make six sentences of it. I have not seen one of their books. Mrs F will not bring me one; she says it is not hers to do. I have not asked where the shop is. I think she would tell me. I think I am going to find it myself first, because I want to have found it, and then ask.*

Then he turned to the inside of the cupboard door, where the date was pinned at the height of his eyes, and under the date, on the same square of paper, in pencil, small, he added the one word he had been adding to every plan he had ever made — not to remember it; so that it was said, on a door, where the house could read it.

*Faster.*

He was seven years and one month old, and the most examined child in the history of four examination boards, and he lay down in the smallest office in England and did not sleep, because it was ten past one and a woman in a committee blouse on BBC2 was about to explain the French Revolution to nobody, and he had eight hundred years, and a bookshop to find.
