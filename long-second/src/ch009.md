# Chapter 9 — The Edge of the Map

“Mrs Figg,” he said, in the café at Egham, on a Tuesday in October, with the pot warm and a biscuit in his hand, “I’m going to find the street. The one with the shops. I thought you should know before I did it rather than after.”

Mrs Figg put down her castle.

“I can’t take you,” she said. “You know that. It isn’t mine to—”

“I know. I’m not asking you to take me. I’m asking you to come to London on Saturday and sit in a café while I look, because you come everywhere else I go and it would be strange if you didn’t, and because I’d rather you were there.” He ate the biscuit. “You don’t have to point. I don’t want you to point. I want to have found it. But I’d like you to be across the road when I do.”

She looked at him for a long moment, over the glasses, with the face that had been changing all year — the surprise gone out of it, mostly, and the arithmetic, and only the gladness left, and under the gladness something he had begun to recognise as *pride*, which was not a thing anyone had ever pointed at him before and which he was still learning to stand in.

“How will you know where to look?” she said. “London’s a big place.”

“I’ve worked it out. I think. If I’m wrong I’ll have had a day in London, and you’ll have had a scone somewhere new.” He finished the biscuit. “Will you come?”

“I’ll come,” said Arabella Figg. “I shan’t point. And Harry — if you find it, I shall have to write and say so.”

“I know,” said Harry. “That’s why I told you first.”

---

He had worked out where to look the way he worked out everything, which was by refusing to guess.

He knew almost nothing. A street, Mrs Figg had said, with shops, and a bank, and a newspaper. London. People in cloaks went to it to do their shopping and nobody on this side had ever noticed; that was the whole of his information, and it was, he had decided in the sports hall, enough.

Because a street like that had to have a door. People in cloaks had to come from somewhere and go to somewhere, and the somewhere had to open onto an ordinary pavement, and the pavement had to be one where a cloak did not draw a crowd. Where in London could you walk in a floor-length cloak and a hat and not be looked at twice? He had thought about it on the four-floor stairs of the library and arrived, by the second landing, at an answer that pleased him with its plainness: the theatres. Costume shops. The streets round Covent Garden and Soho and the Charing Cross Road, where half the people on the pavement were dressed as something and the other half had seen everything.

So that was where he went, on the Saturday, with Mrs Figg beside him on the train in her mackintosh with a castle in the pocket, and then on the top deck of buses, because the top deck of a bus was the best observation platform ever built for a boy who could read a crowd at a face every two seconds and had an afternoon.

He rode the 24, and the 29, and the 176, with his forehead against the glass and Mrs Figg a seat behind pretending to read, and watched the pavements of the West End go by in a slow river of faces, and looked for the thing he had seen three times in his life — the cloak in the wrong season, the hat with the wrong brim, the particular *wrongness* of someone dressed by a different century’s rules and not in on the joke — and for an hour he saw nothing, and then on the Charing Cross Road, going north, he saw a woman.

Emerald green. Ankle-length. A pointed hat she was carrying rather than wearing, as if she had been told to and resented it. She walked fast, like someone with an errand, and the Saturday crowd parted round her and did not look at her, and that — the *not looking* — was what caught him, more than the green. The crowd should have looked. Londoners looked at everything for a quarter-second and moved on; he had watched them do it all morning. They did not look at her at all. Their eyes went over her the way water goes over a stone.

Behind him, Mrs Figg turned a page of her castle with great deliberation, and said nothing, and he loved her for it.

He got off at the next stop.

---

He stood on the east side of the Charing Cross Road for two hours, in a shop doorway and then at a bus stop and then in a shop doorway again, and he watched the far pavement, and in two hours he saw eleven of them.

Mrs Figg sat in the window of a sandwich bar directly opposite with a pot of tea and the castle, not reading, not pointing, with her face turned very slightly away from the stretch of frontage between a large bookshop and a record shop in the manner of a woman who had been asked not to help and was finding it the hardest thing she had done all year.

He kept a tally. A tall man in a cloak like a monk’s. Two women arguing cheerfully, one with a basket that moved on its own — he saw it move; nobody else did. A boy his own age in ordinary clothes holding the hand of a woman who was not in ordinary clothes at all, looking round at the street with the wide-eyed air of a child being taken to town for something exciting. An old man with a beard who stopped on the pavement, looked both ways like a child crossing, and *stepped in* — and that was the thing.

They all stepped in. At the same place.

Not through a door he could see. He had expected that to be the problem and had sat on the train composing how he would handle it. Between the bookshop and the record shop there was a stretch of frontage perhaps thirty feet long, and eleven people in two hours had walked along the pavement towards it and had — he could not put it better — *gone*. Not vanished. Nothing so vulgar. They had simply, at a point about two-thirds along, stopped being on the pavement, and the crowd had flowed on over the place as if it had always been empty, and every time it happened Harry found that he had been looking at the record shop.

Every time. Eleven times. He was the best watcher in Surrey and probably in England and he had been looking at the *record shop*.

That was the moment he understood the curtain, and it was not at all what he had imagined. He had pictured it like a wall, or a lock — a thing on the far side, keeping people out. It was nothing of the kind. It was on *his* side. It was in his head. Something in that thirty feet of frontage reached into the place where he decided what to look at, and moved his looking, gently, an inch to the left, and let him think he had done it himself. He had felt his attention go. He had every one of those eleven moments on the shelf and he went back through them now at leisure and found the same thing in each: a small sideways pressure, like a hand on the back of the head, so soft and so timed to the ordinary jump of the eye that no one on earth could possibly have noticed it happening.

No one who looked at the world at one-to-one.

He stood in his doorway with his heart going and understood what he had. It was a *sentence*. It was the roses — a shape, a grammar, working on him, written by somebody long ago and left running, like the warmth in the blankets. And it was not asking what he *was*. The boy in ordinary clothes had gone through with his hand in a witch’s, and it had let him, because the hand knew the way. It was a sentence for keeping a secret from people who did not have it yet, and he did not have it yet. And it was written for people whose attention jumped once and landed. His jumped two hundred times in the space where theirs jumped once. There was *room* in his looking that the sentence had never been built to fill.

So he went and stood in it.

He crossed the road and walked, slowly, along the thirty feet, and felt the hand come — soft, kind, *nothing to see here, dear* — and let it move him, and watched it move him, the way he had watched the roses. Then he walked back, and let it do it again, and watched again. Then a third time, and this time, where the hand came, he did a thing he had been practising on the roses for a year without knowing what it was for: he stood still inside himself and *didn’t go with it*. He let the push arrive and simply declined to look at the record shop, the way you decline a biscuit, and held his looking where it was, on the stretch of grimy brick between the two shopfronts, and for a second nothing happened, and the hand pushed harder, almost puzzled —

and there was a pub.

Small. Grubby. A low door and a window of thick greenish glass and a sign hanging crooked, and it had been there the whole time, and it was there now, and a man in a cloak came along the pavement and nodded to no one and went into it, and the door shut, and the Saturday crowd went by on either side of Harry Potter where he stood on the kerb with his mouth slightly open, looking straight at the edge of the map.

He turned round.

Across the road, in the window of the sandwich bar, Mrs Figg had put down her castle and was looking at him, and her face was doing the thing it had done on the day of the cat, and she raised her cup to him, very slightly, an inch, the way you would to someone across a room who had just done a thing you had bet on.

He crossed back.

“Well,” said Arabella Figg, when he had sat down opposite her. “Now you’ve found it.” She poured him a cup from the pot, which was still warm, because he had seen to that at half past two from forty yards. “Two hours and eleven minutes. Tibbles had you at three hours. I shall enjoy telling him.”

“You could see it the whole time.”

“I could see it the whole time. I’ve been able to see that pub since I was four years old; my brothers used to take me. I have sat here two hours with it in the corner of my eye and not said a word, and I should like you to know, Harry, that it is the hardest thing I have done since 1946.” She drank her tea. “What did it feel like? Finding it. I’ve always wondered. I never had to.”

He thought about how to say it. “Like a hand on the back of my head,” he said. “Very gentle. Pushing me to look somewhere else. And I just — didn’t.”

Mrs Figg looked at him over her cup for a long moment.

“Nobody can do that,” she said. “It turns away anyone who doesn’t know it’s there. Wizards know. A Muggle with a witch holding her hand gets through, because the hand knows. Nobody *finds* it. That is the whole of what it’s for.” She put the cup down. “I shall have to find a way to put that in a letter too. I don’t know how I shall. *He declined the Leaky Cauldron.*” And then — he saw her decide, the way he had seen so many of them decide that year, to stand to one side of a door — “Well. You’ve found it. What now?”

“Now I ask you to take me in,” said Harry. “To buy books. I’ve found it myself, which is what I wanted. The rest I can’t do alone; I don’t know how the door in the wall works, or what the money is, or what’s a sensible thing to say to a goblin. You do.” He looked at her. “I know you’ll have to write and say so. I know he might say you shouldn’t have. But Mrs Figg — I have read every book on the fourth and fifth floors of a university, and I have never seen a page of theirs, and there is a *bookshop* on the other side of that wall, and I have four years.”

“He’ll say no,” said Mrs Figg. “If I ask him.”

“Then don’t ask him,” said Harry. “Tell him after.”

Mrs Figg sat very still in the window of the sandwich bar, with the pub in the corner of her eye and the boy in front of her and forty years of doing what she was told behind her, and he watched her — he had all the time in the world to watch her — go back through those forty years and find the two occasions on which she had told Albus Dumbledore he was wrong, and weigh them, and find them good.

“I’ve got the scone money,” she said slowly. “He raised it. Four galleons a month since March; I’ve not spent a knut of it; it’s in a jar. It would be about—” she did the sum “—thirty galleons. That buys a good many books.” She picked up the castle and put it in her pocket. “And he can have the bill. In the letter. Itemised.”

She stood up.

“Finish your tea, dear. And Harry — when we go through, you hold my hand. Not because you’re seven. Because I am sixty-one and I have not been in there since my brother died and I should like something to hold.”

---

The wall in the yard behind the pub needed a wand, and Mrs Figg had none, and so they waited, as he had planned to wait anyway, and in four minutes a family came through from the street and the father took a stick from his sleeve and counted bricks and tapped one, and Harry stood eight feet behind with an old woman’s hand in his and *saw* it.

The brick wriggled. To anyone at one-to-one it simply moved; to him it shivered, like a dog shaking off rain, and then the brick beside it, and then the wall was *doing* something — not opening, not folding, but rearranging, each brick turning on an axis it should not have had and sliding past its neighbour in an order, a long patient order that he could *read*, that had the same spine as the roses and the tea, the pointing and the pushing and a bracket in the middle so large and so old that it took him the whole of the half-second to get to the end of it. It was a sentence. Somebody had written it into a wall a very long time ago, so well that it had run every day since without anyone needing to think about it, and it said, as near as he could put it, *for those who know the knock, a way*, and he stood in the yard and watched it say so, brick by brick, and thought: *I could write that.* Not today. Not this year. But he could see every mark of it, and nothing he could see had ever stayed closed to him for long.

“You’re reading it,” said Mrs Figg, very low, beside him. “Aren’t you. The wall.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody reads it, dear. They just knock.”

Then the wall was an archway, and the family went through, and Mrs Figg’s hand tightened on his, and they went through behind them, and the wall closed with a sound like a sigh, and he stopped.

He had stopped on every threshold of his life that mattered, and this was the one all the others had been practice for.

A street. Cobbled, crooked, climbing a little as it went, and *full* — of shops with bowed windows and hanging signs, of people in cloaks and robes and pointed hats and ordinary cardigans, of noise, of smells, of owls. There were owls in cages outside a shop, dozens, blinking at the daylight. There was a stack of cauldrons on the pavement with a sign that said ALL SIZES — COPPER, BRASS, PEWTER, SILVER — SELF-STIRRING. A shop whose window was entirely broomsticks, with a crowd of boys in front of it. A woman going past with a basket in which something was unmistakably moving, and nobody minding. The sky was the same sky. And under it ten thousand people were going about a Saturday’s shopping in a street that nobody on the far side of a wall had ever seen, and the air was thick with the thing from Mrs Figg’s kitchen floor, so thick he could almost see it, the whole street humming at a pitch just under hearing like a struck bell that had never been allowed to stop.

“Oh,” said Harry.

“Yes,” said Mrs Figg, beside him, in a voice he had not heard her use, looking at a street she had not seen in nine years. “Yes. It does rather.”

---

The bank was at the top, and it was white, and the goblins at the door looked at the old woman in the mackintosh and the small boy with his hand in hers with such complete and professional contempt that Harry, who had spent seven years studying faces, felt an instant and slightly alarming kinship.

The goblin at the counter had a long nose and a quill that moved very fast, and he did not look up when Mrs Figg put thirty galleons on the marble in a jam jar, and he did not look up when Harry said, “And I’d like to change Muggle money as well, please. Into yours,” and put a hundred and twelve pounds beside it, which was the whole of the toffee tin and had been counted out by Mrs Prentice that morning with one raised eyebrow and no questions.

The quill stopped. The goblin looked up — a long look, down the long nose, taking in the jumper, the glasses, the fact that the boy was seven — and held out one hand, palm up, without a word.

He counted the money twice, fast, with the air of someone who could have done it once and was being insulting on purpose. Then he did something with brass scales and a small book of tables, and wrote a figure, and began counting coins onto the marble in three stacks with a sound like the beginning of rain.

“Twenty-two galleons,” he said. “Six sickles. Eleven knuts. Seventeen to the galleon, twenty-nine to the sickle, if you don’t know, and you don’t.” He pushed the stacks across. “Next.”

“Thank you,” said Harry.

The quill paused — a quarter-second, which was most of a minute — and the long nose came up a fraction, and the goblin regarded Harry Potter with something that was not warmth, nothing so vulgar, but was perhaps the faintest flicker of a professional noticing that it had been thanked, correctly, by a child who had not been told to.

“Keep it inside your shirt,” said the goblin. “Next.”

Outside, on the white steps, in the sun, Mrs Figg said, “That was Bogrod. He counted my brother’s money twice in 1951. I believe he considers it a courtesy.”

---

Flourish and Blotts had a window full of books stacked to the ceiling and a bell on the door, and inside it was every library he had ever walked into and none of them, because the books *moved*.

Not all of them. Most sat on their shelves like books, floor to ceiling, in leather and cloth and something that might have been dragon, with ladders on rails and a smell of paper and dust and binding glue that went into him like the first day at the branch. But some shifted. A row of fat volumes on a high shelf were, very slowly, rearranging themselves into a better order. A thin book near the door kept trying to turn its own pages and being prevented by a strap. And in a cage by the till — he stopped, and looked, and thought of a woman in a garden saying *some of them bite* — a dozen heavy books with fur on their covers lay in a heap, breathing, and one of them opened one eye at him and closed it again.

He had fifty-two galleons and some silver, and he had planned this hour the way he had planned the sports hall, and he went to work.

He read the spines. All of them. He had read ten thousand spines in the Survey at five and knew what a shelf of spines could tell you — what a field thought was basic, what it thought was advanced, what it had too much of and what it was embarrassed about — and he walked the shop slowly with his hands behind his back like a boy told not to touch, and read, at eye-speed, every title in Flourish and Blotts in about forty minutes, which in the hours he actually lived in was a solid week of cataloguing, and came out the far end with a map.

The field had a canon. There were books that appeared in stacks — set texts, for the school — and books that appeared singly and dusty, and the stacked ones were cheap and the single ones were not. He took the stacked ones that mattered: *The Standard Book of Spells*, all seven grades, because the grammar of the thing was what he was after and seven years of its sentences for the price of a few good dinners was not a thing you walked past. *Magical Theory*, by a man called Waffling, which he opened at random and read a page of standing up and found to be exactly what the title said and badly in need of a Mr Prentice in its margins. *A History of Magic*. *Hogwarts: A History*, thick. A copy of the day’s *Daily Prophet* from a pile by the door, because a newspaper was the fastest way into a place he had ever found.

And then, on a high shelf in the back room where the single dusty books lived, a thin grey volume with no title on the spine at all, which he only took down because it was the one book in the shop his eyes had slid past, twice — and he had learned that very afternoon what it meant when his eyes slid past a thing — and which turned out, when he got it open, to be called *On the Composition of Charms: Notes Towards a Grammar*, by nobody he had heard of, printed in 1911, and which began, on the first page, with the sentence: *It has long been held that a spell is a thing one learns. The present writer wishes to suggest that it is a thing one reads.*

He stood in the back room with the thin grey book in both hands and the breathing books in their cage and the street humming outside, and read the first chapter standing up, and when he had finished he had to sit down on the bottom rung of the ladder, because somebody — seventy-six years ago, in a hand that disliked writing — had stood where he was standing.

Not as far. Nowhere near; the man had worked at one-to-one and said so, apologetically, in a footnote: *the present writer regrets that observation of the casting moment is beyond any human faculty and must be inferred.* He had inferred. He had got the spine, and the pointing part, and a bad guess at the bracket, and then he had run out of road, and the book was thin because the road was short. But he had *seen there was a road*. And nobody, by the dust, had read him since the war.

Harry bought it. It cost more than the seven grades together, and he paid without a word, and the young woman at the till with ink on her fingers — who had watched a child of seven read every spine in the shop without touching one and had decided, visibly, not to have an opinion about it — wrapped the thin grey book separately, in brown paper, and said, as she handed it over, “Nobody’s bought that in the whole time I’ve worked here. What did you want it for?”

“The margins,” said Harry, which was true, and she laughed, not understanding, and he put it in the canvas bag with the others.

He had two galleons left. He stood outside the shop in the sun with a bag that cut his shoulder and Mrs Figg beside him with the jam jar empty in her pocket, and the whole street going past, and he took out *A History of Magic*, because it was the thickest, and turned, as he had turned every book since he was five, to the index first.

He was looking for his mother’s name. That was the whole of it. He had a word in a dream and a sentence from Marge and *your mum especially*, and he had come to a street with a bookshop in the hope that a street with a bookshop would have a page.

He found *Potter* in the index. It had seven entries. The last one said *Potter, Harry — see also Boy Who Lived, The*.

---

Mrs Figg found a bench. She did not say anything. She sat him down on it, outside a shop that sold quills, and sat beside him, and took his free hand in her right one, the mended one, and held it, and he read.

Four minutes. The entry, and the pages it pointed to, and the three further pages in *Hogwarts: A History* that those pointed to, and the lower half of the *Prophet*’s front page, which carried, by the kind of timing he had once called luck, a small piece on the sixth anniversary that autumn of *the fall of You-Know-Who*. Four minutes, with the street going past, and then he closed the books and sat with his hand in Mrs Figg’s and let the four minutes catch up with him, which took, by the only clock that counted, most of a year.

He had known. He kept coming back to that, the way you press a bruise. He had known since a violet hat that strangers were glad to see him; since a garden bench that his parents had been of this world and died in it; since a letter in eleven days that there was a man who had reasons. He had known all the pieces. He had never had them in print, in order, in the cold plain prose of a reference book that did not know he was reading it, under a heading with his own name.

*Lily Potter (née Evans) and James Potter were murdered at their home in Godric’s Hollow on the night of 31 October 1981 by the Dark wizard Lord Voldemort, whose name most of the wizarding community still declines to speak. Their son Harry, then fifteen months old, survived an attempt on his life by the same wizard — the only person ever known to have survived the Killing Curse — and Voldemort’s power was broken in the attempt, by means which remain unexplained. The scar on the child’s forehead is believed to be a consequence of the curse. He is widely referred to as the Boy Who Lived.*

The green light. Six years of a word in a dream, and it had been *this*. A man in his parents’ house, and his mother’s voice saying his name, and then a thing that had killed her and been meant to kill him and had — for reasons the reference books did not know — not. And had, instead, perhaps, done something else. Spun a wheel. He had wondered since he was four where the two hundred had come from. He had never once let himself wonder whether it came from the same place as the scar.

He wondered now, on a bench in Diagon Alley with an old woman holding his hand, and found he could not stop.

“Mrs Figg,” he said, after a long time. “You knew all this.”

“Every word, dear. Since before you could talk.”

“Why didn’t you—”

“Because it wasn’t mine to tell. And because—” her hand tightened “—because I have watched you read ten thousand books, Harry, and I thought: he should have this one off a *page*. Like the rest. Not off my face at a kitchen table. You’d have read my face. You read everybody’s. I wanted you to have it plain, the way you have everything, and then I wanted to be sitting right here when you did.” She was not looking at him. She was looking at the street. “Was I wrong?”

He thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how.

“No,” he said. “No. You were right. I’d rather have had the page.” And then, because it was her, and because he had read the paragraph four times and it had not changed: “They don’t say he’s dead.”

“No.”

“They say *vanished*. *Power broken*. *Many believe*. The careful words. I’ve been reading careful books for three years; I know what the careful words are for.” He looked at the *Prophet* on his knee. “That’s why you watch for cloaks in the wrong season. That’s why there’s a — a protection on my aunt’s roof; she’s never said, but there is, I can feel the edge of it on the step some nights. That’s why the man who writes to you won’t come up the path. It isn’t only *for* me. It’s against something.”

Mrs Figg said nothing for a moment.

“Yes,” she said. “That’s about the size of it.”

He sat with that for a long time, carefully, the way he sat with anything that had edges. Then he picked it up and put it on a shelf — not the shelf of things that had made him who he was; a new one, which he named *things I will deal with when I am ready and not one day before* — and found that the shelf held.

Because the other thing was true too. Every person in this street had woken up, six years ago this month, into a world that was suddenly and inexplicably safe, and had been told that a baby had done it, and had been *glad*. They had bowed to him in Little Whinging because he was the morning they had woken up to. He had done nothing. He had been fifteen months old. He had, apparently, not died. But he could not, sitting there, resent them for it, any more than he could resent Mrs Figg for drinking the tea.

“All right,” he said. “All right. I’ve got the page now. Thank you for sitting here.”

“Oh, get on,” said Arabella Figg, and did not let go of his hand, and they sat a while longer on the bench in the sun with the owls and the cauldrons and the boy with the toad going past, and then she said, “Tea at six. Your aunt will have my hide,” and they went back down the crooked street, and at the wall they waited for a family and went through behind them, and the pub was dark, and the Charing Cross Road was loud and ordinary and full of Saturday, and somewhere between the pub and the station, for the first time in six years, properly, with facts, in the middle of a London afternoon with an old woman beside him not looking, he cried.

Not for long. He let it come, and let it go, and they caught the train, and they were home for tea.
