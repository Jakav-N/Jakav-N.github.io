# Chapter 9 — Interlude: Arabella and Albus

*Wisteria Walk, Little Whinging, a Thursday evening in October 1988*

Arabella Figg had written one hundred and nine letters from the kitchen table at Number Two, Wisteria Walk, over the past seven years, and had never before got stuck on the first line.

The pad was the same pad. The pen was the same pen, a fountain pen that had been her mother’s and that she filled, these days, without any trouble at all, holding the barrel steady with her right hand while the left worked the lever. She had caught herself doing that twice this evening already and stopped to look at the hand each time, turning it in the lamplight, making the fist, opening it. Straight fingers. Forty years of pouring with the left and the thing was simply *gone*, as if it had never been, as if she had dreamed the mornings when she couldn’t do the buttons on her own coat.

Mr Tibbles sat on the table by the sugar bowl, which he was not allowed to do, and watched her not write.

“Don’t you look at me like that,” said Arabella. “You were there. You tell me how to put it.”

The newspaper lay open at the page. LITTLE WHINGING BOY, 8, IS COUNTY CHESS CHAMPION. The photograph was a good one, as these things went; they’d got his eyes, and the way he held the cup, carefully, with both hands, as if he were carrying it for someone else. She had cut round it with the kitchen scissors — right hand — and it was going in the envelope whatever she wrote, because Albus Dumbledore had asked her, in the autumn of 1981, in that voice of his, to send him *anything out of the ordinary*, and if a child of eight in the *Surrey Advertiser* was not out of the ordinary she did not know what she had been posted here for.

Except that she did know, of course. That was the trouble.

She had been posted here to watch for *them*. For a cloak in the wrong season, a stranger asking after the house, the wards shivering in the night the way Albus had said she might feel them even without a wand, in her teeth, like weather. Seven years she had watched for that and seen none of it — a few tourists, the Diggle man bowing in the street like a fool, nothing that meant harm — and in all that time Albus had never once written back *and what is the boy himself like?* He hadn’t needed to ask. She had told him, every month, in her letter, in the space at the end: quiet, clever, a reader, no trouble, too thin but they feed him, he smiles at the cats.

She looked at the straight fingers of her right hand, and at the newspaper, and at the cat.

“He asked me,” she said to Mr Tibbles. “That’s the part. Seven years and he never asked for anything, and then he looked at my hands and he *asked*.”

She uncapped the pen.

> *Dear Albus,*
>
> *I enclose a cutting from the local paper which you will want to see. The arrangements it mentions are true — the school and the county have moved him up for his lessons and he goes to the big school now for some of them, and there is talk of examinations next summer. The uncle is delighted and tells everyone. The aunt says nothing. I have watched her say nothing for seven years and I believe this is the first time she has not been frightened while doing it.*
>
> *You will say this is Muggle business and none of ours and the wards hold and he is happy. All of that is true. I am writing to tell you a thing that is not Muggle business.*
>
> *Albus, the boy has done magic in my kitchen four times since the spring, deliberately, with his hand on the thing he meant to mend, and the last time it was me.*

She stopped there, and looked at what she had written, and found that her eyes had gone hot, which was absurd at her age over a sentence.

> *I have told you before about Tibbles and the stairs. I told you about the other cats, one at a time, and you wrote back that children are often kind to animals and the Evans line was always strong and I was not to worry. I did not argue. I am arguing now.*
>
> *Last Saturday he looked at my hands. You have seen my hands. He said they looked sore and asked, very politely, if he might hold them for a minute. Then he held them, and said nothing, and I felt it — I cannot do magic, Albus, but I grew up in a house full of it and I know the feel of it in a room the way you know when the kettle’s about to go — I felt it come up through him and into my hands like a bath filling, and it went to the knuckles as if it knew the way, and when he let go my fingers were straight. They are straight now. I am writing this with the right hand.*
>
> *He is eight years old. He has had no wand, no teaching, no one to show him; there is nobody in that house who could, and nobody in this one either. He has worked it out for himself, on his knees on my kitchen floor, by*

Here she stopped again, for a long time, because she had got to the thing she had understood on the Saturday and had been turning over all week, and did not know how a squib put it to the greatest wizard in the world.

> *— by caring about things, Albus. That is what it looks like from where I sit. He does not wave at them or wish at them. He minds about them until they come right. I have never seen a wizard do magic that way and I have seen a good many wizards.*
>
> *And then he said to me — I shall put it down exactly — "You be careful who you show that to," I had said to him, and he said, "I know. That's why I showed you."*
>
> *He knows I am watching him. I do not know how long he has known. I think it may be years. I think he worked out what the cats were for and what my questions were for and decided, some time ago, that I was on his side — and I am, Albus, I am, that is the whole of my difficulty. He has decided to let me see. I believe he has decided to let everyone see. There is a cup on the Dursleys' sideboard and a photograph in a frame and talk of examinations, and I think he has stopped hiding on purpose, all at once, the way a child decides a thing, and I think he has been hiding an extremely long time, and I do not think any of us has the least idea what was under the lid.*
>
> *You will want to know if he is happy. He is. He is happier than I have ever seen him. That is what frightens me.*
>
> *I remain, as ever, at my post.*
>
> *Arabella*

She read it through twice, blotted it, and folded it round the cutting. Mr Tibbles watched her address the envelope.

“There,” said Arabella. “Now he knows.”

The cat looked at the envelope, and then at her, with the expression that Harry Potter — had he been there — would have recognised at once, and that she had never been able to name in nine years of living with it: the look of a creature who has sat through the lecture and is waiting, not optimistically, to see whether anyone was listening.

*The Headmaster’s office, Hogwarts, the following Monday*

“Children,” said Albus Dumbledore, “are often kind to animals.”

“Albus.”

“And the Evans line was always strong. Lily could do things at eleven that I—”

“*Albus.*”

Minerva McGonagall had the cutting in her hand. She had read it standing, twice, and was now reading it a third time sitting down, which Dumbledore had observed over sixty years to be the sequence in which she received news she did not like. Behind her, Fawkes shifted on his perch and made a small enquiring sound, and the hundred small silver instruments on the side tables went on turning and puffing in their mild way, and the afternoon light came in long and gold across a desk that had Arabella Figg’s letter open on it, face up, read.

“He fixed her hands,” said McGonagall.

“So she writes.”

“Arabella Figg’s hands. I have known Arabella Figg since 1961 and she has not been able to hold a teacup in her right hand since the Grindelwald years, and a child of eight has mended them *on her kitchen floor*, and you are telling me about *animals*.”

“I am telling you,” said Dumbledore mildly, “what I believe. That the boy is extraordinarily gifted, as we had every reason to expect. That accidental magic in a strong child can take the shape of wishes, and a child who loves an old woman and her cats may wish them well with real force. That he has had no training and can have had no training, and what Arabella saw is a thing I have seen before in gifted children: the wild magic going where the heart points. It is not nothing, Minerva. I do not say it is nothing. I say it is not *yet* a thing to act upon.”

“He asked permission first.”

Dumbledore said nothing.

“He looked at her hands, and he *diagnosed* them, and he asked if he might hold them, and he held them until they were straight, and then he told her to be careful who she showed it to.” McGonagall put the letter down flat on the desk and her hand flat on the letter. “That is not a wish, Albus. I have taught a thousand first-years. I know what a wish looks like. That is a child who knows precisely what he is doing and has decided to let one person see it.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “I rather thought that too.”

She looked at him sharply. He had turned a little in his chair, towards the window, and the light caught the half-moon spectacles and hid what was behind them, which she had also observed over sixty years to be a thing he did at the exact moments he least wished to be read.

“Then why—”

“Because the wards hold.” He said it quietly, and she heard, under the mildness, the thing that was actually there, which was not complacency. It was the particular stillness of a man holding a door shut with his back against it and talking to you pleasantly over his shoulder. “Because he is eight years old and happy — Arabella says so, and Arabella has never in seven years told me he was happy before, and I find I set a great deal of store by it. Because the moment any of us walks up that garden path, the childhood ends: not the Dursleys’ version of it, which I do not pretend is good, but the ordinariness. The not-knowing. He has three more years in which nobody bows to him in a shop and means it—”

“Dedalus Diggle,” said McGonagall, “bowed to him in a shop. Arabella wrote to you about it. Two years ago.”

“—in which almost nobody bows to him in a shop,” said Dumbledore, and the corner of his mouth moved. “I know what is coming for him here, Minerva. I helped to arrange some of it. He will walk into this castle and be the most famous child in Britain before he has found his dormitory, and every face in the Great Hall will be waiting to see what he is, and whatever he is will have to carry that. I should like him to have a self to carry it with. One he made somewhere quiet, where nobody was watching.”

“Somebody *is* watching. We pay her ten galleons a month and keep her in cat food.”

“Somebody kind is watching, from two streets away, with instructions to report and not to interfere, and she has done exactly that, and done it well, and on her evidence the boy is thriving.” He turned back from the window. “I will not send a wizard to that house before his eleventh birthday. Not you, not Hagrid, not myself. I have thought about this for seven years, Minerva, and it is the one decision I made that autumn that I am still certain was right.”

McGonagall looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up the cutting again and looked at that instead, at the small boy holding a cup with both hands.

“What is a *county chess champion*,” she said.

“I confess I am not entirely sure. A county is a sort of large parish. Chess I believe you know.”

“I know *wizard’s* chess. The pieces argue.” She read the paragraph beneath the photograph for the fourth time. “*Moved up for his lessons. Examinations next summer.* He is eight. What examinations do Muggle children sit at eight?”

“None, as a rule. Which I imagine is why it is in the newspaper.”

“And this doesn’t trouble you.”

“It troubles me a great deal,” said Dumbledore. “Not for the reasons you mean. Muggle fame is a candle, Minerva; it burns bright in a small room and goes out the moment you open the door. When he comes here nobody will care that he passed a Muggle examination at eight. They will care about a night in Godric’s Hollow that he cannot remember. The candle is not the problem.” He took his spectacles off and polished them on his sleeve, which she had seen him do perhaps four times in sixty years. “The problem is what Arabella says at the end. That he has stopped hiding. That he was hiding a long time. That none of us has any idea what was under the lid.”

“Then *look*.”

“No.”

“Albus—”

“No. If I look, he will know he is looked at, and then he is not making a self any more, he is making a performance for the man who left him on the step. I will not do that to him. I did enough to him on that step.” He put the spectacles back on. The mildness came down over him again like a blind, but she had seen the window open and would not forget it. “I will write to Arabella. I will thank her, and tell her to go on exactly as she is, and to be kind to him, and above all not to let him see that she is afraid of him. Children can bear a great deal, Minerva, but not that. Not from the one person who was kind.”

McGonagall put the cutting down.

“She isn’t afraid of him,” she said. “Read it again. That is not what the letter says.”

He read it again. She watched him do it, the gold light moving on the desk, the instruments ticking, Fawkes very still, and she saw him arrive at the last paragraph and stop there, exactly as she had, three times.

*He is happier than I have ever seen him. That is what frightens me.*

“No,” said Dumbledore slowly. “No, I see. She is not afraid *of* him.”

“She is afraid *for* us,” said McGonagall. “Which is a very different thing, and in my experience the more sensible of the two.” She stood, and smoothed her robes, and looked at him with the face she kept for students who had done something clever and were about to be told it did not excuse them. “Three years, you say. I shall hold you to it. On his eleventh birthday I am delivering that letter myself, Albus, in person, by hand, and I am going to look at that child with my own eyes, and if he is what Arabella Figg thinks he is, I want it on record that I said so first.”

“Noted,” said Dumbledore gravely. “Shall I write it down?”

“*I* shall write it down,” said McGonagall, and did, in the margin of the cutting, in green ink, and left it on his desk, and went to teach the third years.

He sat a while after she had gone. The light moved. Fawkes sang two notes and stopped. On the desk, under McGonagall’s green margin — *M.M. said so first, Oct. ’88* — a small boy in a school jumper held a cup with both hands, carefully, as if he were carrying it for someone else, and looked out of the newsprint at the greatest wizard of the age with eyes that the photographer had got exactly right, and Albus Dumbledore, who had made a decision in 1981 that he was still certain was correct, looked back at them for a long time, and found that for once in his life he could not read what was behind them at all.

Then he took a fresh sheet and wrote to Arabella, and the letter said what he had told Minerva it would say, and at the end of it he added one line that he had not planned, and did not cross out.

*If he asks you what you are, tell him the truth. I suspect he already knows. I suspect that is the least of what he knows.*

---

*Wisteria Walk, the Thursday after*

Arabella Figg read the reply at the kitchen table with Mr Tibbles on the sugar bowl, and when she got to the last line she read it twice, and put the letter down, and looked at the cat.

“Well,” she said. “He heard that much.”

She made a fist of her right hand, and opened it, and made a fist again. Then she got up and put the kettle on, and took the good tin down from the top shelf, where it had been for seven years waiting for somebody worth opening it for, and checked how many were left for Saturday.
