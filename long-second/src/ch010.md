# Chapter 10 — Petunia’s Drawer

The first spell in *The Standard Book of Spells, Grade 1* was the one for making light, and he had read it on the train with Mrs Figg beside him, and he read it again that night under the stairs with the torch on and the house asleep, and halfway down the page he put the torch down, because he did not need it.

Not because he had done the spell. He had no wand, and the book was very clear that one needed a wand, in a tone that suggested the question had never seriously been raised. He put the torch down because he had read the description — *the Wand-Lighting Charm produces a steady light at the tip of the caster’s wand, sufficient to read by; the incantation is accompanied by a short upward flick* — and under the description, in the place where he had learned at four to look for the real meaning of a thing, he had found a shape he knew.

It was the candle.

Not exactly. The candle had been *fire*, a happening, a thing he had spent a month learning to bracket. This was *light*, a happening of a different family — but the spine was the same, the pointing and the pushing, and the bracket in the middle was a cousin of his own, a cleaner one, a bracket that someone had plainly spent a great deal of time sanding down. He could *see* it. He sat in the dark with the book on his knees and the words *Lumos* and *upward flick* on the page, and behind the words, the way the pub had been behind the record shop, he could see the sentence they stood for — and it was *his* sentence. A version of it. Written by somebody else, long ago, so well that it had been given a name and a wrist movement and put in a book for eleven-year-olds, who would say the name and do the movement and never once, in seven years of schooling, see the sentence underneath.

He had been reading their language off roses for a year, with no dictionary. Here was the dictionary. Seven volumes of it, bought with scone money, in a canvas bag at the foot of the camp bed.

He read *Grade 1* that night, all of it, every spell, and for each one he did the same thing: read the description, found the sentence under it, took it apart into the parts he had names for, and then — this was the part that kept him up till the boiler — compared. His bracket for *warm* against their bracket for *warm*. His *mend* against their *mend*, which they called *Reparo* and did in a way he found both clumsier and more general than his own, like a spanner against a key. Theirs were better, mostly. Not always. Theirs were *polished*, worn smooth by centuries of hands, and his were rough-cut from a kitchen floor, and where theirs were better it was the way a road is better than a path, and where his were better it was the way a path sometimes goes where the road can’t.

And in the margin of the thin grey book from 1911, where its author had written, in despair, *the present writer regrets that observation of the casting moment is beyond any human faculty*, Harry wrote, in pencil, in the round hand, small, the first margin of his own life:

*Not quite. — H.P., October 1987*

He slept two hours. It was, he thought in the morning, the best night since the pencil, and he had said that about several.

---

He read the seven grades in the week, which by his own clock was most of four years, and he spent the four years the way a man who has just been given a dictionary for a language he has been speaking alone spends them: finding out how much he had got right.

A great deal, it turned out. More than he had expected and less than he had hoped. The spine of every spell in seven volumes was the spine he had read off the roses — the pointing, the pushing — and he had that cold. The brackets were where the real work lived, and there were a hundred and sixty of them in the Standard Books, and he had nine, and he spent most of a subjective year simply *collecting*, the way he had collected spines in the Survey, until he had all hundred and sixty taken apart and laid out in his head where he could see, at once, what a civilisation had thought it worth sanding smooth over a thousand years.

They had warm and cold and light and dark. They had *move* in forty flavours. They had *mend* and *break* and *clean* and *lock* and *open*. They had a whole chapter, in *Grade 5*, on making things *not be noticed*, and he read it with the hand on the back of his head from the Charing Cross Road fresh in his mind and understood, with a pleasure that had him grinning alone in a cupboard at two in the morning, exactly how it had been built and exactly why it had not been built for him.

And they had almost nothing on *why*. That was the strange thing, and he turned it over for a long time. Seven volumes, a hundred and sixty spells, each with its name and its movement and its three paragraphs of *what to expect*, and not one line in any of them on what the sentence underneath was *made of*. Waffling tried. Waffling had two hundred pages of trying and Harry read them in an evening and found a decent man groping along a wall in the dark with a very good vocabulary, and he put a Mr Prentice margin on every third page, and the margins were mostly *surely not* and twice *yes* and once, on a page about why incantations in Latin should work at all, *he has nearly got it and doesn’t know*.

They told it with a stick and a word, Mrs Figg had said. The book was the word. The stick, he began to think, reading Waffling’s chapter on wands three times, was something more interesting than a symbol. Waffling called it a *focus* and spent nine pages failing to say what he meant, but Harry had spent a year shouting sentences across a field with nothing but his hand, and he knew what it cost, and he read between Waffling’s lines a thing that Waffling could not have known he was saying: that the stick was a *tool*. A specific one. A way of sending the sentence so that it arrived with most of its strength instead of a tenth. He had been shouting. They had a telephone.

He could not buy one. He had asked Mrs Figg, in the café, and she had gone still and said that a wand was the one thing she would not, could not, and that children were not sold them before their letters for very good reasons that were on the far side of a line she had already crossed once that month. He had not pushed. He had four years. He kept *telephone*, underlined, where he kept things, and went back to the sentences.

---

He asked his aunt about his mother on a Wednesday in November, with the kitchen window shut against the dark and the smell of the last of the bonfires coming in under it, and Vernon at a conference in Birmingham, and Dudley at Piers Polkiss’s for the night, and Petunia at the sink with her sleeves pushed up, washing a single cup.

He had made the tea. That was new — he had never made her tea in seven years; it was not a thing the house permitted — and she had looked at the cup when he put it down beside her, and then at him, and said nothing, and drunk it.

He had the facts. He had had them a month, off a page, on a bench, with a hand to hold; he had the date and the place and the name nobody said and the careful words. What he did not have was *her*. The woman on the swing — he had not seen the swing yet; he did not know there was a swing — the sister, the girl who had made a flower open and close in her hand. The reference books did not have her. The *Prophet* did not have her. There was one person in the world who did, and he had been managing her like weather for seven years and had never once tried the handle.

“Aunt Petunia,” said Harry. “Will you tell me about my mother? I know what happened to her. I read it. In a book, in the street Mrs Figg took me to. I know who, and when, and where. I don’t know *her*. You’re the only one who does.”

The cup went still in the water.

He watched the side of her face in the window’s reflection and saw it do what it had done at the cooker when he was two, the white coming up — and then, for the first time in his life, saw it *stop*. Hold. And slowly, like a tide going out that had been coming in for twenty years, go back down.

“I wondered,” said Petunia, “when you’d ask.”

“I didn’t want to frighten you.”

“No.” She took her hands out of the water and dried them on the tea towel, one finger at a time, the way she did when she was deciding something. “No. You never have. You’ve been very—” She stopped. “You’ve been very careful of me, haven’t you. Since you were two. I did notice.”

He said nothing. It was the most she had ever said to him that was not an instruction.

“Sit down,” said Petunia.

---

She told him at the kitchen table with the bonfire smell coming under the window and the garden gone black outside, and she did not tell it well, because she had never told it to anyone, and some of it came out in the wrong order and some of it came out as weather, and he sat very still and let it all come, in the long quiet, and arranged it afterwards.

There had been two girls in a town up north that she did not name, and the younger had been able to make a flower open and close in her hand, and the older had been able to make nothing at all. That was where it started, and in a sense that was all of it, and she said so, flatly, looking at her own hands on the table: “That’s all it ever was. She could and I couldn’t. People think there’s more. There isn’t.”

There had been a boy — “an awful boy, from the bad end of town, with hair that wanted washing” — who had known what the younger girl was before she did, and had told her, and the older girl had hated him for it with a completeness she could still feel, thirty years on, in her teeth.

There had been a letter, when the younger girl was eleven, on thick yellowish paper, in green ink. Petunia said the name of the school as if it were a stone she had carried in her mouth for twenty-five years: “Hogwarts.” And then, lower: “She went. Of course she went. And I wrote to them. Did you know that? I never told anyone that, not your uncle, nobody. I wrote to the headmaster, eleven years old, and asked if I could come too.”

“What did he say?”

“He said no.” She was looking out at the black garden. “He was kind about it. That was the worst part. He wrote back to a child of eleven in his own hand and he was *kind*. I’ve still got it. Somewhere.”

She gave the headmaster’s name. He knew it — it was in *Hogwarts: A History*, and in every account of the war, always with the same line beside it: *the only one he ever feared* — but he had never heard it said aloud, and he sat very still as she said it, because it was the name of the man Mrs Figg wrote to, and the man had just become a person who had once written kindly to a girl of eleven and told her no.

She told him about the summers, when the younger girl came home with her trunk and her wand and her new words, and the older girl had sat in her room with the door shut and heard them laughing downstairs. About how the younger girl had *tried*. “She tried. I’ll say that for her. She kept trying till the end and I wouldn’t have it.” About a wedding she had not gone to, and a christening she had not been asked to, and a card with a photograph of a baby with its father’s hair, which she had put in a drawer and had looked at, she admitted to the table, perhaps twice a year since.

And then there had been a night at the end of October, six years ago, and a man in a cloak on the doorstep at midnight, and a basket.

“There was a letter,” said Petunia. “In with you. From him.” Her hands had gone flat on the table, pressing down. “It said she was dead. Both of them. It said how — I didn’t understand half of it and I didn’t want to, there was a man, a *wizard*, a bad one, and he’d — and you’d — ” She stopped. “It said you’d lived. It said nobody knew why. And it said—”

She stopped again, for longer, and he waited, and understood that he had come to the drawer itself, the one that had been nailed shut his whole life, and that she was standing in front of it deciding, and that nothing he could say would help, so he said nothing.

“It said that as long as you lived under my roof,” said Petunia, very low, “there was a thing he’d done — a protection — that would hold. That you’d be safe here in a way you couldn’t be anywhere else. Because of her. Because she was my sister and I’d taken you in. It didn’t say from what. It didn’t need to. It said *as long as he calls this place home*.” She looked at Harry for the first time since she had sat down. “That’s why I sat by that lake. Did you think I didn’t know what I was doing? Nine days on a bench with a magazine I couldn’t read. I know what the university is. I know what the trains are. You’ve been walking out of this house an inch at a time since you were five and I’ve let you, every inch, because God knows there’s little enough here to hold anyone. But you sleep under this roof. You sleep under it until the letter comes. I promised her that. I never promised her anything else in her life and I promised her that, to a piece of paper, in the middle of the night, with you asleep in a basket on my step, and I have kept it.”

The kitchen was very quiet.

Harry sat in it and found that he could not, for a moment, say anything at all — and that the moment, which was long for anyone and for him was a working afternoon, was not the long quiet he lived in. It was something else. A stillness he had no name for and had never been inside, and he sat in it and looked at the woman across the table who had made his life a low grey misery for seven years and who had been keeping a promise the entire time, badly, grudgingly, in a house that hated it, to a sister she had not spoken to in a decade, with nobody to tell.

He had thought he had read her. He had read the fear. He had read the drawer. He had never once read *this*, because it had never occurred to him that there was anything in his aunt he could respect, and he sat with that and let it be true and let it be his fault, and then he put it down, because there was a thing to do, and the thing was in front of him.

“Thank you,” he said. “For keeping it.”

Petunia made a small sound that was not a laugh and not the other thing.

“Don’t,” she said. “Don’t you thank me. I have not been—” She shut her mouth on it. Then, after a while, to the table: “I have not been what I should. I know that. I’ve known it the whole time, and I’ve done it anyway, and I couldn’t have told you why, except that every time I looked at you I saw her at eleven with that letter in her hand, going somewhere I couldn’t follow. And then you turned out *clever*.” It came out almost as an accusation, and then wasn’t. “Clever like her. Cleverer. Fifteen papers. In the newspaper. A *university*. And I thought, there it is, there it goes again, and I couldn’t — I didn’t know how to—” She stopped. “You made me tea.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody in this house has made me a cup of tea in seventeen years.”

“I know,” said Harry. “I should have done it sooner. I was being careful of you. I think I was being careful of the wrong thing.”

She looked at him across the kitchen table in the dark, with the bonfire smell and the black window, and he watched a thing happen on her face that he had waited seven years to see without ever once letting himself expect it, and it was not love — he was not a fool — but it was the thing that lives next door to love and lends it sugar.

“You look like him,” she said. “Your father. The hair. But you’ve got her eyes, and you look at people the way she did. As if you were working out how to help and hadn’t decided whether they deserved it.” She stood up. “Wait there.”

---

She came back with a shoebox.

It was a different shoebox from his — hers was for a pair of court shoes, 1972, from a shop that no longer existed — and she put it on the table and took the lid off and did not look inside, and said, “I kept some things. I don’t know why. Don’t ask me why.”

He didn’t. He looked.

A letter, on thick yellowish paper, in a hand that slanted and was kind, beginning *Dear Miss Evans* and dated the summer of 1971. Another letter, heavier, newer, folded small, which she took out and put face-down under her hand before he could read more than the first word, and the first word was *Petunia*, and the hand was the same, and he let it go. A christening card with a photograph of a baby with black hair standing straight up, and a woman holding him, laughing, with red hair down her back, and the baby’s eyes were green and so were hers.

And under that, at the bottom, gone soft at the corners from a quarter-century of being not looked at twice a year, a photograph of two girls on a swing in a park. The older one pushing. The younger with her head back and her hair flying, in the middle of a shout of laughter you could hear through the paper, and the older one — eight, perhaps, or nine; a year older than he was now — looking at her with an expression that Harry recognised at once, because he had seen it on his aunt’s face that evening across the table and had only now understood what it was.

She had loved her. Before the flower, before the boy, before the letter in green ink. There it was, in black and white, in a shoebox, on a swing.

“You can have that one,” said Petunia, not looking. “The swing. I’ve got the rest.”

He took it. He held it the way he had held two sheets of paper in a school hall in August, with both hands, as if carrying it for someone else.

“Aunt Petunia. The letter. The one from the step. May I—”

“No.” It was quick, and then softer. “No. Not yet. There are things in it about that night you don’t need, and things about the protection I don’t understand, and I think — I think he’d want to tell you himself. The headmaster. When they send for you.” She put the lid back on the box. “Ask him. Ask him why he left you with me. He had his reasons. I’ve had six years to think about whether they were good ones and I still don’t know, and I’d like to hear him say.”

“I will,” said Harry.

“And Harry.” She had never used his name as the start of a sentence either. It was a night for it. “When you go. When the letter comes and you go off up north with your trunk and your — all of it. Come back in the summers.” She said it to the shoebox. “Not for me. I shan’t pretend. For the roof. The paper said *as long as he calls this place home*, and I don’t know how long that’s meant to hold, and I’m not having it fall down for want of you sleeping here six weeks a year.” A pause. “And your uncle will want to tell the Rotary.”

It was so exactly her, and so exactly not, that he laughed — a real one, out loud, in that kitchen, for the first time in his life — and after a second, to the shoebox, so did she.

---

He lay under the stairs that night with the photograph of the swing propped against the wall beside the date, and he did not open the Standard Books, and he did not turn the television on, and he went back through the evening once, slowly, and then put it on the shelf where he kept the things that had made him who he was, in the place of honour, next to the straight fingers.

*As long as he calls this place home.* A protection, left on a doorstep with a baby and a warming charm, that depended on the roof of the one person in the world least willing to hold it up, and who had held it up anyway, for six years, with her jaw set, for a sister on a swing. He had felt the edge of it on the step some nights, going out for the milk, a faint hum in the stone like the street in the Alley turned down to nothing; he had thought it was the house. It was her. It had been her the whole time.

He had been so sure the Dursleys were weather. He had built his whole childhood on it. And weather had turned out, at a kitchen table in the dark, to have kept a promise.

And he had a new rule, and he said it, in a whisper, in the dark, under the swing, because the evening had earned a thing said:

“I don’t lie to Aunt Petunia. Not any more. She has kept a thing for six years that she did not have to keep. The least I owe her is the truth when she asks for it, and the truth when she doesn’t.”

He looked at the swing for a long time.

Three years and eight months. He was seven, and he had the facts off a page and now, at last, the person off a face, and seven volumes of a dictionary in a bag at the foot of the bed, and a photograph of his mother laughing, and an aunt who had made him laugh, and a hundred and sixty brackets taken apart in his head, and a protection on the roof above him that he now understood well enough to respect.

He was going to need every one of them.

---

“You made her tea, I suppose,” said Mrs Figg, in the café, on the Thursday, when he had told her — not all of it; some of it was Petunia’s and would stay so — but the shape.

“How did you—”

“Because that’s what you do, dear. You find the door they already use.” She poured, right-handed. “Your mother was the same. Everyone said.” She drank. “And I shall tell you something now that I shouldn’t, because you’ve had a week of being told things and one more won’t hurt. I’ve lived two streets from that woman for six years, Harry, and I’d not have put a knut on her. Not one. I owe her an apology I shan’t ever be able to make, because she’d take it as an insult, and she’d be right.” She set the cup down. “Keep the swing somewhere safe.”

“It’s on the wall,” said Harry. “Next to the date.”

“Good,” said Arabella Figg. “That’s about where it belongs.”
