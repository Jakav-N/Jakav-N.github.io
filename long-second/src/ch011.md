# Chapter 11 — Nine Papers

The invigilator checked his candidate number three times, and then went and fetched the deputy head to check it a fourth, and Harry sat at the front desk of the Stonewall High sports hall with his two pens and his ruler laid out parallel and waited, politely, to be allowed to sit a mathematics examination.

“It’s right,” said the deputy head, who had a list. “Potter, H. Front row, desk one. County arrangement.” She looked down at him over the list with the expression of a woman who had read the memo and had not, until this moment, entirely believed it. “Good luck, Harry.”

“Thank you, Mrs Okafor.”

Behind him, a hundred and forty sixteen-year-olds in various states of terror shuffled and coughed. He could hear Marcus, four rows back, breathing like a man about to go over the top. Rachel was somewhere to his left; she had squeezed his shoulder on the way in and whispered, “Don’t show us up,” which was the kindest thing anyone had said to him all week. Outside, through the high windows, it was a bright June morning in 1989 and the cricket pitch was being mown, and the smell of cut grass came in over the smell of floor polish and fear.

“You may begin,” said the invigilator.

A hundred and forty papers turned over with a sound like a flock lifting off a field.

Harry turned his, and read it, and that took eleven seconds, and then he had a problem he had been thinking about for months and had never quite solved, and the problem was this: an hour and a half.

Ninety minutes, at two hundred to one, was three hundred hours. Twelve and a half days, sitting at a desk, in a room where he was not permitted to read, or move, or take out the blue notebook, or do anything whatever but the paper in front of him — a paper he could complete, at the speed of his hand, in perhaps twenty-five minutes, and that was being generous to his handwriting, which had never been fast and never would be. He had sat tests before. He had never sat one with *rules*, where the end of the paper was not the end of the sitting, and he had spent some of the spring genuinely worried about what twelve days in a sports hall would do to him.

He had decided, in the end, to treat it as a gift. Nobody else in his life had ever given him twelve days with nothing to do but think, and an obligation to look as if he were doing nothing.

So he did the paper. He did it carefully, at the speed of his hand, showing all working, because the mark scheme would want the working and because Mr Prentice’s margins had taught him that writing for someone slower was a courtesy, not a chore. He checked it twice. It took twenty-eight minutes by the sports hall clock. Then he laid the pen down parallel to the ruler, and folded his hands on the desk, and looked at a point on the far wall just above the badminton net, and went inside.

He spent the first day of it on the mathematics pads — he was up to the fourteenth, and there was a chapter he had been putting off about a thing he’d found in the structure of the roses’ sentence that looked, from a certain angle, like a thing from the group theory lectures, and he wanted to see whether the resemblance held or was only his wishing. It held, as far as he could take it without paper. He took it a long way. By the time the invigilator walked past his desk for the third time, glanced down at the folded hands and the finished paper and the small boy gazing peacefully at the badminton net, and walked on with the face of a man who has decided not to have an opinion, Harry had filled in his head the rough shape of a chapter that would take him a month of evenings to write down, and was happy.

The second day he spent on the people. A hundred and forty of them, breathing. He had never been in a room with so many minds working so hard at once, and he could hear them — not the thoughts, nothing like that, but the *weather* of them: the pens that stopped and started, the sighs, the boy at the back who was going to fail and knew it and kept turning to the last page as if a different question might have appeared there. He minded about that boy, properly, for most of an hour of his own time, and nothing came, because there was nothing to mend; the boy simply hadn’t done the work, and the thing in Harry had no sentence for that, and he filed the fact under *limits* and moved on.

The third day he thought about his mother. He let himself. He had very little of her — a word in a dream, a sentence from Marge, *your mum especially, everyone said* from a woman on a garden bench — and he had learned to be careful with how often he took it out, because it did not wear well, and because there was a kind of thinking about her that was really thinking about himself. But he was sitting in an examination she had never sat, in a world she had left, being the clever one, and he found he wanted to tell her, the way he had wanted to tell the dream about the pencil. *I’m doing the papers, Mum. Nine of them. The man said I should let them see, and I’m letting them.* The badminton net did not reply. He hadn’t expected it to.

“Pens down,” said the invigilator. “Stop writing now, please.”

He had been sitting with his pen down for sixty-two minutes, which was most of a fortnight, and had not been bored for one second of it, and he thought, standing up into the flock-noise of a hundred and forty people exhaling, that he had learned something that morning that no examination board had a grade for: that he could do this. That he could sit in a room where the rules said *nothing* and fill the nothing himself, for twelve days, out of his own head, and come out the far end having done good work and thought about a boy at the back and said a thing to his mother. The sports hall had been the playpen. He had been afraid of the playpen since he was two. He wasn’t any more.

Marcus found him in the corridor, grey-faced.

“How was it?”

“Fine,” said Harry. “Question seven’s a trap. The second part wants you to notice the first part was a special case.”

Marcus closed his eyes. “Right,” he said. “Right. Good. Brilliant. Eight more of these.” He opened them. “You’re enjoying this.”

“Yes,” said Harry, and then, because it was Marcus and it was true: “I’ve never had anything to pass before.”

---

He sat nine. Mathematics, two papers; the three sciences; English language and English literature, which had worried the county more than they had worried him; history; French, because Stonewall taught it and he had taught himself the rest from a *Teach Yourself* in the branch and three months of Radio 4’s French service in the small hours; and geography, which he had taken largely for the maps.

The summer in between was the strangest of his life, because for the first time since the doorstep he was *waiting* for something and could do nothing whatever to hurry it, and he found, to his interest, that he minded far less than he had expected. The results would come in August. Until then there were the roses, and the blue notebook, and the mathematics pads, and the Open University’s summer schedule, and Mrs Figg’s garden on Saturdays, where she had taken to leaving a book out on the bench — never a wizarding book, she was careful about that, but books *about* the world he was going to: a history of Scotland, a field guide to owls, once a very old road atlas with a page folded down at a stretch of the north where there was, as far as the atlas knew, nothing at all. She never said anything about the books. He read them and put them back where he’d found them and said nothing either. It was a conversation, and it was going very well.

He turned nine on the last day of July. Nobody at Number Four marked it, which was the custom; but when he came back from the library that afternoon there was a parcel on his camp bed in brown paper with no name on it, and inside was a blue fountain pen — a good one, old, with a lever — and a card that said only *for the pads. E.P.*, and he sat on the bed and held it for a long time by his own clock, and then he wrote the date on the next clean page of the fourteenth pad with it, and under the date, *two years*.

---

Results came on a Thursday.

Stonewall gave them out in the hall, in envelopes, from a table with a tablecloth on it, and the sixth-form corridor was full of sixteen-year-olds opening theirs and screaming or going very quiet, and Harry stood in the middle of it, up to most people’s waists, and waited his turn at the P’s with his hands in his pockets.

Rachel had got there first. She came out of the scrum with her envelope open and her face lit up like a window, and saw him, and bent down and showed him — seven, two of them A’s, enough for the sixth form she wanted — and he said “Rachel, that’s *wonderful*,” and meant every syllable, and she laughed and hugged him, briefly, in the way of a girl who has just stopped being a child and has decided to be kind about it, and went to find her mother. Marcus came next. Marcus had a C in maths, which was the one he had needed and the one he had been sure he would fail, and he stood in the corridor looking at the letter C for a long time, and then at Harry, and said, “Question seven,” and Harry said, “Question seven,” and Marcus put his big hand on top of Harry’s head for a second, the way you’d steady yourself on a gatepost, and said, “Cheers, mate,” and that was all, and it was a great deal.

Then it was his turn.

Mr Haines was behind the table. He had taken the P’s on purpose; Harry could see it in the way he was sitting. He found the envelope without looking, and held it a moment, and did not hand it over.

“I’ve known since Monday,” he said. “They telephone the school first. I’ve been teaching thirty-one years, Potter, and I have never once wanted to open someone else’s results, and I have had this in my desk since Monday and I have not opened it, and I should like that noted.”

“Noted, sir.”

Mr Haines gave him the envelope. Harry opened it standing up, with the hall roaring round him, and read it, which took about a second, and then stood holding it, and the second went on for a very long time.

Nine A’s. The highest grade there was. In every subject, both maths papers, the sciences, the two Englishes, history, French, geography. A single sheet of paper with his name at the top and nine letters down the side, all the same letter, and it was the first piece of paper in his entire life, from the outside world, that said what he was.

He had expected to feel something large. He felt, instead, something quite small and quite precise, like a key turning. *They can see.* That was all. Nine years of being the only one who knew, and now there was a certificate.

Mr Haines was watching his face.

“Well?”

Harry turned the sheet round and held it up.

Mr Haines looked at it. He took his glasses off and looked at it again. Then he put the glasses back on and sat back in his chair behind the table with the tablecloth and said, quite quietly, so that only Harry heard it under the roar, “Thirty-one years,” and then, a little louder, in the voice he used for the fourth years when they had done something right and he did not want to make too much of it: “Yes. Well. I did say the moments one was straightforward.”

“You did, sir.”

“Go and telephone your uncle, Potter. Before the newspapers do.”

---

The newspapers did it anyway.

The *Advertiser* was Thursday evening, because Mrs Achebe clicked her pen. The nationals were Saturday, because the *Advertiser* had a stringer, and because a boy of nine with nine A’s was the sort of thing that filled the bottom of page five in the dead week of August when nothing else was happening. SURREY BOY, 9, PASSES NINE GCSEs — YOUNGEST EVER. Three of them ran it. One of them ran the Guildford photograph again, the cup and the school jumper, and called him a *prodigy*, and the word sat on the kitchen table at Number Four for a whole weekend being read aloud by Vernon in a voice that got slightly deeper each time.

Vernon, that weekend, was a man transfigured. Harry had seen him pleased before — the Rotary, the chess, the funeral suit in Mrs Bell’s office — but this was a different order of thing, and he watched it with the attention of a naturalist who has finally seen the rare bird at the top of the tree. His uncle moved differently. He answered the telephone before the second ring. He said *my nephew* to the man from the garage, to Mrs Next Door, to Marge — twice to Marge, who had rung back to make sure she had heard right, and had been told, in a voice that Harry would remember for the rest of his life with something close to affection, that she had heard perfectly well the first time, and that the Dursleys had *always* encouraged him, and that if she wanted to say anything further about bad blood she could say it to the *Daily Mail*.

Petunia said nothing, which was what Petunia said. But on the Saturday morning, at the till at the greengrocer’s, she opened her purse to pay, and Harry, standing with the basket at knee height as he had stood at that same till since he was three, saw inside it, folded small behind the stamps, a square of newsprint with a photograph on it, and looked away before she could see him seeing.

He never mentioned it. He never would. He put it on the shelf with the straight fingers and the garden bench and the margins, in the place where he kept the things that had been done for him quietly by people who would have denied it, and the shelf was getting, he noticed, rather full.

---

He gave one interview, by design, and it was the most boring interview of 1989, and he was prouder of it than of the nine A’s.

The nationals wanted television. He said no, through Mrs Bell, who was delighted to say it for him — the county had opinions about television and nine-year-olds, and so did Harry, who had sat on a lounge carpet at one in the morning for a year watching what the box did to people who stood in front of it. He had no intention of being a candle. But a newspaper interview was a different animal: it could be *managed*, if you were patient and dull and had rehearsed every answer for a fortnight, and it bought something he wanted, which was to be a *known thing*. A filed thing. A boy in a drawer marked *prodigy, Surrey, polite* that the world could stop wondering about.

So he sat in Mrs Bell’s office with the orange squash, with Mrs Bell behind her desk and Vernon in the funeral suit in the good chair, and a young man from the *Telegraph* with a notebook and a tape recorder asked him what it was like, and he told him, in sentences a nine-year-old might plausibly produce, that he liked reading, and that the school had been very kind, and that his uncle and aunt had always encouraged him. He said he wanted to go to university one day. He said his favourite subject was mathematics because you could always check whether you were right. He said, when asked about the chess, that he had been very lucky in his opponents. Vernon beamed. The young man from the *Telegraph* wrote it all down and visibly despaired of the whole business, and Harry watched the despair with deep satisfaction.

He had planted exactly one line, and he planted it at the end, where the young man asked — because they always asked, he had read enough of these to know — who he would like to thank.

“Mrs Prentice at the library,” said Harry, “who held the door. And Mrs Figg, who minds me on Saturdays, and has kept an eye on me for a very long time. I don’t think I’d have done any of it without them.”

It went in. He checked, the following Tuesday, on page nine of the *Telegraph*, under a headline about a *modest prodigy*. It was a perfectly ordinary thing for a child to say. It would mean nothing to anyone who read it on this side of the curtain. And if anyone on the far side read Muggle newspapers — he did not know whether they did; he suspected the kind man did; he suspected the kind man read everything — then somewhere in Scotland, or wherever such men sat, a person who had asked Arabella Figg to watch over a small boy had just been thanked for it, in the *Telegraph*, by name, by the boy, who had known about the watching for years and had decided to say so in print where everyone could see and no one could understand.

It was the nearest thing to a letter he could send. He hoped it had arrived.

---

The money came last, and came strangely, and taught him a great deal about what the ordinary world did when it decided to be kind to a child.

A man called Pargeter telephoned Mrs Bell in September. He was a solicitor, in Guildford, and he represented a client who had read page five of the *Mail* and wished to do something about it, and the something, when it had been explained to Vernon across Mrs Bell’s desk at a meeting Harry was permitted to attend in silence, turned out to be a trust. An educational trust, in Harry’s name, funded to a sum that made Vernon sit very still in the funeral suit, administered by Mr Pargeter’s firm, to pay for books, tuition, examination fees, travel to and from any place of learning, and *such other expenses as the trustees shall in their discretion deem conducive to the beneficiary’s education*, until the beneficiary attained the age of twenty-one.

“The trustees,” said Vernon, after a moment, “being—”

“Myself,” said Mr Pargeter, “and my partner, Mrs Hale. Our client was specific on that point. He felt — forgive me, Mr Dursley — that the funds should be kept quite separate from the household, so that the family should never be put to the awkwardness of seeming to benefit. It’s a standard provision.” He said this in a voice as smooth as a bowling green, and Harry, watching his uncle’s face work through the discovery that a large sum of money had arrived in the family and could not be *touched*, understood that it was not a standard provision at all, that Mrs Achebe had clicked her pen somewhere in the background of this, that Mr Pargeter had met Vernon Dursley before he had met Vernon Dursley, and that kind people, when they chose to be, could be quite as careful as he was.

Vernon signed. He had to; the alternative was explaining to the Rotary why he had refused a scholarship. He signed with the expression of a man accepting a medal he was not allowed to wear.

“Who is he?” Harry asked Mr Pargeter at the door, when Vernon had gone ahead to the car. “The client.”

“I’m afraid I can’t say.”

“Can you tell him thank you?”

Mr Pargeter looked down at him from a great height, with a face that gave away nothing at all, and then it gave away a very small amount at one corner.

“I can tell him,” he said, “that you asked. I rather think that will do.”

He never did find out. He had theories; he had a shortlist; he suspected, on the basis of nothing but the smoothness of Mr Pargeter’s voice and the timing and the fact that the sum had been *exactly* enough, that it was a man who had made a fortune in something dull and had once been a boy at a kitchen table whom nobody had seen. He hoped so. He hoped the man had read page five and put the paper down and thought: *there you are*, the way Mrs Prentice had, and picked up the telephone. He hoped it had felt as good as the straight fingers.

He had money now. Not his to spend; but *there*, in Guildford, in a solicitor’s discretion, for books and fees and travel to any place of learning, and the far side of the curtain had shops, and streets, and a bank — Mrs Figg had let that one slip, in the garden, in July, and he had filed it the way he filed everything, and had not yet taken it out to look at.

He took it out now, lying under the stairs in September with nine A’s on the sideboard in a frame of their own and two years on the plan, and looked at it for a long time.

*Travel to and from any place of learning.*

He was nine. He had learned to read off cereal boxes and to write in a language nobody spoke and to sit for twelve days in a sports hall without going mad. He had two years before a letter came from a school in Scotland. And he found, lying there, that he did not intend to spend them waiting for the letter to tell him where the shops were.
