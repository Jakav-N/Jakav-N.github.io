# Chapter 12 — Professor Carrow

The letter from the university came to Mrs Bell, not to Privet Drive, because Mrs Achebe had learned.

It was from the mathematics department at Royal Holloway, which was a college of the University of London that happened, by the kind of luck Harry had stopped believing in and started arranging, to sit in a park at Egham, eleven miles up the line. The head of department had read page five. The head of department would be interested in meeting the boy. The head of department wished to make clear that this was not an offer of anything, that the college did not take students of nine, that there were no precedents and the department was not in the business of creating them, and that she would nonetheless be in her office on Thursday afternoons until Christmas and would see him for an hour if the school and the family thought it appropriate.

The letter was signed *H. Carrow, Professor*, in a hand that looked as if it had been written at speed by someone who disliked writing letters, and Mrs Bell read it out across her desk with the orange squash and the funeral suit present and the county’s Mrs Achebe on the telephone extension, and when she had finished there was a short silence in which Harry could hear his uncle working out how to say *University of London* at the Rotary.

“The trust,” said Mr Pargeter, who was also on the extension, in the bowling-green voice, “would regard a weekly train to Egham as travel to a place of learning. If that is of any assistance.”

“He’s nine,” said Petunia.

It was the first thing she had said in any of these meetings, in eighteen months of them, and the room turned to look at her, and she sat in her gloves with her hands in her lap and looked at the letter on the desk, and Harry — who had spent nine years learning to read the side of her face — saw that it was not the fear, this time. It was the other thing. The thing under the fear, which he had seen once, at the sink, the night of Mrs Bell’s first letter. It was a woman watching a train go away.

“He’ll go with me the first time,” she said. “To see where it is. After that he can go alone. He’s sensible.” She said *sensible* as if it were a word she had found in a drawer and was surprised to find still fitted.

So on the first Thursday in October, Harry Potter and his aunt took the train to Egham, and sat opposite each other for twenty-two minutes without speaking, and walked up through a park full of enormous trees going gold to a building that looked like a French château that had been ordered from a catalogue, and Petunia stood at the bottom of the steps in her good coat and looked up at it for a long moment, and said, “Well. Go on, then,” and did not come in.

He understood that too. He was getting better at it.

---

Professor Carrow’s office was on the second floor at the end of a corridor that smelled of radiators and had chalk dust in the air you could see when the sun came through. The door was open. He knocked anyway.

“Potter. Come in. Sit anywhere there isn’t paper.”

There was nowhere there wasn’t paper. He moved a stack from a chair to the floor with care and sat. Professor Carrow did not get up. She was somewhere in her fifties, grey-haired, in a jumper with a hole at one elbow and reading glasses pushed up on her head, and she had a face like a good sharp knife kept for best, and she was looking at him across a desk buried in offprints with an expression he recognised at once, because he had seen it on Dr Prosser and on Mrs Prentice and on no one else in his life: a professional, declining to be impressed until given cause.

“I have read your newspaper cuttings,” she said. “They are very nice. They tell me you can pass a GCSE. So can three hundred thousand people a year. I’m told you also play chess, which I don’t, and that you’re polite, which is a thing I find in a mathematician roughly as useful as a nice singing voice.” She took the glasses down off her head and put them on. “So I am not going to test you, because I don’t know what I’d be testing. I’m going to give you a problem. It is a real problem — small, but real, which is to say nobody has written down the answer, including me. You take it away, and if you can do anything with it you come back next Thursday and show me, and if you can’t, you come back next Thursday and tell me exactly where you got stuck, which is a great deal more useful than most people think. Yes?”

“Yes,” said Harry. And then — because he had decided, on the train, that he was done being small in any room on either side of a curtain — “May I show you something first?”

The knife-face sharpened slightly. “Go on.”

He took the pads out of his bag. Not all of them; there were eighteen now, and the bag had a strap that cut. He had brought the first six, the ones that went from the beginning — the rebuilding of arithmetic under the stairs — up to the place where the group theory came in, and he put them on the one clear corner of the desk in order, and said:

“I started writing these last year. They’re what I worked out before I had books, mostly, done again properly so that somebody else could follow it. I’ve never shown them to anyone. I’d like you to be the first, if you’re willing, because you’re the first person I’ve met who I think could find the mistakes.”

Professor Carrow looked at the six pads. She looked at him. Then she picked up the first one, and opened it, and began to read.

He had thought a great deal about what this moment would be like and had prepared himself for most of the possibilities, including the one where she read a page and closed it and was kind. He had not prepared himself for what actually happened, which was that she read, and went on reading, and the hour went by, and the sun moved across the chalk dust, and she did not look up, and he sat in the chair with his hands in his lap and watched a grown woman read his handwriting with a pencil in her hand and a line between her brows, turning pages at the speed of someone who was *checking*, and it was, he would think later, the longest he had ever gone without being bored in a room where nothing was being said.

At one point she stopped, and went back two pages, and forward again, and wrote something in the margin.

At a quarter to five she closed the third pad and sat back and took her glasses off and rubbed her eyes, and looked at him across the desk, and the knife-face had not softened at all, which he found he was glad of.

“Potter,” she said. “Three questions. Answer them straight.”

“Yes.”

“Who taught you this?”

“Nobody. Books, after I was five. Before that, nobody. I had one textbook and a cupboard.”

“Who helped you write it down?”

“Nobody. A dead man’s margins, a bit. Mrs Prentice bought the pads.”

“And what is on page forty-one of the second pad?”

He had the page on the shelf; he had every page. He looked at it. “The part about the two kinds of infinity. Where I show the one is bigger than the other.”

“Yes. Well, it’s wrong.”

He felt it go through him like the ice. Not the fall — the *gear*. The world going slow and vast and bright.

“Where?”

“Fourth line from the bottom. You assume the list can be made and then build the thing that isn’t on it, which is right, that’s Cantor, you’ve got Cantor — but you’ve let the thing you build have two names in your notation and you haven’t noticed, and so you’ve proved slightly more than you meant to, which in this case means you’ve proved nothing. It’s a famous slip. Half the first-years make it. It took a hundred years to find a way round it that holds.” She put the glasses back on. “The rest of pad two is correct, as far as I can see at speed, and pad three is correct, and the thing you do at the end of pad three with the groups is not in any textbook I know and I should like to think about it. But page forty-one is wrong, and you asked for someone who could find the mistakes, and I have found one, and I am telling you to your face. How does that feel?”

He had been waiting since a man in a brown jacket at one in the morning for someone to do exactly this on purpose, and he sat in Professor Carrow’s office with the sun going and the radiators ticking and found he had to take a moment before he could answer, because the honest answer was absurd.

“It feels wonderful,” he said.

Professor Carrow looked at him for a long time. Then one corner of the knife-face did something it had clearly not done in a while.

“Yes,” she said. “It does, doesn’t it. Nobody ever believes me about that.” She pushed the six pads to one side of the desk, carefully, into a stack of their own. “I shall keep these, if I may, and read the rest properly, which will take me some weeks because I am not you and I have a department. Bring the others next time. And the problem.” She took a sheet from a drawer, wrote on it for a minute in the fast hand that disliked writing, and held it out. “It’s about colouring. Don’t let that fool you.”

He took it. At the door he stopped.

“Professor. You said you don’t know what you’d be testing.”

“I did.”

“Do you now?”

She considered him across the paper with the glasses on, the way she might have considered a proof that had turned out to have more in it than the first page suggested.

“I know that a child of nine has written a textbook,” said Professor Carrow, “and that some of chapter nine is wrong, and that he was *pleased* when I told him. I know that I have taught for twenty-six years and have never once seen that face on a student of any age. And I know that I should very much like to see what happens to the colouring problem.” She picked up her pencil. “Shut the door behind you, Potter. There’s a draught. Thursday.”

---

He did the colouring problem on the train.

Not all of it — it was a real problem, she had told the truth about that, and the full thing had a floor he couldn’t find — but a piece. A corner of it, the way he had found a corner of Goldbach: a special case where the colours behaved, and a reason why, and a sketch of why the reason stopped working when the case stopped being special. It took him twenty-two minutes, which was the length of the line from Egham to Little Whinging, which was, in the hours he lived in, nearly three days, and he spent them with his forehead against the window and the gold trees going by, and when he got off he had it, and he wrote it up that night in the fountain pen in the round hand for a reader who was slow and had a department, and on the Thursday he put it on the one clear corner of Professor Carrow’s desk, and she read it standing, and did not sit down for some time.

“Where did that come from?” she said at last.

He had known she would ask. He had thought all week about what to say, and the truth was not available — *from a thousand years, Professor, give or take* — and he had promised himself on a garden bench in June that he was done with lies, so he gave her the part of the truth that would fit through the door.

“I had a long train journey,” he said, “and nothing else to think about.”

Professor Carrow looked at the sheet, and at him, and he watched her decide — the way he had watched Dr Prosser decide, the way he had watched Mrs Figg — to take the answer she had been given and not ask for the rest of it. It was a thing adults did, he was finding. The good ones. They looked at you and saw there was a door you weren’t going to open, and instead of pushing they stood a little to one side of it, so you’d know where they were if you ever came out.

“Twenty-two minutes,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Hm.” She sat down. She pulled a pad towards her. “Then let’s see if we can’t find the floor between us. I’ve been stuck on the general case since 1983 and I am sick of the sight of it.”

---

That was the autumn. Thursdays, the train, the château in the park, two hours in a room that smelled of chalk and radiators with a woman who did not call him a prodigy — never once, not in two years, and he noticed, and he loved her for it in the exact and particular way one loves the one person who treats one as ordinary. She called him Potter until the Thursday she found the second mistake, in pad eleven, and after that she called him Harry, and he never knew whether she had noticed the change herself.

She taught him what research *was*. Not the mathematics — he had most of the mathematics, and where he didn’t, the library had it, and he had the library now. The *shape* of the thing. That there were journals, and that the journals were a conversation, slow, in print, between people who would mostly never meet, going back a hundred years and more; that you did not just solve a problem, you found out who had tried, and how, and where they had stuck, and you joined the conversation at the place where it had gone quiet. That the first thing a real mathematician did with a result was try to kill it. That you wrote for the person who would read you in fifty years, not the one who would read you on Thursday. That *of course* was indeed where the bodies were buried; she laughed for a full ten seconds when he told her about page 141, which was the first time he heard her laugh, and said, “Everyone has a page 141, Harry. Mine was in a book by my own supervisor. I was twenty-three before I dared tell him.”

And she gave him the library.

He had asked for it in the second week, in one sentence, the way he had asked for Stonewall’s, and she had looked at him over the glasses and said, “The departmental card is for staff and research students,” and he had said nothing, and waited, and she had said, “Oh, for heaven’s sake,” and written a note, and he had walked across the park in the rain with the note in his pocket to a building with a copper roof gone green, and a woman at a desk had read the note and looked at him and read it again and given him a card with his name on it, and he had walked in.

It was not a branch. It was not Stonewall. It was a university library: six floors, a hundred and twenty thousand volumes, and the journals — rows and rows of them, bound by the year, going back to before his grandparents were born, the whole slow conversation of a century shelved in order and waiting — and he stood on the ground floor of it with the card in his hand and the rain on the copper roof and felt, for the first time since the doorstep, the thing he had been wrong about his whole life.

He had thought the hunger was *him*. A fact about his engine. Two hundred to one, and no engine runs that fast on a water glass, and he had taken the hunger for a feature of his own machinery, like the slow hands, like the glasses.

It wasn’t. It had been the *shelves*. It had only ever been the shelves. He stood in a building with enough on its shelves to feed even him, and the hunger that had sounded like the hall clock for nine years went quiet, all at once, as if someone had reached into the hall and stopped the pendulum with a finger, and in the silence where it had been he could hear the rain, and the lift, and his own breath, and nothing else at all.

He read it. Of course he read it. Thursdays, and then — Carrow wrote a second note, Mrs Achebe clicked her pen, the trust paid for a season ticket — Tuesdays as well, and then any day the sixth form had no need of him, which was most days, because the A-level course the county had built for him was a formality he could have sat in October and was sitting in June for the look of the thing. He went up on the train alone with Petunia’s *sensible* in his pocket like a ticket and sat in the journals till the library closed, and came home in the dark through Little Whinging with his bag cutting his shoulder and his head full, properly full, for the first time in his life, and lay under the stairs with the boiler whooshing and did not turn the television on, some nights, because for once there was nothing left over to need it for.

The hunger had not been him. He wrote that down, in English, on the last page of the eighteenth pad, in the blue pen, and he underlined it, which he almost never did.

*It was never me. It was only ever the shelves.*

---

In November, in the garden at Wisteria Walk, with the last of the leaves coming down and the cats under blankets on the bench, Mrs Figg asked him how the university was.

“Big,” said Harry. “The library’s a hundred and twenty thousand books.”

“Is it, now.” She was pruning, right-handed, with the secateurs she had used left-handed for forty years. “And is that enough for you, dear? A hundred and twenty thousand?”

He thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how to think about anything by then.

“For now,” he said. “For this side.”

Mrs Figg pruned a stem, and looked at it, and dropped it in the trug.

“The other side has a library too, you know,” she said, to the geraniums. “At the school. They say it’s the biggest there is. I never saw it, of course.” A pause, and a stem. “Not as big as a hundred and twenty thousand, I shouldn’t think. But then the books are different. Some of them bite.”

He stood in the November garden with the cats asleep and his hands in his pockets and a season ticket to Egham in his coat and turned that over.

Twenty months.

“I’ll be careful,” he said.

“You be careful,” agreed Arabella Figg, and the secateurs went snip, and a leaf came down between them, slowly, the way everything did.
