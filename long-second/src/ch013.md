# Chapter 13 — Finals

Mrs Osei said *not like that* for the last time on a Thursday in February, and then she said nothing for a long moment, and then she said, “Go on, then,” and stepped back from the bench, and that was how he knew.

It was a small thing. The whole of physics, he had come to understand in a year of Thursdays in Dr Lindqvist’s room, was built out of small things done carefully by people who were not in a hurry, and this was one of them: a measurement, of a quantity that everyone knew to three decimal places and nobody had bothered to take to four, because the fourth did not matter to anything anyone was building. It mattered to Harry because he had been reading the journals back to 1960 and had found, in a paper from 1974, a footnote in which a man had wondered whether the fourth place might not be quite what the third predicted, and had not had the equipment, and had moved on. Nobody had gone back. The equipment was in Dr Lindqvist’s room. Mrs Osei knew how to work it. He had asked her, in November, whether a thing could be *set up*, and she had looked at him for a long time and said, “Show me the paper.”

It took four months, Thursdays. It took him learning to do with his hands what he could already do in his head, which turned out to be a different thing entirely and a humbling one, and it took Mrs Osei saying *not like that* in eleven different tones of voice and once, memorably, *not like that, Harry, unless you want to be the first eight-year-old in this department to lose an eyebrow*. It took the man from 1974 being, in the end, right: the fourth place was not quite what the third predicted, by an amount too small to matter to anything anyone was building and too large to be nothing.

“Well,” said Dr Lindqvist, when he had looked at the numbers three times with his arms folded. “Well, damn it. Somebody should have done that fifteen years ago.”

“Somebody did,” said Harry. “He didn’t have the room.”

The paper was two pages. It went to a journal that printed short things quickly, and it had three names on it — Lindqvist, Osei, Potter, alphabetical, which Carrow approved of — and when it came back accepted in May, Mrs Osei pinned the acceptance letter to the noticeboard in the lab with her name on it in print for the first time in twenty years of standing next to undergraduates, and looked at it for a while with her hands in the pockets of her coat, and said, not to Harry, “My mother would have liked that,” and went to make the tea.

He put that on the shelf. The shelf was very full.

---

The finals were Carrow’s idea, and Gerald Prentice’s objection, and the Dean’s problem, and Harry stayed out of all three until he was asked.

“He has read the course,” said Carrow, at a meeting in a room with a long table and a view of trees, in March. “Not *done* it. Read it. The whole of the undergraduate mathematics, the whole of the physics, and I am told by Lindqvist that he has now also read the half of physics that lives in a room. He has a paper in a journal that most of our *staff* could not get into and a second one coming. He is going to be nine in July.” She did not look at the Dean. “I want him to sit finals. Not for the degree. For the *paper*. Because in two years he is going somewhere he will not tell me about, and I should like him to leave here with a piece of paper that says what he is in a form that nobody can argue with, including whoever is waiting for him at the other end.”

“The regulations—” said Gerald Prentice.

“The regulations say *any registered student*. Edith tells me there is a precedent for *any person*, at the examination boards, and that a child of six established it by reading the regulations upside down across a table.” Carrow’s knife-face did not move. “Register him. Backdate it. Call him whatever the form requires. I will set the papers myself and mark them blind and the external will mark them again, and if he fails them I shall eat the form.”

The Dean looked at Harry, who had been sitting at the far end of the table for twenty minutes with his hands in his lap.

“Mr Potter,” said the Dean. “Do you want this?”

He had thought about it since Carrow had raised it, which was most of a month. “Yes,” he said. “Not for the degree, Professor Carrow’s right about that. For what she said. I’m going somewhere in two years where nobody has ever heard of a GCSE, and I’d like to arrive with one thing in my hand that they *have* heard of, even if they don’t know what it means.” He paused. “And because I’ve read the course, and I’d like to know whether I read it right. Professor Carrow has found eleven mistakes in my pads in two years. I’d like to know if there are more I haven’t caught.”

The Dean looked at him for a while.

“Register him,” he said to Gerald Prentice. “Backdate it. And Gerald — if anyone from the Senate asks, send them to me, and I shall send them to Professor Carrow, and I think we may rely on the Senate not asking twice.”

---

He sat them in June, in the same hall as the third-years, at the front, at a desk that had been lowered for him by a porter who had asked no questions and had plainly been told the answer anyway.

Eight papers in two weeks. Three hours each. Three hours, at two hundred to one, was twenty-five days — and he had learned, in a sports hall two summers ago, what to do with twelve, and these were different, because these were *hard*. Not the way the GCSEs had been hard, which was not at all; the way the colouring problem had been hard. Carrow had set them herself and she had set them to find out something, and he could feel, turning each paper over, exactly what she had wanted to find out, and he found that he loved her for it, and that he was not going to give her the satisfaction of a single dropped sign.

So he did them. Carefully, at the speed of his hand, showing all working, and each one took him an hour and a quarter, and then he laid the pen down parallel to the ruler and folded his hands and looked at the clock on the far wall and went inside.

He spent the first paper’s twenty days on the doorstep charm.

He had been meaning to for a year. He had the feel of it on the shelf, the whole of it, from a night when he was fifteen months old: the warmth rising through cloth exactly where the cold bit worst and nowhere else, patient, unbothered, as many times as he asked. It was the first sentence he had ever heard. He had never tried to *read* it, because for years he had not known it was a sentence, and then because he had been busy, and then because — he admitted this to the clock on the far wall — he had been a little afraid of what he would find.

He read it now. Twenty days, in a university examination hall, with his hands folded, going back along the shelf to a stone step in November and *listening*. The spine was the spine. The pointing part pointed at a child. The pushing part pushed warmth. And the bracket in the middle — he turned it over and over, the way he had turned the roses’ bracket at six — the bracket was not *warm*. It was *warm enough*. It was a bracket that *checked*. It had a part that went looking, the way mending did, except that what it went looking for was not a wrongness but a *temperature*, and it compared what it found to something, and gave exactly the difference, and then went looking again. He had never seen a bracket like it in a hundred and sixty spells and forty books. There was a warming charm in *Grade 2* and it was a blunt thing, *warm, so*, and it would have cooked a baby or done nothing. This one *watched*. This one had been written by somebody who had stood over a basket on a step and thought about what a cold child *actually needed*, which was not heat but attention, and had built the attention in.

It was not in any book. He was sure of that; he had the books. Somebody had written it that night. For him. In the time it took to walk down a garden path with a basket.

He sat in the examination hall with the physics paper finished in front of him and twenty days of nothing to do, and he thought about a man who could write a sentence like that on a doorstep at midnight, and had, and had then gone away for ten years and paid the bills for books he was not supposed to know about and told the boy which chapter to read, and he found — he examined it carefully, because it was the thing he had been a little afraid of — that he was not angry.

He was *curious*. The man could *write*. Not shout; the warming charm did not shout, it listened. Harry had been assuming, since Mrs Figg said *stick and a word*, that nobody on the far side could do what he did. Here was a sentence, ten years old, folded into a blanket, that had been written by someone who could at least *think* like that, once, when it mattered.

He took it apart that night, all of it, every part, and gave it a name, because Carrow had taught him to, and the name was *the doorstep sentence*, and under the name: *Not in any book. He made it himself, for me, in the time it took to put me down. I had better find out whether he can still do that, or whether it was only the once.*

---

The second paper’s twenty days he spent on the languages, because he had been meaning to take stock of them and had never had twenty days spare.

He had seven. He had not set out to have seven; they had simply happened, the way the Survey had happened at five, because the university had a language shelf and the Open University had a language slot and Radio 4 had a French service at four in the morning, and because a mind at two hundred to one with a grammar book and a night could, it turned out, do in a week what a classroom did in a year. French first, because of the radio. Latin, because of the spells, and because half the fourth floor cited things in it. German, because the other half cited things in German and the Germans had got there first with better names. Greek, because of the mathematics. Russian, because there had been a shelf of it nobody had touched since 1962 and he could not bear a shelf nobody had touched. Italian, by accident, from the Latin. And a start on Arabic, because of a footnote in a history of algebra, and because the script had looked, the first time he saw it, like the parts of a sentence going past — the way the roses looked, if roses could be written down.

He could read all seven. He could speak four, slowly, with an accent Mrs Figg described as *like a very polite newsreader from somewhere cold*, because speaking was the mouth and the mouth was slow and nobody in Little Whinging would practise with him. He put them on the shelf, in the examination hall, and looked at them, and thought: *seven, and nobody on the far side has ever asked whether their sentences work the same in Arabic*. He filed that later, under *Sentences in Things*. He had a feeling it was going to matter.

The third paper he spent on chess, because he had won the Surrey adult championship in April against a bank manager from Woking who had played for England in 1961 and had shaken Harry’s hand afterwards with a face like a man meeting weather, and Harry had come away from it with a trophy and a hundred pounds for the tin and a new and uncomfortable knowledge, which was that he was going to have to stop.

Not playing. Stop *winning*. He had done the sum on the train home: there were perhaps two hundred people in England who could give him a game now, and at the rate he was going there would be twenty by Christmas and none by the time he was ten, and then the only thing left was the thing the newspapers wanted, which was a *story*, and the story would not be about chess. He had promised himself at six that he would not be a thing people watched in their lounges. So he had written to the county association, through Mr Pole, who ran the Stonewall club and had become a friend by the simple method of losing to him every Wednesday for two years with undiminished pleasure, and had said that he would be glad to play, and teach, and set problems for the junior section, and would not be entering further competitions, and Mr Pole had read the letter twice and said, “You’re eight,” and Harry had said, “I know. That’s rather the point,” and Mr Pole had looked at him for a long moment and said, “Your call, son. Wednesday?” and that had been that.

He did not regret it. He examined the not-regretting in the examination hall and found it was made of the thing Carrow had said about mathematics: that a thing could be for itself. Chess had been for itself. It had been the first place he ever sat across a board from a mind he hadn’t made, and been *hunted*, and it had taught him what that was, and that was worth more than two hundred trophies. He would play Mr Pole on Wednesdays until the letter came. That was enough.

---

The results came in a sealed envelope to Carrow’s office, because the Dean had decided that was simplest, and she did not open it in front of him, which he respected. She read it with her back to him, by the window, with the lake going gold, and then she turned round, and the knife-face was doing a thing he had seen twice.

“First,” she said. “Obviously. Top of the year; also obviously, and I shall have the second-placed young man in here on Monday to tell him so and he will not enjoy it.” She put the paper down. “Eight papers. I set them to find eleven things out and I found out ten of them and you got them all right and the eleventh I could not set a question for because it was *whether you would get bored*, and the invigilator tells me you sat with your pen down for an hour and three-quarters of every paper looking at the clock, so I take it the answer is no.”

“I was reading something,” said Harry.

“You had no book.”

“I didn’t need one.”

Carrow looked at him over the glasses for a long moment, and he watched her decide, the way she had decided over four hundred and six library slips at five, to stand to one side of a door.

“Hm,” she said. “Degree ceremony is the fourteenth of July. You will wear a gown. It will not fit. Lindqvist has asked to read the citation and I have said he may if he keeps it under a minute, which he will not.” She sat down. “And Potter. On the fifteenth of July you are a graduate of this university, and on the sixteenth you are registered for a research degree, with me, on the thing from 1979, because I have had the form on my desk since March and I am tired of looking at it. Three years, it says. I expect you to take one, because I have seen what comes out of that tin in a month, and I am told you have an appointment in two.”

“Yes, Professor.”

“Then we had better not waste them,” said Carrow, and pulled a pad towards her, and the thing from 1979 began.

---

The ceremony was in the hall with the long windows, on a hot afternoon, and the gown did not fit, and he did not care at all, because of who was in the third row.

Petunia had come. He had not known whether she would; he had asked her at the sink, in the new way, the front door, *will you come, Aunt Petunia, I’d like you to*, and she had stood with her hands in the water for a long moment and said *your uncle will want to*, which was not an answer, and then on the fourteenth she had been at the station at nine in her good coat and her good gloves and a hat he had never seen, and had sat on the train opposite him for twenty-two minutes without speaking, and had walked up through the park with him and Vernon in the funeral suit and Dudley eating a lolly, and had gone *in*, this time, through the doors, and sat in the third row, and he had watched the side of her face from the stage when they called his name, and it had not done the white thing, not once, and when they put the hood on him she had looked down at her gloves and then up again and her face had done something he had seen exactly once before, across a kitchen table, with a shoebox between them.

Mr Haines had come, in a tie, and sat at the end of the third row with his hands on his knees and the chalk gone from his elbows for once, and when Dr Lindqvist read the citation — a minute and forty seconds; Carrow had been right — and got to the part about the fourth decimal place, Mr Haines had nodded, slowly, the nod of a man who has written to an examination board about a diagram for twenty-nine years and has just heard someone else be right about something.

Mrs Prentice had come, in the good coat, and sat beside Mrs Figg, who had come in a hat with a cherry on it that Harry suspected she had bought for the occasion and would wear to his wedding, and the two of them had sat through the whole ceremony with their handbags on their knees like a pair of judges, and had not spoken to each other once, and had, he saw from the stage, each taken out a handkerchief at exactly the same moment, and each pretended not to see the other do it.

Carrow was on the stage, in her own gown, which fitted, and when he came past her in the line she did not shake his hand, because she did not do that, but she said, very low, so that only he heard it under the applause, “Page nine,” and he said, “Page nine,” and that was the whole of it, and it was a great deal.

Outside, in the sun, there were photographers, because there were always photographers, and he let them have one picture — a small boy in a gown that did not fit, holding a scroll with both hands, carefully, as if carrying it for someone else — and said no to everything else, through Mrs Bell, who had come too and had taken up a position between him and the press with the face of a woman who had kept a drawer marked *no fuss* for four years and had finally found a use for it.

BOY, 8, TAKES FIRST-CLASS DEGREE — YOUNGEST IN BRITISH HISTORY. Five of them. One of them found the girl who had gone up to Oxford at twelve and asked her what she thought, and she said, with what Harry considered real grace, that she hoped he was enjoying himself, and he cut that one out and kept it, because it was the first time anyone in a newspaper had asked the right question.

Vernon bought eleven copies. The frame in the lounge got a third brother, and then a fourth, and Petunia, that evening, at the sink, said to the window, “That hat of Figg’s,” in the fence voice, and then, after a moment, in the other one: “She cried. I saw her.”

“So did Mrs Prentice.”

“Hm,” said Petunia. And then, drying a cup, not looking at him: “So did I. Don’t tell your uncle.”

---

He turned nine on the last day of July, and took stock, in English, in his head, with the swing on the wall and the date beside it and the hood from the gown folded on the end of the camp bed because he had not known where else to put it.

*Nine. Something over fifteen hundred of mine, by the honest count, though I have stopped being sure the count is the point.*

*A degree. Nine papers out, one in physics with my name third. A research degree begun, on a thing from 1979. A book in the shops that has paid for a bed, and a second one for a child of six that is in my head and will be on paper by Christmas. The Edition at six hundred pages in a tin. Nine children on Saturdays. A clock, a mirror, a spectroscope, a graver, and three things in oak with sentences in them, one on a kitchen table two streets away. Seven languages, four of them aloud. A county championship I have stopped defending because I would like to keep the game. The whole of the far side’s shelf that can be bought with scone money, read; and the doorstep sentence, read at last, and it was written by someone who can listen.*

*Two years.*

*Next: the research degree, in two, because Carrow says so and because I should like to arrive with Doctor in front of my name in a world that has never heard of one and find out what they make of it. More of their books; there must be a shelf behind the shop. Everything about carving. And the thing I have been circling since the warming charm, which is that if he could write that on a doorstep, there may be others who can listen, and I have been assuming there were none, and I should stop assuming and go and find out.*

He looked at the last line for a while. Then he turned to the inside of the cupboard door, and under the date, under *Faster*, under *They shout; I can hold*, in the blue pen, small, he added to the things said there:

*He listened once. Find out who else can.*

Then he went down to the lounge in his socks, because it was ten past one, and Harry Potter, BSc, nine years old, sat on the carpet eighteen inches from the screen with the sound at two, and a woman in a committee blouse began to explain the structure of the atom to a graduate of the University of London, who had read it, and listened anyway, in case she went a different way.
