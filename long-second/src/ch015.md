# Chapter 15 — The Grammar

The book about Azkaban was forty pages long and had been printed in 1931 and smelled of a shelf nobody had dusted, and he read it on a Tuesday night in September under the stairs by the light of a disc of oak with a sentence in it, and when he had finished it he put his hand over the disc and sat in the dark for a long time without doing anything at all, which he almost never did.

It was not a good book. The man who had written it had been a Ministry clerk with a grievance and a prose style like a wet dog, and most of the forty pages were about the prison’s administration. But in the middle, for six pages, he described the guards, and he described them the way a frightened man describes a thing he has seen once and cannot stop seeing, and the description went into Harry like the doorstep cold.

They were not people. They were not animals. They were — the clerk did not have the word, and groped for it for a page and a half — *shapes*. Tall, hooded, drifting. And where they went, warmth went out of the world: not heat, the clerk was careful about that, the air was no colder, but *warmth*, the other thing, the thing that made you want to get up in the morning. They fed on it. That was the word the clerk used, and he used it nine times, as if he could not get it out of his mouth. They *fed*. They stood at the doors of the cells and fed on whatever it was in a person that was glad, until there was nothing left but the memory of having been, and the prisoners went quiet, and then went mad, and the Ministry called it security.

Harry sat in the dark with the book on his knees and did not think about the prisoners first. He would, later, and for a long time; the List would get a line that night and the line would stay. But what he thought about first, because he could not help it, because it was the way his mind went, was the *shape*.

A thing that fed on warmth. On the thing in a person that was glad. On — he turned it over, carefully, the way he turned anything with edges — on exactly the thing that had come up through him on a kitchen floor at six and gone looking for a cat’s hips. The clerk had no word for what the guards ate. Harry had a word. He had had it since a bus stop. It answered *caring*; it was made, somehow, of the stuff of minding; and here was a creature that stood at a door and *ate that*, and left a person with nothing to mind with.

If it ate sentences, it was made of them.

He kept that as a question, because he had nothing yet to make it an answer: *a sentence that eats sentences. Self-holding — nobody has to cast it; it casts itself, over and over, and the casting is the eating. If that is what it is, then it has a grammar, and if it has a grammar it has a part that could be made to stop.* And under it, in English, for whoever he would one day say it to:

*There is a prison in the sea where people are kept with these at the door. The Ministry calls it security. I have read the law and the law permits it. I do not know yet whether the men in that prison deserve to be there and I do not need to know, because nobody deserves* that. *The List has a line for pain. This is a different kind. This is the cold, and somebody built a building round it.*

He took his hand off the disc and read the six pages again, slowly, the way he had read the General’s waiting room at five, for the shape of what the clerk was not saying, and found it, and kept that too, and did not sleep, and for once did not go down to the television either.

The List got its line in the morning. *8. The cold.* Then, after a while, smaller, because it was not a thing worth a life, it was a thing he had noticed and wanted to remember noticing: *Nobody has tried to read one. Everybody has tried to keep them off. Different question.*

He mapped the roof in October, on four nights, in his socks, with the milk as an excuse.

He had felt the edge of it since he was six — a hum in the step some nights, going out early, like the street in the Alley turned down to nothing — and he had thought it was the house, and then Petunia had told him it was her, and then he had bought two monographs on the structure of wards at eight and read them in an evening and understood what he had been standing on. A ward was a sentence in a *place*. Not carved, like runes; *anchored*, to something that would hold it, the way the wall in the yard was anchored to the bricks. And this one was anchored, the monographs said in their dry way, to *blood and residence*: to a living person who shared the blood of the sacrifice, and to a roof that the protected person called home. Both or neither. The sentence held as long as both held, and when either went the sentence went, and that was why Petunia had sat by a lake for nine days, and why she had said *come back in the summers* to a shoebox.

He wanted to see it. Not feel it; *see*, the way he had seen the wall say its sentence. So he went out for the milk at six in the morning four days running in October, and stood on the step with the bottles in his hands and the frost on the path and *listened*, and on the fourth morning, with the fear-gear not available because nothing was frightening and the calm he had learned at the roses in its place, he stood far enough back inside himself to watch, and it was there.

It was enormous. He had expected a sentence; this was a *book*. It ran round the whole of Number Four, through the walls and under the floor and up through the roof-tiles into the grey, and it was old in the way the wall in the yard was old, and it was written — he stood on the step with the milk going cold in his hands and read it, a mark at a time, for a quarter of a second that was most of an hour — it was written in the same hand as the doorstep sentence. The same *listening*. The same bracket that checked and gave exactly the difference. Except that what this one checked for was not cold. It checked for *intent*. It stood at the edge of the garden and looked at everything that approached and asked, of each thing, a question Harry could not quite read — the bracket was too deep; it went down into something about blood that he had no marks for yet — and if the answer was wrong, the thing did not come in. Could not. Would find, he suspected, that it had been looking at the record shop.

And at the heart of it, where the whole book was anchored, was a shape he knew, because he had seen it on a swing.

He stood on the step a long time, that fourth morning. Then he carried the milk in, and put it on the table, and found his aunt at the sink, and said:

“Aunt Petunia. The protection. The one in the letter. I’ve just read it. It’s on the whole house. It’s — it’s very good. It’s the best thing I’ve ever seen anyone write.” He hesitated. “And it’s anchored to you. Not to the house. To *you*. If you weren’t here it wouldn’t be either.”

Petunia did not turn round. But her hands went still in the water, and after a moment she said, to the window, in a voice he had not heard before:

“I know. The letter said. *As long as she lives and he calls it home.*” A pause. “I didn’t tell you that part.”

“No.”

“I didn’t want you to think you had to be — careful of me. Again. For that.” She took her hands out of the water. “I’m not going anywhere, Harry. I’m fifty-one. I’ve no intention of making a liar of a piece of paper.”

He did not know what to say to that, and said, “Thank you,” and she said, “Lay the table,” and he laid it, and that was all, and it was the whole of a morning’s reading made into a sentence of its own.

He took the ward apart that night, every part of it he could reach, and gave it a name — *the roof* — and filed it beside *the doorstep sentence*, with this under it: *Same hand. Same listening. He can still do it, or he could in 1981. The bracket at the heart of it is about blood and I cannot read it yet. Learn to.*

And then, because the monographs had told him how a ward was anchored and he had just read one, he made a small one of his own, that week, on the inside of the cupboard door, under the date: a sentence of thirty marks cut into the paint with the graver, anchored to the door and to him, that did nothing but *notice*. Anyone who came down the hall towards the cupboard, it told him — a touch at the back of the mind, like a hand on the shoulder, three seconds before the footsteps. It was the roof turned down to nothing and pointed at a hall. He had not needed it in seven years. He made it because he could, and because a ward was the one thing in the two monographs he had not yet done with his hands, and because the next time he read the roof from the step he wanted to have *built* one, so that he would know which parts of the book were hard.

He finished the Second Edition on the twentieth of December, 1989, and it was nine hundred and forty pages, and nobody alive could read it.

He had been writing it for two years, since the ninth of January 1988, when the runes had made him see that a sentence could outlive the mind that held it. It had gone into the Edinburgh tin a hundred pages at a time, in the marks, with the nearest English beside each mark, and then into a second tin, and then into a box file from Carrow’s cupboard, and it was finished in the way a grammar is finished, which is that it had everything in it he knew and a chapter at the end for the things he did not.

The marks, and what each one was for: two hundred and twenty by the end, where there had been forty-one on the first night. The spine. The brackets, all of them — a hundred and sixty of theirs from the Standard Books and the shelf behind the shop, and sixty of his own — each with its name in their language and his, and what it did, and what it cost, and *how it had been sanded*: where theirs was a road and his was a path, where the road went wrong, where the path went somewhere the road could not. The chapter on holding. The chapter on carving, with the pot stand in it, and the light, and the ward on the door. The doorstep sentence, taken apart, and the roof, as far down as he could read it, with the blood-bracket left blank and marked *cannot read yet*. And the last chapter, which was a list of questions, and the first question was *what are they, before wands?* and the last was *the cold*.

He did not show it to anyone. There was nobody who could read it. Mrs Figg had seen the tins and had not asked, and Carrow had seen the box file go out of her cupboard and had not asked, and he had stopped minding that some years ago, mostly, because he had decided at eight that a reader in fifty years was a reader, and the book was for them.

And in the back of it, in a section of its own, were the sentences.

Not theirs. His. Built from the grammar, the way Carrow had taught him a result was built — from what you knew, towards what you wanted, with every step shown. Two hundred of them by December, composed on the train and in the carrel and in the long quiet, and most of them bad, and forty of them not, and of the forty, thirty he could *send* — by hand, without a telephone, at a tenth, each one costing like a hill, which meant perhaps one a night and sleep till the boiler. He had rationed them the way he had rationed the toffee tin. One a night, for a year, was three hundred castings, and he had learned something from every one, and the chief thing he had learned was which of his sentences were worth a telephone and which were only clever.

The one he was proudest of was a mending sentence that did not need to be told what was wrong.

Every mending in the Standard Books was a *this*. *Reparo*, for a thing that was broken; a dozen others, for a dozen specific wrongs, each with its own bracket and its own name. The Healing text was four hundred pages of specific wrongs with specific sentences for each, and a table on page ninety-one with its columns transposed. And the thing that had come up through him on a kitchen floor at six had not been told what was wrong with a cat’s hips. It had *gone looking*. He had seen it go. He had had a name for the looking part since he was six and had never once seen it in any of theirs.

So he made a sentence that was *only* the looking part. No *this*. A bracket that went into a thing and asked, of every part of it, *are you the shape you are for?* — and came back, not with a mending, but with a *map*. Where the wrong was. What kind. How deep. The way Mrs Osei’s instruments came back with a number and left it to you what the number meant.

It took him three weeks to compose it true. And then, on a Saturday in November, on Mrs Figg’s kitchen floor, with his hand in the fur over Tibbles’ haunch and Mrs Figg at the sink not watching, he sent it — and it *went*. Not at a tenth. Whole. A looking did not need strength; it needed only to be let in, and the old cat let it in, and it came back into Harry’s mind as a map, and the map said: *hips, both, worn, the left worse; a tooth at the back, gone bad, that nobody knew about; nothing else.* He sat on the floor with the map in his head and the cat looking at him with the lecture-hall face and understood that he had just made the first sentence of his own that worked without a telephone, and that it worked because it asked instead of pushed, and that of all the things in nine hundred pages this was the one.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “Tibbles has a bad tooth. At the back, on the right. He’s not said.”

She turned round from the sink.

“He wouldn’t,” she said, after a moment. “He never does.” And then: “How do you—” and stopped, because she had decided at a café table at six that she would not ask, and she looked at the cat, and at the boy on the floor, and said, “I’ll take him Monday,” and went back to the sink, and the vet found the tooth on the Monday and took it out and said it had been bad for a year.

He called the sentence *the asking*, because that was what it was, and under it in the Edition he wrote, in English: *A Healer with this would not need page ninety-one. Nobody has it because nobody can see the looking part, because the looking part is the part that happens before the shouting starts. I can see it. I can send it, now, without a telephone, because a question costs nothing. I have used it on three cats and a librarian’s wrist and my aunt’s knee, each with permission, and it has not once been wrong. When I have a telephone I am going to find out whether it can be taught, and if it can, I am going to teach it to a hospital.*

The light had been easier, and he had made it first, in September, because the torch batteries were a cost and a disc of oak with the Wand-Lighting Charm cut into its face was not. It sat on the shelf by the camp bed and gave a steady white light when a hand was on it and none when there was not, and it would do so, he thought, for as long as the oak was whole, for anyone, and he had made a second one and given it to Mrs Prentice for the returns cupboard, where the bulb had gone in 1986 and the council had never replaced it, and she had put it on the shelf and switched it on with her hand and said “Hm,” and used it every day after, and never asked where the switch was.

*Three and Thirteen* took three nights in October and came out in February, and it was the only thing he had ever written that was for people who were slow *on purpose*, and Mrs Prentice read it first, which was the point.

He had had it in his head since the sports hall. The first book had been for the boy at the back; this was for the child at the kitchen table that nobody could see — the one on the List, under *Waste* — and children of six did not want stepping stones. They wanted someone to walk beside them and point. It was eighty pages, with the pencil going and the swing on the wall. It was called *Three and Thirteen*, because that was what sixteen was, and because he had sat under a staircase at six with a blue book about even numbers and had nobody to tell. It began with conkers and ended with a mountain nobody had climbed, and in between it did the thing the men in brown jackets had done for him at one in the morning for four years: it *went a different way*, and said so, and said *in fact let’s do that again more carefully*, and hesitated before the hard steps so that you knew where they were.

Carrow read the draft and said, “This is not for me,” and then, after a page, “No. This is not for me at all,” and then read the whole of it without stopping and put it down and said, “Give it to Edith first. If Edith says it’s right, it’s right.”

Mrs Prentice read it on a Thursday, after four, at the returns desk, with the stamp beside her and the oak light on the shelf behind her, and she read it the way she read everything, which was all the way through without looking up, and when she had finished she sat for a while with her hands on the cover.

“My husband,” she said, “would have liked this.”

“I wrote it for him. Partly. For the margins.”

“I know you did, dear. It’s got his *enough for one night* in it, on page forty, you’ve put it in a boy’s mouth.” She took her glasses off and polished them on her cardigan and put them back on, which was how she signed things. “It’s right. Every word. It’s the first book I have read since 1979 that I should like to put in a child’s hand and not watch them read it, because I shouldn’t need to.” She pushed it back across the desk. “Tell Helen’s woman to print a great many. And Harry — the money.”

“The hospital. The children’s one. All of it. Mr Pargeter’s drawing it up so it goes straight there and never touches the trust.” He paused. “The first book paid for a bed. This one isn’t shoes either.”

“No,” said Mrs Prentice. “No, I shouldn’t think it is.” And then, in the filing voice, going back to the stamp: “There’s a nine in the bottom corner. It wants to be *generosity* and it isn’t.”

The press printed a great many. *Three and Thirteen*, by Harry Potter, aged nine, with a two-line foreword by Professor H. Carrow that said *I did not help with this book and I should like that known*, went into the shops in the second week of February and was in its third printing by Easter, and the first cheque to the children’s hospital was for a sum that made Mr Pargeter’s bowling-green voice go, for one syllable, slightly uneven. There was a photograph in the *Telegraph* of a girl of seven reading it on a bench, and she was frowning at page forty, and he cut that one out and kept it, because it was the second time a newspaper had got the point.

Rachel Okafor, who was eleven now and had done a page of fractions in a stable two years before, read it on a Saturday morning in the children’s corner with the other thirteen round her, and looked up at the end and said, “It’s *us*. The kitchen table. You put us in,” and he said yes, and she said, “Good,” and went back to the beginning.

The branch library was to be closed in April, and he found out from the *Advertiser* on a Thursday, and by the following Thursday it was not, and he never told Mrs Prentice how, and she never asked, and both of them knew.

It was the council. There was a list, in the paper, of eleven branches in the county that were *under review*, which Harry had read enough council prose to translate, and Little Whinging was the fourth, and the paragraph beside it said *low footfall*, and he sat in the café at Egham with the *Advertiser* flat on the table and Mrs Figg watching him over her castle, and read the paragraph four times, and felt the thing come up in him that had come up over the newspaper with the girl in the gown — slow and hot, and answering nobody.

“Dear,” said Mrs Figg.

“They’re going to close it.”

“I saw.”

“She’s held that door for me since I was five. She bought the pads. She rang Gerald. She moved the Hilbert to the bottom shelf. There are fourteen children in that corner on a Saturday.” He put the paper down. “*Low footfall*. I’ve been in that building two hundred days a year for four years and they’re counting *footfall*.”

Mrs Figg did not say anything. She had learned, over four years, when he was thinking and when he only looked as if he were, and this was the first kind, and she turned a page of her castle and waited.

He thought about the front door. The council had a front door — a meeting, a petition, a form — and he could see exactly how it would go, because he had read four years of council prose and knew the shape of a thing that had already been decided. He thought about Vernon, and the Rotary, and rejected it, because Vernon’s esteem would not stretch to a library. He thought about Carrow and rejected that too, because a professor writing to a council about a branch library in Surrey was a thing that would be filed and forgotten.

And then he thought about the newspaper, and what it had wanted from him for three years, and what he had never once let it have.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “I’m going to have to be in the paper. On purpose. Just once. Not about me — about *her*. I’ve spent three years being the dullest prodigy in England so that nobody would want to write about me, and I think I’m going to have to spend some of that now.” He picked up the *Advertiser* again. “They’ll print anything with my name in it. I’ve made sure of that by never giving them anything. So I’m going to give them something, and the something is going to be a branch library with a woman in it who held a door.”

Mrs Figg looked at him over her glasses for a long moment.

“Your mother,” she said, “would have done exactly that, and then been furious with herself for enjoying it.”

He rang the *Advertiser* himself, from the telephone in the university library, which was allowed for research, and asked for the young man who had done the degree piece, and the young man came on the line with the voice of a journalist who has been handed a thing he had given up hoping for, and Harry said, in the flat Surrey vowels, at the speed of his mouth, the sentence he had built on the train:

“I read that the council is reviewing the Little Whinging branch. I learned to read in that library. I read every book in it before I was six. The librarian, Mrs Prentice, is the reason I have a degree. If you would like to write about that, I’ll give you an hour, on Saturday, at the library, with her there and the fourteen children who come on Saturdays, and you can photograph us all in front of the stepladder that sticks.”

The piece ran the following Thursday, on page one, below the fold, with a photograph of a small boy and a grey-haired woman in a cardigan and fourteen children of assorted sizes standing in front of a stepladder in a room of nine thousand books, none of them smiling, the boy and the woman looking at the camera with the exact same expression, which the young man from the *Advertiser* described in his copy as *formidable*. BOY GENIUS: DON’T CLOSE THE LIBRARY THAT MADE ME. Harry had given him eleven sentences and the young man had used all eleven and had not needed to add a word, and the sentence that went under the photograph was the one Harry had built last and placed first: *“There are children in Little Whinging right now who are what I was at four. Fourteen of them come here on a Saturday. Somebody has to hold the door. Mrs Prentice is who does it. Count that instead of footfall.”*

The council took Little Whinging off the list on the Tuesday. The letter said *in the light of representations received*. Mrs Prentice pinned it to the noticeboard by the returns desk, under the Open University listings with the slots ringed in pencil, and said nothing about it to anyone, and the following Thursday, after four, when he came in, there was a new stepladder in the adult section, aluminium, that did not stick, and she said, without looking up from the stamp:

“Council sent it. Apparently someone wrote to the paper about the old one.”

“Did they?”

“Hm.” The stamp came down. “I kept the old one. It’s in the returns cupboard. I find I’ve grown fond of it.” She looked up, over the glasses, and her face was doing a thing he had seen on it exactly once, in a doorway, in January, over an envelope for a children’s hospital. “Go and do the crossword, dear. There’s a seven in the corner that wants to be *gratitude* and is one letter short.”

He went and did the crossword. The seven was not *gratitude*. He knew what it was before he sat down, and he did not fill it in for a long time, because he wanted to sit with the clue.

The thing from 1979 gave up its first piece in October, on a Thursday, at the board, and Carrow did not swear; she sat down, which was worse.

“Say it,” said Harry, because it was his turn.

“It holds.” She was looking at the board, not at him. “The lemma holds. That is a third of the problem and I have been stuck on that third for ten years and you have done it in a term, and I do not know, Potter, I genuinely do not know whether to be delighted or to retire.”

“Delighted,” said Harry. “You found the Hungarian trap in it in August. I’d have been in there a month.”

“You’d have been in there a week and you know it.”

Lemma two took the winter, because lemma two was hard — properly hard, the kind of hard that did not care how fast you thought, that only cared whether you had thought the *right* thing — and he gave it the nights the Edition did not need, and the train, and the long quiet, and had it on a Tuesday in February. Lemma three took a fortnight, because lemma three turned out to be lemma two seen from the other side, and once he had said so — standing at the board with the chalk, to a woman who had been looking at it from the first side for eleven years — Carrow had sat down very suddenly in the chair with the paper on it and said, “Oh, for *God’s* sake,” in a voice that was not angry at all. And then there had been the thing itself, the theorem the lemmas had been for, which was not hard once the three of them were standing, the way a roof is not hard once the walls are up, and he had built it over two Tuesdays while she watched with her feet on the paper and her glasses off, and on the third Tuesday he had written the last line and put the chalk down and turned round.

“Say it,” said Carrow, very quietly.

“It holds.”

“Yes.” She did not move. “Yes. It does.” And then, after a minute, in the ordinary voice, as if nothing had happened: “Eleven years. I started that the year you were born, Potter. I was thirty-eight. I had hair.” She put her glasses on. “A thesis. Write it. In English, for the examiners, who are slow, and one of whom is going to be from Cambridge and will arrive having decided not to believe a word, and I want him to leave having read every one.”

“Yes, Professor. Friday.”

“*Friday*—” She stopped. She looked at the box file on the shelf behind him that had come out of her cupboard. “Yes. I suppose Friday.”

He wrote it in four nights. A hundred and sixty pages, in the round hand that had learned at six to go at a reader’s pace, with every step laid down like a stepping stone and no more than one stone at a time, and he gave it to Carrow on the Friday in a box file, and she took it home to Yorkshire for Easter, and when she came back in April she put it on the desk between them and said:

“There is nothing wrong with it. I looked for a fortnight. I had my brother look, who is a better mathematician than I am and has never admitted it. There is a comma on page ninety that I would have placed differently.” She pushed the box across. “Submit it. The viva will be in June. I shall ask Aldous from Cambridge to be external, because he is the rudest man in the field and if he passes it nobody will dare say a word.”

The viva was on the ninth of June, in a room at Royal Holloway with a long table and a view of trees, and it lasted four hours and eleven minutes, and the rudest man in the field came out of it with his tie loosened and said, to Carrow, in the corridor, in a voice meant to be overheard, “Helen, I came here to fail a child and I have been *taught*.”

Professor Aldous had arrived from Cambridge having decided not to believe a word. Harry had seen it in the first second — the way the man sat, the way he put the box file on the table without opening it, the way he looked at the small boy in the school jumper at the far end with an expression Harry had last seen on Mr Bellamy of four examination boards, before Mr Bellamy had been shown the regulations upside down. The internal examiner was Dr Lindqvist, who had read the thesis twice and understood two-thirds of it and said so, which was honest, and which Aldous had plainly taken as a sign that the whole thing was a Royal Holloway conspiracy to put a child in a gown.

So Aldous had gone for the throat, and Harry had let him.

It was, he thought afterwards, the best four hours of his life to date. Because Aldous was *good*. Not kind — not remotely; Carrow had chosen him for exactly that — but good, the way the men in brown jackets were good, the way Mr Haines was good about diagrams: a man who had spent forty years knowing where the bodies were buried in this one field and had come to dig them up. He went for the lemma first, the one from 1979, and he went for it from the Hungarian side, and Harry said, “Yes — that trap; Professor Carrow found it in August; it’s the second footnote on page forty-one, and here is why it doesn’t bite,” and showed him on the board, and Aldous was quiet for a moment and then went for the second lemma from a side Harry had not seen anyone come from, and Harry stood at the board for eleven minutes — most of a day and a half — and *thought*, with the room watching, and found the answer, and it was in the thesis, on page ninety, next to the comma Carrow would have placed differently, and he said so, and Aldous turned to page ninety and read it and said, “Hm.”

By the second hour Aldous had stopped going for the throat and started going for the *mathematics*, which was a different thing and a better one, and by the third hour the two of them were at the board together, Aldous with the chalk and Harry at his elbow, working out whether a thing Aldous had suggested — a thing that was not in the thesis at all, that Aldous had thought of in the car — would extend the result to a case Harry had marked as open, and it did, nearly, and the nearly was interesting, and Lindqvist had stopped taking notes and was simply watching, and Carrow, who as supervisor was supposed to say nothing, said nothing with such force that Harry could feel it from the board.

In the fourth hour Aldous sat down and loosened his tie and said, “All right. All right. Last question, and it’s not a mathematical one, and you may decline it.” He looked at Harry down the long table. “You are nine years old. You have written a thesis that I would have been proud to write at forty and that I could not have written at any age, because I did not see the overlap and I have looked at that problem for a decade. I want to know — not how. Helen has warned me that you will not say how and that I am not to ask. I want to know *why*. Why mathematics. A mind like yours could have gone anywhere. Why *this*.”

Harry thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how.

“Because you can always check whether you’re right,” he said. “I told a newspaper that when I was seven and it was the one true thing I told them. Everywhere else I’ve ever been, people decide whether a thing is right by who said it. Here it doesn’t matter who said it. It matters whether it holds. A child of nine can stand at a board in front of a man from Cambridge who came to fail him, and if the thing on the board holds, it holds, and the man from Cambridge has to say so.” He paused. “It’s the only room I’ve ever been in where that was true. I wanted to find out what it felt like to be judged by the thing instead of the face. It feels—” he searched “—it feels like being *seen*. Properly. For the first time. I’d like everyone to have that. I think it’s what the maths is for. It’s not the only thing I do. It’s the thing I do that nobody can argue with, and I wanted one of those before I went and did the others.”

The room was quiet for a moment.

“Hm,” said Professor Aldous. He looked at Carrow. “Helen. I shall want it noted in my report that the candidate answered a question that was not on the examination, at length, in a way that I found I agreed with, and that I am sixty-three and did not expect to agree with anything this afternoon.” He stood up. “Pass. No corrections. The comma on page ninety is in the wrong place and I shan’t make him move it.” He put on his coat. “And I should like him in Cambridge for a day in the autumn. To talk. To a room. I have a room full of people who think they know what this problem was, and I should like them to be *taught*.”

The ceremony was on the fourteenth of July, in the hall with the long windows, on a hot afternoon, and the gown fitted this time, because Carrow had had it made, and he did not care at all, because of who was in the third row.

They had all come. Petunia, in the hat from last year and gloves that were new, with Vernon in the funeral suit beside her and Dudley — ten, enormous, in a tie Petunia had tied — on the end, eating nothing for once, watching the stage with an expression Harry had never seen on him and filed, carefully, as *interest*. Mr Haines, in the tie, with his hands on his knees and no chalk on his elbows. Mrs Prentice in the good coat and Mrs Figg in the cherry hat, side by side again, handbags on knees, not speaking, and this time — he saw it from the stage — holding hands, which they would both have denied to the grave. Dr Lindqvist, who had come to sit in the audience rather than on the stage because, he said, he wanted to see it properly. Mrs Osei, in a coat Harry had never seen her without the lab under. Mr Pole from the chess club, who had lost to him every Wednesday for three years and had come to lose to him, he said, in a gown. Rachel Okafor and her mother, and Lee, in a shirt with a collar, at the back, because Mrs Prentice had asked and the Dean had said yes. And at the very end of the row, in a suit that did not fit him either, with his tie loosened in a hall where every other tie was tight, Professor Aldous of Cambridge, who had not been invited and had come anyway, and who caught Harry’s eye from the stage and did not smile and inclined his head one inch, which Harry understood was a thing he had probably not done for anyone since 1962.

Carrow read the citation herself. She had told Lindqvist he could not be trusted to keep it under a minute, and she kept it under forty seconds, and the forty seconds said what the thesis was and what it had cost and who had been stuck on it since 1979, and ended: *The candidate is nine years old. I have been asked by a great many people what that means. I do not know what it means. I know that he found the overlap on the back of my door while I was looking at the front of it, and that I have never once in four years had to tell him a thing twice, and that he has never once in four years failed to tell me when I was wrong. I have supervised nineteen doctoral students. I should like it on record that he was the easiest, and that this is the hardest thing I have ever had to say in public.*

And then the Vice-Chancellor, who had been told what to say and said it anyway as if he had thought of it: “Doctor Potter.”

He walked across the stage in a gown that fitted and took the scroll with both hands, carefully, the way he had taken everything in his life that had been handed to him on a stage, as if carrying it for someone else, and he looked at the third row, and Petunia was looking at her gloves, and then she looked up, and her face did the thing, and he thought: *for the roof. And for the tea.*

Outside, in the sun, there were photographers — a great many; this one had gone round the world, and there had been a man from an American network on the telephone to Mrs Bell every day for a fortnight, and Mrs Bell had taken to answering it with *no* before he had finished the question — and he gave them one picture, and said no to everything else, and the picture was a small boy in a gown holding a scroll, and the headline, in nine countries, was some version of DOCTOR, 9, and he did not read any of them, because he had a year and a day, and nothing in nine countries was going to be as interesting.

Vernon bought every copy the newsagent had. The lounge ran out of wall.

“*Doctor*,” said Petunia, that evening, at the sink, to the window, in the fence voice. And then, drying a cup, in the other one: “She’d have — your mother. She’d have—” She stopped. “Well. She’d have *known what to say*. I don’t.”

“You came,” said Harry. “That was what to say.”

He turned ten on the last day of July, 1990, and took stock, in English, in his head, with the swing on the wall and the date beside it and a box file under the bed with nine hundred and forty pages in it that nobody alive could read.

*Ten. The honest count is somewhere short of eighteen hundred and I have stopped doing it every birthday because the number has stopped meaning what it meant. I was a thousand at six and I am not twice the person. I am the same person with more shelves, and more of what is on them is out of my head and in the world, which is the only count that matters now.*

*Read: everything the shop will sell and six things it would not have sold if the young woman with the ink had known what they were. The roof. The doorstep. The clerk’s six pages. The grammar, whole.*

*Written: the Edition, finished — nine hundred and forty pages, for a reader I have not met. Two hundred sentences of my own, forty good, thirty I can send by hand, one — the asking — that goes whole without a telephone and has not once been wrong. Fourteen papers. A thesis. Two books, both of them paying for a ward.*

*Made: a light, two; a ward on a door; a stand on a table two streets away; a clock that loses two seconds a week now; a graver; a spectroscope. Nothing yet that I could give to a Muggle hospital, because I have read the Statute, and I do not know, and I am going to find out before and not after.*

*Done: a doctorate, which is a piece of paper from this side that the other side has never seen. A library kept open by eleven sentences in a newspaper, which I did not enjoy and would do again tomorrow. Fourteen children on Saturdays, two of whom are going to a school in September that their mothers did not think they could get into.*

*Have not: a telephone. One year.*

*Found: that he can still write. The roof is his hand. He has asked me two questions through Mrs F and I have answered both and asked him for the names of two dead people and he sent them, and I have written them in the back of the Second Edition, because somebody should have them written down.*

He looked at the last line for a while. Then he turned to the inside of the cupboard door, where the paper with the date was nearly full now — *Faster*, and *They shout; I can hold*, and *He listened once; find out who else can* — and under them, in the blue pen, small, he added to the things said there what the year had taught him and what he would carry up the drive in twelve months like a lamp:

*The cold has a grammar. Nobody has read it. Read it.*

Then he went down to the lounge in his socks, because it was ten past one, and sat on the carpet eighteen inches from the screen with the sound at two, and a man in a brown jacket began to explain the structure of a cell to nobody, for the fourth time in four years, and Harry Potter, ten years old, Doctor of Philosophy, with a year to go, listened anyway, because the man might go a different way, and because he had promised himself at six that he would never again stop being found.
