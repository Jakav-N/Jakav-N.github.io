# Chapter 15 — The Grammar

The book about Azkaban was forty pages long and had been printed in 1931 and smelled of a shelf nobody had dusted, and he read it on a Tuesday night in September under the stairs with the torch, and when he had finished it he turned the torch off and sat in the dark for a long time without doing anything at all, which he almost never did.

It was not a good book. The man who had written it had been a Ministry clerk with a grievance and a prose style like a wet dog, and most of the forty pages were about the prison’s administration. But in the middle, for six pages, he described the guards, and he described them the way a frightened man describes a thing he has seen once and cannot stop seeing, and the description went into Harry like the doorstep cold.

They were not people. They were not animals. They were — the clerk did not have the word, and groped for it for a page and a half — *shapes*. Tall, hooded, drifting. And where they went, warmth went out of the world: not heat, the clerk was careful about that, the air was no colder, but *warmth*, the other thing, the thing that made you want to get up in the morning. They fed on it. That was the word the clerk used, and he used it nine times, as if he could not get it out of his mouth. They *fed*. They stood at the doors of the cells and fed on whatever it was in a person that was glad, until there was nothing left but the memory of having been, and the prisoners went quiet, and then went mad, and the Ministry called it security.

Harry sat in the dark with the book on his knees and did not think about the prisoners first. He would, later, and for a long time; the List would get a line that night and the line would stay. But what he thought about first, because he could not help it, because it was the way his mind went, was the *shape*.

A thing that fed on warmth. On the thing in a person that was glad. On — he turned it over, carefully, the way he turned anything with edges — on exactly the thing that had come up through him on a kitchen floor at six and gone looking for a cat’s hips. The clerk had no word for what the guards ate. Harry had a word. He had had it since a bus stop. It answered *caring*; it was made, somehow, of the stuff of minding; and here was a creature that stood at a door and *ate that*, and left a person with nothing to mind with.

If it ate sentences, it was made of them.

He wrote that down, in the blue notebook, in his marks, as a question, because he had nothing yet to make it an answer: *a sentence that eats sentences. Self-holding — nobody has to cast it; it casts itself, over and over, and the casting is the eating. If that is what it is, then it has a grammar, and if it has a grammar it has a part that could be made to stop.* And under that, in English, in the round hand, because this part was for the reader and not for him:

*There is a prison in the sea where people are kept with these at the door. The Ministry calls it security. I have read the law and the law permits it. I do not know yet whether the men in that prison deserve to be there and I do not need to know, because nobody deserves* that. *The List has a line for pain. This is a different kind. This is the cold, and somebody built a building round it.*

He turned the torch back on and read the six pages again, slowly, the way he had read the General’s waiting room at five, for the shape of what the clerk was not saying, and found it, and wrote that down too, and did not sleep, and for once did not go down to the television either.

The List got its line in the morning. *8. The cold.* Then, after a while, in smaller writing, because it was not a thing worth a life, it was a thing he had noticed and wanted to remember noticing: *Nobody has tried to read one. Everybody has tried to keep them off. Different question.*

He mapped the roof in October, on four nights, in his socks, with the milk as an excuse.

He had felt the edge of it since he was six — a hum in the step some nights, going out early, like the street in the Alley turned down to nothing — and he had thought it was the house, and then Petunia had told him it was her, and then he had bought two monographs on the structure of wards at eight and read them in an evening and understood what he had been standing on. A ward was a sentence in a *place*. Not carved, like runes; *anchored*, to something that would hold it, the way the wall in the yard was anchored to the bricks. And this one was anchored, the monographs said in their dry way, to *blood and residence*: to a living person who shared the blood of the sacrifice, and to a roof that the protected person called home. Both or neither. The sentence held as long as both held, and when either went the sentence went, and that was why Petunia had sat by a lake for nine days, and why she had said *come back in the summers* to a shoebox.

He wanted to see it. Not feel it; *see*, the way he had seen the wall say its sentence. So he went out for the milk at six in the morning four days running in October, and stood on the step with the bottles in his hands and the frost on the path and *listened*, and on the fourth morning, with the fear-gear not available because nothing was frightening and the calm he had learned at the roses in its place, he stood far enough back inside himself to watch, and it was there.

It was enormous. He had expected a sentence; this was a *book*. It ran round the whole of Number Four, through the walls and under the floor and up through the roof-tiles into the grey, and it was old in the way the wall in the yard was old, and it was written — he stood on the step with the milk going cold in his hands and read it, a mark at a time, for a quarter of a second that was most of an hour — it was written in the same hand as the doorstep sentence. The same *listening*. The same bracket that checked and gave exactly the difference. Except that what this one checked for was not cold. It checked for *intent*. It stood at the edge of the garden and looked at everything that approached and asked, of each thing, a question Harry could not quite read — the bracket was too deep; it went down into something about blood that he had no marks for yet — and if the answer was wrong, the thing did not come in. Could not. Would find, he suspected, that it had been looking at the record shop.

And at the heart of it, where the whole book was anchored, was a shape he knew, because he had seen it on a swing.

He stood on the step a long time, that fourth morning. Then he carried the milk in, and put it on the table, and found his aunt at the sink, and said:

“Aunt Petunia. The protection. The one in the letter. I’ve just read it. It’s on the whole house. It’s — it’s very good. It’s the best thing I’ve ever seen anyone write.” He hesitated. “And it’s anchored to you. Not to the house. To *you*. If you weren’t here it wouldn’t be either.”

Petunia did not turn round. But her hands went still in the water, and after a moment she said, to the window, in a voice he had not heard before:

“I know. The letter said. *As long as she lives and he calls it home.*” A pause. “I didn’t tell you that part.”

“No.”

“I didn’t want you to think you had to be — careful of me. Again. For that.” She took her hands out of the water. “I’m not going anywhere, Harry. I’m fifty-one. I’ve no intention of making a liar of a piece of paper.”

He did not know what to say to that, and said, “Thank you,” and she said, “Lay the table,” and he laid it, and that was all, and it was the whole of a morning’s reading made into a sentence of its own.

He wrote the ward up that night, every mark he had, in the blue notebook, and gave it a name — *the roof* — and at the end of it, under *the doorstep sentence*, he wrote: *Same hand. Same listening. He can still do it, or he could in 1981. The bracket at the heart of it is about blood and I cannot read it yet. Learn to.*

The second edition took the winter, and it was the first book he ever wrote that was finished, and nobody could read it.

He had known since the first trip that he was going to have to write it. Blishwick had written forty pages in 1911 saying there was a road and he could not see down it. Harry could see down it. He had seen down it since the roses, and had a hundred and sixty of their brackets in his marks and sixty of his own, and he had read the Standard Books and Waffling and the enchantment book and the two on wards and the four journals and the Healing text, and he had a *grammar* — not a guess at one; the thing itself, the parts and how they went together, the way a sentence was built and sent and held and anchored and carved — and it was in a blue notebook in a private alphabet in a cupboard under the stairs, and if he fell under a bus it would be gone, and the next person to see down the road would start, as Blishwick had, from nothing.

So he wrote it down. Properly, in order, the way he had written the mathematics pads for a reader who was slow: first the marks, and what each one was for; then the spine; then the brackets, all two hundred and twenty of them, each with its name in their language and his, and what it did, and what it cost, and — this was the part that took the winter — *how it had been sanded*. Where theirs was a road and his was a path. Where the road went wrong. Where the path went somewhere the road could not. He wrote it in his marks, because English had no words, and he wrote beside each mark the nearest English he could find, so that someone who had the patience and a thousand years could, perhaps, learn to read it. He called it *On the Composition of Charms: A Second Edition*, because Blishwick had been a Hufflepuff who went to France and deserved the credit, and under the title he wrote: *Begun from where E.B. stopped, by someone who can see the road, for whoever comes after and can’t.*

It was four hundred and twelve pages by March. He kept it in a biscuit tin under the camp bed — the old Edinburgh one, which Mrs Figg had given him when the Forth Bridge arrived — and he did not show it to anyone, because there was nobody who could read it, and that was the loneliest fact of his life and he had stopped minding it some years ago, mostly.

And in the back of it, in a section of its own, he began to *compose*.

Not their sentences. His. Written from scratch, on paper, from the grammar, the way Carrow had taught him that a result was built — from what you knew, towards what you wanted, with every step shown. He could not cast them; he had no telephone, and the ones he could send by hand cost like a hill and arrived at a tenth. But he could *write* them, and he could hold them, and he could look at them on the page and see whether they would work the way Carrow could look at a proof and see whether it held, and he did, through the winter, forty of them, and most of them were bad, and six of them were not.

The one he was proudest of was a mending sentence that did not need to be told what was wrong.

Every mending in the Standard Books was a *this*. *Reparo*, for a thing that was broken; a dozen others, for a dozen specific wrongs, each with its own bracket and its own name. The Healing text was four hundred pages of specific wrongs with specific sentences for each, and a table on page ninety-one with its columns transposed. And the thing that had come up through him on a kitchen floor at six had not been told what was wrong with a cat’s hips. It had *gone looking*. He had seen it go. He had the mark for the looking part in his notation since he was six and had never once seen it in any of theirs.

So he wrote a sentence that was *only* the looking part. No *this*. A bracket that went into a thing and asked, of every part of it, *are you the shape you are for?* — and came back, not with a mending, but with a *map*. Where the wrong was. What kind. How deep. The way Mrs Osei’s instruments came back with a number and left it to you what the number meant. He wrote it in forty lines of his marks and it took him three weeks and he could not cast it and he knew, looking at it on the page in the back of the biscuit tin, that it was *right*, the way page thirty-one had been right at five and the overlap had been right on the back of a door.

He called it *the asking*, because that was what it was, and under it he wrote, in English: *A Healer with this would not need page ninety-one. Nobody has it because nobody can see the looking part, because the looking part is the part that happens before the shouting starts. I can see it. When I have a telephone I am going to send this first, and if it works I am going to teach it to a hospital.*

The branch library was to be closed in April, and he found out from the *Advertiser* on a Thursday, and by the following Thursday it was not, and he never told Mrs Prentice how, and she never asked, and both of them knew.

It was the council. There was a list, in the paper, of eleven branches in the county that were *under review*, which Harry had read enough council prose to translate, and Little Whinging was the fourth, and the paragraph beside it said *low footfall*, and he sat in the café at Egham with the *Advertiser* flat on the table and Mrs Figg watching him over her castle, and read the paragraph four times, and felt the thing come up in him that had come up over the newspaper with the girl in the gown — slow and hot, and answering nobody.

“Dear,” said Mrs Figg.

“They’re going to close it.”

“I saw.”

“She’s held that door for me since I was five. She bought the pads. She rang Gerald. She moved the Hilbert to the bottom shelf.” He put the paper down. “*Low footfall*. I’ve been in that building two hundred days a year for four years and they’re counting *footfall*.”

Mrs Figg did not say anything. She had learned, over four years, when he was thinking and when he only looked as if he were, and this was the first kind, and she turned a page of her castle and waited.

He thought about the front door. The council had a front door — a meeting, a petition, a form — and he could see exactly how it would go, because he had read four years of council prose and knew the shape of a thing that had already been decided. He thought about Vernon, and the Rotary, and rejected it, because Vernon’s esteem would not stretch to a library. He thought about Carrow and rejected that too, because a professor writing to a council about a branch library in Surrey was a thing that would be filed and forgotten.

And then he thought about the thing he had been avoiding for four years, which was the newspaper, and what it had wanted from him, and what he had never once let it have.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “I’m going to have to be in the paper. On purpose. Just once. Not about me — about *her*. I’ve spent four years being the dullest prodigy in England so that nobody would want to write about me, and I think I’m going to have to spend some of that now.” He picked up the *Advertiser* again. “They’ll print anything with my name in it. I’ve made sure of that by never giving them anything. So I’m going to give them something, and the something is going to be a branch library with a woman in it who held a door.”

Mrs Figg looked at him over her glasses for a long moment.

“Your mother,” she said, “would have done exactly that, and then been furious with herself for enjoying it.”

He rang the *Advertiser* himself, from the telephone in the university library, which was allowed for research, and asked for the young man who had done the degree piece, and the young man came on the line with the voice of a journalist who has been handed a thing he had given up hoping for, and Harry said, in the flat Surrey vowels, at the speed of his mouth, the sentence he had built on the train:

“I read that the council is reviewing the Little Whinging branch. I learned to read in that library. I read every book in it before I was six. The librarian, Mrs Prentice, is the reason I have a degree. If you would like to write about that, I’ll give you an hour, on Saturday, at the library, with her there, and you can photograph us both in front of the stepladder that sticks.”

The piece ran the following Thursday, on page one, below the fold, with a photograph of a small boy and a grey-haired woman in a cardigan standing in front of a stepladder in a room of nine thousand books, neither of them smiling, both of them looking at the camera with the exact same expression, which the young man from the *Advertiser* described in his copy as *formidable*. BOY GENIUS: DON’T CLOSE THE LIBRARY THAT MADE ME. Harry had given him eleven sentences and the young man had used all eleven and had not needed to add a word, and the sentence that went under the photograph was the one Harry had built last and placed first: *“There are children in Little Whinging right now who are what I was at four. Somebody has to hold the door. Mrs Prentice is who does it. Count that instead of footfall.”*

The council took Little Whinging off the list on the Tuesday. The letter said *in the light of representations received*. Mrs Prentice pinned it to the noticeboard by the returns desk, under the Open University listings with the slots ringed in pencil, and said nothing about it to anyone, and the following Thursday, after four, when he came in, there was a new stepladder in the adult section, aluminium, that did not stick, and she said, without looking up from the stamp:

“Council sent it. Apparently someone wrote to the paper about the old one.”

“Did they?”

“Hm.” The stamp came down. “I kept the old one. It’s in the returns cupboard. I find I’ve grown fond of it.” She looked up, over the glasses, and her face was doing a thing he had seen on it exactly once, in a doorway, in January, over an envelope for a children’s hospital. “Go and do the crossword, dear. There’s a seven in the corner that wants to be *gratitude* and is one letter short.”

He went and did the crossword. The seven was not *gratitude*. He knew what it was before he sat down, and he did not fill it in for a long time, because he wanted to sit with the clue.

The thing from 1979 gave up its first piece in May, on a Thursday, at the board, and Carrow did not swear this time; she sat down, which was worse.

“Say it,” said Harry, because it was his turn.

“It holds.” She was looking at the board, not at him. “The lemma holds. That is a third of the problem and I have been stuck on that third for eleven years and you have done it in a term with your pen down in an examination hall, and I do not know, Potter, I genuinely do not know whether to be delighted or to retire.”

“Delighted,” said Harry. “You found the Hungarian trap in it in February. I’d have been in there a month.”

“You’d have been in there a week and you know it.” She took her glasses off and rubbed her eyes. “A paper. On the lemma alone. The journal will want it; it’s a known gap. And then the other two-thirds, which are harder, and which I begin to think you are going to do before July of next year because you have an *appointment*, and I am going to have to find a way to put that in a thesis without the examiners asking what sort of appointment a ten-year-old keeps.”

“Say it’s a school.”

“It *is* a school, is it.”

“A sort of one. In Scotland. I can’t tell you more than that, Professor, and I’m sorry, and I’ve never lied to you and I’m not going to start by pretending I can.” He looked at the board. “You’ll get a letter from me. Every term. I’ve promised a physics teacher the same and I keep my promises to teachers.”

Carrow looked at him for a long time over the top of the glasses she was still holding.

“Scotland,” she said. “Hm.” She put the glasses on. “Then we had better finish the thing from 1979 before Scotland gets you. Lemma two. Board.”

He turned ten on the last day of July, 1990, and took stock, in the blue pen, in English, in the fortieth pad, with the swing on the wall and the date beside it and a biscuit tin under the bed with four hundred and twelve pages in it that nobody alive could read.

*Ten. The honest count is somewhere short of eighteen hundred and I have stopped doing it every birthday because the number has stopped meaning what it meant. I was a thousand at six and I am not twice the person. I am the same person with more shelves.*

*Read: everything the shop will sell and six things it would not have sold if the young woman with the ink had known what they were. The roof. The doorstep. The clerk’s six pages. The grammar, whole.*

*Written: four hundred and twelve pages of it. Forty sentences of my own, six good, one — the asking — that I think is the most important thing I have ever made and cannot cast.*

*Done: a lemma, a third of a thesis, a third paper, a library kept open by eleven sentences in a newspaper, which I did not enjoy and would do again tomorrow.*

*Have not: a telephone. One year.*

*Found: that he can still write. The roof is his hand. He has asked me two questions through Mrs F and I have answered both and asked him for the names of two dead people and he sent them, and I have written them in the back of the List, because somebody should have them written down.*

He looked at the last line for a while. Then he turned to the inside of the cupboard door, where the paper with the date was nearly full now — *Faster*, and *They shout; I can hold*, and *He listened once; find out who else can* — and under them, in the blue pen, small, he wrote the thing the year had taught him and that he would carry up the drive in twelve months like a lamp:

*The cold has a grammar. Nobody has read it. Read it.*

Then he went down to the lounge in his socks, because it was ten past one, and sat on the carpet eighteen inches from the screen with the sound at two and the blue notebook on his knee, and a man in a brown jacket began to explain the structure of a cell to nobody, for the fourth time in four years, and Harry Potter, ten years old, with a year to go, listened anyway, because the man might go a different way, and because he had promised himself at six that he would never again stop being found.
