# Chapter 16 — The Press

Cambridge was a Tuesday in October, and a room with forty people in it who had come to see a child, and he gave them an hour and a half on the thing from 1979 and sent them away having seen a theorem instead, which was what Aldous had asked for.

He had thought about how to do it on the train, with Mrs Figg beside him reading a castle and the fens going past flat and wet. He had never spoken to a room. He had spoken to a long table at five and a sports hall at seven and nineteen third-years at a board, and none of those had been a *room* — forty people who knew the field, who had each read the thesis and come with a question folded in a pocket. He could not go at the speed of his mind; they would not follow. He could not go at the speed of his hand; they would be bored. He had to go at the speed of *theirs*, which was a thing he had been learning for five years from a woman with a knife for a face, and he found, standing at the front of the room with the chalk, that he knew it the way he knew the hall clock.

So he taught. He did what the men in brown jackets did. He went a different way, and said so, and said *in fact let’s do that again more carefully*, and hesitated before the hard steps so that they knew where they were, and when a man in the second row put his hand up with the question from his pocket, Harry answered it from the side the man had not come from, and the man sat for a moment and then wrote something down. By the end there was no sound in the room but the chalk, and when he put the chalk down Aldous, at the back, with his tie loosened, did not clap, because nobody there did, and inclined his head one inch for the second time in his life.

Afterwards, in a corridor with a draught, Aldous said: “What next?”

“Physics,” said Harry. “There’s a thing Lindqvist and I have been circling for two years. And then a thing I can’t tell you about, in September, for seven years.”

“Helen said.” Aldous looked at him for a while. “You are going to be the best mathematician of the century, and you are going to go and do something else. I find I mind that less than I expected. I think it is because I have just watched you *teach*, and I have been in this building forty years and had not seen it done.” He put his hands in his pockets. “Write to me. About the physics. Not the other thing.”

“Every term,” said Harry, who was collecting teachers to write to, and had four.

---

The physics was the thing on page one hundred and forty, and it had been sitting in a tin since he was six, and in November Lindqvist and Mrs Osei and he put it on paper together and Lindqvist, when it was done, sat with his arms folded and his eyebrows up and said a thing Harry put on the shelf next to Carrow’s citation.

He had written it at six, in the two hundred pages for a man he had not met, in a month of nights with the pencil going: a reason why the thing Mrs Osei had measured to the fourth decimal place in 1989 should be what it was and not what the third predicted. He had not known it was a reason. He had thought it was obvious, the way the twelve pages Ashworth had found in the second tin had been obvious. Lindqvist had read it four times in 1988 and said *nobody has it*, and then had said nothing more for two years, because — he admitted it in November, in the lab, with the rain on the skylight — he had not believed it, and had spent two years trying to show it was wrong, and had failed, and had then spent a third year with Mrs Osei trying to *measure* it, which was the only argument that counted, and in October the measurement had come in, and it was what page one hundred and forty said.

“I have been a physicist for twenty-five years,” said Lindqvist, with his arms folded. “I have eleven papers that people cite. This is the one I shall be remembered for and it isn’t mine. I should like that in the acknowledgements, in those words, and I should like it understood that I am not being modest, which I have never been accused of. I am being *accurate*.”

“It’s yours,” said Harry. “I wrote a page at six. You spent three years finding out whether it was true. That’s the physics. The page is only a page.”

“Hm,” said Mrs Osei, from the bench, and it was a different *hm* from the usual, and he kept it.

The paper went off in December with three names on it, alphabetical, and a line in the acknowledgements that said exactly what Lindqvist had said, and when it came back in March the editor’s letter said that the referees had asked for the raw data twice and had been sent it twice and had, the second time, apologised.

The bridge was Carrow’s doing, and the money was the first money he had ever made that was not from a page, and it taught him that the world would pay a great deal for a thing he could do on a train.

There was a firm in Reading that built footbridges, and they had built one over a river in the north that moved when people walked on it — not dangerously; *uncomfortably*, the engineer said, on the telephone to Carrow, as if a bridge were a shoe — and they had spent a year and a great deal of money trying to find out why and had not, and somebody on their board had read the *Telegraph*, and Carrow had been asked whether the University of London would *consult*, and had said that the University of London would do nothing of the kind but that she knew a boy who read on trains.

They sent the drawings. He read them on the eight thirty-one, with Mrs Figg beside him, and had the shape of it by Staines, and the reason by Egham, and it was not a hard problem; it was a problem nobody at the firm had thought to look at from the side, because the side was a thing you learned from the fifth floor’s history shelf and not from an engineering course — a thing about how people walk when a surface under them moves, and how the walking and the moving make each other worse, in a loop, that had been written down in 1831 after a bridge in Manchester fell down for exactly that reason and had been forgotten by everyone who did not read history. He wrote it out that night, twelve pages, with a diagram, in the round hand, with the fix on the last page, and Carrow sent it to Reading, and Reading sent back a cheque to the trust, through Mr Pargeter, for five thousand pounds, and a letter that said their engineers had read it and were *mortified*.

He sat with the cheque for a while. Not the number; the *fact* of it. He had not known, until that week, what his time was worth to anyone who did not love him, and it turned out that it was worth, to a firm in Reading, five thousand pounds for a train journey, and the train journey had cost him nothing at all.

He wrote to Mr Pargeter in January, on one side of one sheet, and said that the trust now held more than he could spend on travel to any place of learning in seven years, and that he should like the trustees to deem some things conducive. A year’s travel kept back, always, because a boy with no money had no doors. Everything from *Three and Thirteen*, still, to the ward. Everything from the first book, from now on, to the famine that the thin green book had begun with, which was still going on, under a different name, in a different country, and would be when he was fifty. Half of the bridge money to Mrs Prentice, for the children’s corner, in a form the council could not take back — books, and a second chair, and a cupboard with a lock. And the other half kept, in the trust, because he had read the Statute and did not know what was going to be allowed, and he wanted, when he found out, to have the price of a thing in his pocket and not have to ask anyone.

*I am keeping the shoes*, he wrote to Mr Pargeter. *I am giving the rest. I should like a statement each quarter so that I can see whether I am doing it, and not only saying it, because I have read a book that says the saying is where people go wrong.*

Mr Pargeter wrote back that the trustees had deemed everything conducive, that a statement would come each quarter, and that in thirty-one years of practice he had never before been asked by a beneficiary to be *audited*, and found that he did not know what face to make about it, and had settled on his ordinary one.

---

He designed the press in March — in his head in a night, and on paper for the back of the Edition in a week — and he could not build it, and he knew exactly how, and that was the strangest feeling of his life.

It had been coming since the runes. *Sentences in things.* He had named it at seven and had made, since, a pot stand and two lights and a ward on a door, one at a time, by hand, with a graver and a held sentence, each one a night’s work. And the enchantment book had told him how a sentence was put into a thing the other way — not carved; *pressed*, by someone who held the whole of it in their head and pushed it into the matter the way you press a seal into wax, and it took a master a day for a simple one and a week for a good one, and that was why enchanted things were rare and expensive and most people had never owned one.

And chapter nine of the enchantment book — *tell him to read chapter nine twice*, and he had, and then eleven more times — had said a thing in passing, which was that the pressing and the carving were not two things. The carving was a *pressing that had been made permanent*. And the author of chapter nine had wondered, in a footnote, whether a thing could be *made* that did the pressing — not a wizard; a *thing*, enchanted itself, that held a sentence and pressed it into whatever was put in front of it, over and over, the way a seal does — and had concluded that it could not, because no wizard could hold a sentence precisely enough to press it into the pressing-thing without the sentence going ragged, and a ragged seal made ragged copies, and ragged copies of a sentence were worse than none.

Harry had read that footnote at seven and put a margin on it that said *they shout* and gone on.

He read it again in March, at ten, with nine hundred and forty pages of grammar in a box file and three carved things in the world and a feather that held, and he sat under the stairs with the book open and understood, very clearly, in the long quiet, that the footnote was describing the thing he had been for since a doorstep.

Nobody could hold a sentence precisely enough. *He* could. He had been holding sentences precisely for four years; he had set three into oak with nothing but holding; there was a chapter in the Edition about it. If he had a telephone — if he had a telephone and a year — he could press a sentence into a thing so cleanly that the thing could press it into other things, and the copies would not be ragged, because the original would not be, because he could see it go in.

A seal. A *press*. A thing that made enchanted things the way a printing press made books, and he knew what the printing press had done to the world because he had read the whole of the fifth floor’s history shelf at six, and he sat under the stairs with his heart going and designed it.

Not the whole thing; he did not know enough about matter yet, about what would hold a sentence and what would spit it out, and the enchantment book was vague about that in the way books were vague about things their authors had never had to do. But the *sentence* — the one the press itself would hold, the one that said *take what is held in you and put it into what is placed before you, exactly, and again* — that he could write, and he wrote it, over a week of nights, in the marks, two hundred lines, with the asking built into it so that the press would *check* each copy before it let it go, the way the doorstep sentence checked a child’s temperature before it gave warmth. He could not cast it. He could not build it. He could look at it on the page and see, the way Carrow saw a proof, that it would hold.

And then, because he had read the fifth floor, he sat with the design and did the thing the fifth floor had taught him to do with any machine, which was to ask what it would be *for*, and the answer came up through him like the tide at Mrs Figg’s hands.

The doorstep sentence. The one that checked for cold and gave exactly the difference. Pressed into a blanket. A thousand blankets. In a children’s hospital eleven miles from a photograph on page seven, where a girl of six had no hair and the ward was cold at night because the heating was a line in a budget.

He made two.

Not pressed; he had no press and no telephone. Carved, the only way he had: the doorstep sentence, every mark of it, cut with the graver into two slats of oak from Haines’s drawer, each the length of a hand, over two nights in April with the ward on the door telling him the hall was empty, and the sentence held so steady while he cut that he felt it *set* with the last mark the way the pot stand had. Then he slid each slat into the hem of a blanket from the airing cupboard — Petunia counted blankets, and he told her, at the sink, that he had two for an experiment and would bring them back, and she looked at him and said *an experiment* and said nothing else — and he lay under one that night in the cupboard with the light off and waited.

It came. Warmth, through cloth, exactly where the cold bit worst and nowhere else, patient, unbothered, the same as a stone step in November. The sentence had not cared that the blanket was from a Muggle airing cupboard. It had not cared that the oak was from a physics teacher’s drawer. It had checked how cold he was and given exactly the difference and gone on checking, and he lay under it in the dark, nine and a half years after the first time, and understood that the thing he had been for since a doorstep *worked in a blanket*.

He did not give them to anyone. He folded them and put them in the trunk, under the Edition, with the slats in the hems, because he had read the Statute and he did not know, and he put it in capitals under the design, where the thing Carrow had trained in him insisted it go: *IT WORKS. TWO BLANKETS, IN A TRUNK, WARM. I DO NOT KNOW IF THIS IS ALLOWED. FIND OUT BEFORE, NOT AFTER.*

He called the design *the press* and the sentence in it *the seal*, and under them, in English, for the reader who was slow, he wrote the plainest thing in nine hundred pages: *This is the lever. Not a wand in a hand. A sentence in a thing, made by someone who can hold, copied by a thing that can check. One good sentence, pressed a thousand times, is a thousand people warm. Nobody has built this because nobody could hold the first copy. I can. I have made two by hand and they work. Give me a telephone and a year.*

Then he closed the trunk and lay in the dark with the swing on the wall and the date beside it — four months now — and found, for the first time since the cupboard week at five, that he was not waiting for the far side to send for him.

He was waiting for them to get out of the way.

Dudley asked him in May, on the stairs, with the Smeltings entrance paper in his hand and the look of a boy who had been trying for a week to find a way to ask that did not sound like asking.

“You did tables,” said Dudley. “With the ball. I know it was you. I worked it out ages ago.” He held out the paper. “This is in June. Dad says I’ll walk it. I won’t walk it. I can’t do the — the ones with the letters.”

Harry took the paper. It was the ordinary kind, the kind the boy at the back of the sports hall had failed; Dudley could do perhaps half of it, and would do a quarter, because the half he could do he would not believe he could.

“Not the front door,” said Harry.

“What?”

“Nothing. Yes. Three weeks. Not at the table — Uncle Vernon would want to help, and then you’d fail it. In the garden. You kick, I talk. You won’t notice.”

Dudley looked at him for a moment with his small eyes, and then, because he was eleven, and had been given a thing by a cousin he had spent six years not liking and did not know what face to make about it, said, “All right,” and went downstairs.

Three weeks in the garden, in the evenings, with a football and the letters-ones put into the football by the method of the tennis ball on the stairs, which Dudley pretended not to recognise and recognised. He sat the paper on the fourteenth of June and came home two feet taller than he had left, and told the table that it had been easy, really, you just had to not be thick about it, and Vernon beamed, and Petunia did a steak-and-kidney, and looked across the table at Harry, once, and he understood that she had seen every kick.

In July the blazer came, maroon, with a boater, and Dudley hated it, and wore it round the house for a whole weekend anyway, and on the Sunday he stopped at the cupboard door, in the blazer, and said, not loudly, “Thanks,” and went on down the hall, and Harry put it on the shelf with the straight fingers and the purse at the greengrocer’s, because it belonged there.

---

*Why Things Fall* came out in June, for the children in the corner who had finished *Three and Thirteen* and asked what the mountain was made of, and it was eighty pages, and it had Saturn in it, and a mirror a man and a boy had ground, and a bridge in Manchester in 1831, and the money went to the famine, and Mrs Prentice read it first and said it was right and that there was a six in the corner that wanted to be *gravity* and wasn’t.

There were twenty in the corner by then, on a Saturday, and Mrs Okafor came and sat with the younger ones, and Rachel, who was going to the grammar school in September with Lee, took the fractions, and Harry took whoever had the look — the look he had had at four, at a kitchen table, of a child who could see the whole of a thing and had nobody to tell — and there were three of those, now, out of twenty, and he had their names, and he had told Mrs Prentice that when he went in September she was to keep the corner open and write to him about the three, every term, and she had said *hm* and written it in the diary.

He had the trunk by then. Mrs Figg had brought it back from the fourth trip with a face like a woman carrying a coffin she approved of, and it stood in the cupboard with the Edition in it and the two blankets and the carved things in their tin and the robes and the cauldron and the books, and on the Thursday before his birthday the fourth paper of the year came out, the one with Lindqvist, and he put the offprint in the trunk too, on top, because it was the one he was going to show to whoever asked what a doctor was.

---

He took stock on the twenty-ninth of July, two days before, at the kitchen table, where the light was best, in English, in his head, because the cupboard was full of a trunk and because he wanted to do it somewhere he could see the garden.

*Ten. Eleven on Wednesday. I have stopped counting the other way. Call it the better part of two thousand, and call it not the point. The point is what is out of my head and in the world, and I did not understand that until I was six and a pencil moved, and I have been making up for the first five years since.*

*Out of my head, on this side: twenty-two papers. Three books, two of them paying for a ward and a famine. A doctorate, in a world that has never heard of one, and a room in Cambridge that was taught. A bridge in the north that does not move. A mirror, a clock, a spectroscope, a graver. Twenty children in a corner, and three of them are what I was. A trust with more in it than I can spend, and a man who sends me a statement each quarter so that I can see whether I am giving it away or only saying so.*

*Out of my head, on the other side: nine hundred and forty pages of grammar that nobody alive can read, for a reader in fifty years. Two hundred sentences, forty good, thirty I can send by hand, one that goes whole and has not once been wrong. Three things in oak, and a ward on a door. Two blankets in a trunk, warm, that I do not know if I am allowed to give to anyone, and will not until I do. The seal. The roof, read. The doorstep, read. Six pages by a clerk about the cold, read until I could see the shape of what he wasn’t saying. Two names in the back of the Edition. Four hundred questions, in order, for a man who has asked me three.*

*Can: hold. A feather that stays. A pot warmed from forty yards. A pencil that writes, a page that turns, a graver that sets. The asking. Nothing for myself; the door faces out; that is the design.*

*Have not: a telephone. Two days.*

He sat with that line for a long time. Then the last thing, the thing he was going to carry up the drive like a lamp, in the words he would give the reader in fifty years, because he wanted them to know what he had thought he was for, at ten, two days before:

*I am not going to that school to learn magic. I have learned the magic that is in the books; I learned it on a kitchen floor and in a cupboard and on a bench in a street with an old woman holding my hand, and I have made things with it that work. I am going to that school because it is where the telephone is, and the hospital is, and the man who can listen, and the cold, and because the two halves of one country have never once sat down at a table and somebody has to be the table. I have read both sides. I am the only one who has. I am the better part of two thousand years old and I have a doctorate and a trunk with two warm blankets in it and I am eleven on Wednesday.*

*Let them deal with it. That is their job. Mine is to stop making it hard.*

Outside, Petunia was deadheading the roses by the fence, right-handed, with her back to the window, and he watched her for a moment at the speed he always watched things, which was slowly, and then he went out to do the gutters, because he had promised, and because a doctor who kept his promises to his aunt was, he had decided, the only kind worth being.

The date on the inside of the cupboard door had two days left on it. He had stopped looking at it some time ago. He did not need to. He had known since a garden bench at five exactly how long he had, and he had used every day, and on Wednesday morning at nine o’clock somebody from the far side was going to knock on the front door of Number Four, Privet Drive, and he was going to open it, and whoever it was had better, he thought, up a ladder in the July sun with his hands full of leaves, have brought a very strong cup of tea.
