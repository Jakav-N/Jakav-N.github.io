# Chapter 18 — Interlude: The Watchers’ File

*The Deputy Headmistress’s office, Hogwarts, a Thursday evening in late June 1991*

Minerva McGonagall had forty-one letters to prepare that summer, and forty of them took her an hour and a half.

It was the same every June. The Book gave up its names; the Quill wrote the addresses in its slanting green hand; she checked each one against the register, because the Quill was old and had once, in 1974, sent a letter to a boy who had been dead for eleven years, and she had never entirely trusted it since. Then the Muggle-born ones, and the ones raised by Muggles, went into a separate pile for the visits — a dozen, most years — and the rest went to the owlery, and she had a cup of tea, and it was done.

The forty-first letter was on her desk by itself, and beside it was a file, and the file was two inches thick.

She had not meant to read it tonight. She had meant to look at it, and sort it, and read it properly on Sunday with the whole afternoon. But she had opened it to see what was on top, and what was on top was a newspaper cutting with a small boy in a school jumper holding a cup with both hands, and in the margin, in her own green ink, *M.M. said so first, Oct. ’88*, and she had sat down.

Seven years of Arabella Figg. That was the bulk of it — the monthly letters, in a hand that had changed, she noticed, somewhere in the autumn of 1988, from the careful left-handed slant of a woman with bad joints to something freer and faster and rather less legible. Albus had kept them all. Of course he had. He had kept them in order, with the cuttings Arabella had enclosed slipped in at the dates they belonged to, and reading down through the file was like watching a photograph develop in a tray: a quiet, thin child who smiled at cats; a boy with a library ticket; *he has done something to Tibbles, Albus*; a chess cup; a secondary school at eight; *nine GCSEs*, whatever those were, with a national newspaper’s cutting and the word *prodigy* in a headline; *he asked me what I was and I told him*; a university.

A *university*. She read that letter twice. Arabella had written it in October of 1989 with the particular flatness she used when she did not trust herself to be anything else: *He goes to a college in Egham on Thursdays, Albus, and sits with a professor of mathematics for two hours, and the professor has told the school she has never seen anything like him, and he is nine years and two months old.*

And near the bottom of the file — not at the bottom; there were two more of Arabella’s letters under it, from May and June — a thing that was not a cutting and not a letter but a slim pamphlet, eleven pages, stapled, with a journal’s name across the top in a plain Muggle typeface and a date, April 1991, and two names.

*H. Carrow and H. Potter.*

She held it for some time. She did not understand one line of the mathematics; it was not her field and it was not, she suspected, anyone’s at Hogwarts. But she understood what a published paper was, in any world, and she understood the date, and she did the subtraction, and then she sat in her office with the June light going long across the desk and the forty ordinary letters in their neat pile and said, aloud, to nobody:

“Ten.”

Then she picked up the file and the pamphlet and went to find the Headmaster.

---

“Albus. This child has an A-level in *Further Mathematics*.”

“So Arabella tells me.” Dumbledore did not look up from the thing he was doing, which involved a small silver instrument and a very long pair of tweezers. “I confess I had to look it up. It is a Muggle examination, taken at eighteen, in the harder parts of their mathematics. The *further* appears to be in earnest.”

“He has *four*. At ten. And nine of the other ones at nine, and—” she put the pamphlet down on his desk, on top of the instrument, which he permitted “—*this*. Which is a paper. In a journal. With a professor. I have read the covering letter from the professor, which Arabella obtained, I do not ask how, and which describes him as — I am quoting — *the most original mathematical mind I have encountered in twenty-eight years of teaching, and I include my own supervisor, who has a prize named after him.*”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “I thought that was rather well put.”

“You have read it.”

“I have read all of it, Minerva. Several times. I obtained the offprint from the college library myself, in March, by writing to them on headed paper and saying I was a professor, which is true, and that I took an interest in the work, which is also true. They were most obliging.” He set the tweezers down. The silver instrument, released, gave a small puff of lilac smoke and settled. “Sit down. You have the face.”

“I have *a* face. It is the face of a woman who told you in October of 1988, in writing, in green ink—”

“—that you said so first. Yes. I have it. It is in the file. I have never disputed it.” He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes, and in the moment before the spectacles went back she saw the thing she had seen once before, in this room, over this file: the window, briefly open. “Sit down, Minerva. Please. I should like to talk about him, and I find I have had nobody to talk about him *to*, for a very long time, except Arabella, and Arabella is in love with him, in the way one is with a child one has watched grow, and cannot be relied upon for anything but the truth.”

She sat.

---

They talked until the light had gone and Fawkes had put his head under his wing.

He had read everything. That was the first thing she understood, and it changed the shape of the conversation. She had come in with a file to confront him; he had the file by heart. He knew the chess tournaments by name. He knew about the library in Egham and the woman in the branch library before it — *a Mrs Prentice, Minerva, who appears to have done more for that boy with a library ticket than I managed with a basket and a letter* — and the boy’s GCSE subjects, all nine, and the fact that he had declined to appear on Muggle television, twice, which Dumbledore found, he said, the single most reassuring fact in the file.

“Why?”

“Because it means he knows what fame costs, at ten, without having paid it. He has been offered the Muggle version and turned it down, politely, through a headmistress. He gave one interview, a very dull one — I have it here — and in the whole of it the only line that is not a platitude is the last, in which he thanks two women by name for *holding the door* and *keeping an eye on him for a very long time*.” Dumbledore turned the cutting towards her. “He knows about Arabella. He has known, I think, for years. And he thanked her in a national newspaper, in words that would mean nothing to anyone but us, and that she would see, and that I would see. Minerva, I have been a headmaster for thirty-five years and I have had letters from Ministers and I do not think I have ever been so neatly *addressed*.”

McGonagall looked at the cutting for a long moment.

“He was writing to you.”

“He was writing to whoever Arabella wrote to. He did not have my name then. He has it now; Petunia Dursley gave it him, in April of last year, along with the name of the school and the circumstances of his parents’ death and, I rather think, a photograph. Arabella reported it with some astonishment. She has lived two streets from that woman for ten years and had not thought her capable of a kindness, and I confess I had made the same mistake, from considerably further away.” He was quiet a moment. “He made her a cup of tea. That was apparently the whole of the method.”

“And you still did not go.”

“I still did not go.” He said it plainly. “I said eleven. I have had three years to regret it and I have, on and off, in the small hours, and each morning I have looked at Arabella’s letters again and seen a boy who is happier every month, and has a professor, and a library, and a woman who holds doors, and a List—”

“A what?”

“Arabella’s word. He has written something down, she says, and keeps it, and will not show her, and calls it the List, and has told her only that it is *the things worth a life*. She has been trying to work out what is on it for a year.” The corner of his mouth moved. “So have I.”

McGonagall sat with that. Outside, the last light went off the lake.

“Albus,” she said at last. “Three years ago, in this room, you told me that you wanted him to make a *self* somewhere quiet, where nobody was watching, before he had to carry what is coming for him here. I thought you were wrong. I wrote it in the margin.”

“You did.”

“I think now that you were right, and that it has happened, and that it is not at all the self you imagined.” She tapped the pamphlet. “You pictured a boy. An ordinary one, perhaps a clever one, who would arrive here not knowing what he was and learn it from us, gently, in his own time. That boy does not exist. Arabella has watched him *decide*, in the spring of 1988, at seven, to stop being small — her word — and he has spent three years since being seen by every institution in Surrey that would look, and publishing, and lighting candles in a cupboard, and asking a squib what she is and *thanking her for it*. He is not going to arrive here to be told what he is. He has gone and found out. He bought the books, for all we know.”

Dumbledore said nothing for a moment.

“He has not bought the books,” he said. “Arabella would know. He does not keep things from her.” And then, more slowly, in the voice she had learned to listen for: “But I take the point. I have been preparing, for ten years, to receive a child. I begin to think I should prepare to receive a colleague, and that he will be eleven, and that both things will be true at once and I shall have to hold them.”

“*You* will not have to hold anything on the thirty-first of July,” said McGonagall, “because *I* am delivering that letter, by hand, in person, as I said I would, and you agreed, and it is in the file.”

“It is in the file,” Dumbledore agreed. “I had not forgotten. I should have asked you if you had not asked me.” He leaned forward. “Minerva. When you go. I want to know two things, and only two, and I do not want you to find them out by asking. I want you to *look*.”

“Yes?”

“Whether he is happy. And whether he is kind.” He sat back. “Everything else — the mathematics, the examinations, the candles — I can read in the *Surrey Advertiser*. Those two I cannot, and they are the only two that will matter in this castle, and I have been trying to read them off Arabella for ten years and she says *yes* and *yes* and I believe her and I should like it from someone who has not fed him biscuits.”

McGonagall stood. She picked up the file, and the pamphlet, and the cutting with her own green ink in the margin.

“I shall look,” she said. “And Albus — I shall also *ask*. Whatever I like. He is eleven; I am his Deputy Headmistress; I am entitled. And if the answers are what Arabella’s letters suggest they will be, I warn you now that I intend to come back here and say so, at length, and you will sit there and listen.”

“I should expect nothing less,” said Dumbledore, and meant it, and she knew he did, and that was the whole of why she had stayed thirty-five years.

---

The Quill wrote the address on the forty-first letter the next morning, in her office, in green, with the Book open beside it and the June sun on the desk, and she watched it do so as she always did, because of 1974.

*Mr H. Potter*

*The Cupboard under the Stairs*

*4 Privet Drive*

*Little Whinging*

*Surrey*

The Quill lifted. McGonagall did not move.

She read the second line again. The Quill did not make mistakes of *that* kind; it made mistakes of the other kind, the dead-boy kind, the kind where it was eleven years behind the world. It did not invent rooms. It wrote where a child *slept*, and it had written, in its slanting ancient green, that the Boy Who Lived slept in a cupboard under the stairs.

Seven years of Arabella Figg. Two inches of file. GCSEs and A-levels and a chess cup and a professor and a university and a paper in a journal in print since 1865, and not one line — not *one*, she went back through the whole of it in her head and could not find one — about where he slept. Arabella had never been inside Number Four. Arabella had never been asked. The file knew everything about Harry Potter that could be seen from a garden gate, and it had never once occurred to anyone to wonder what was behind the front door.

She picked up the letter. She carried it, and the Quill’s green line, to the Headmaster’s office, and she did not knock, and she put it on the desk in front of him, face up, and said nothing at all, which in thirty-five years she had done perhaps twice.

Dumbledore read it.

He read it for a long time, for a man who could read a page in a glance, and when he had finished he did not look up, and the room was very quiet, and Fawkes, on his perch, did not sing.

“I did not know,” he said.

“No.”

“Arabella did not know.”

“No. She could not have. She has never crossed the step.” McGonagall’s voice was perfectly level, which was how he knew. “Neither has anyone else, Albus. For ten years. On your instruction.”

He closed his eyes.

She let him have the moment, because thirty-five years had taught her when to, and because she had had her own moment in her office with the Quill and knew what it cost. Then she said, because it needed saying and he was the only one who could hear it:

“It is in the file now. The Quill has put it there. We will not pretend afterwards that it was not.”

“No,” said Dumbledore. He opened his eyes. The mildness had not come down; she watched him decline to let it, which was a thing she had rarely seen. “No, we will not. I said on this step that I did enough to him, and I did not know the half of what I meant.” He looked at the green line. “Minerva. When you go. Ask him. Do not assume. He may — I find I do not know *what* he may. He has made a self in there, you said. He may have made it on purpose. He may have reasons for a cupboard that are his own. Ask him, before either of us decides what it means. He is owed that much, and I think—” he stopped, and chose the words, which she had also rarely seen “—I think he is a person to whom one had better not arrive with the answer already in one’s pocket.”

“I shall ask,” said McGonagall. “And I shall listen. And then, Albus, whatever he says, we are going to talk about Petunia Dursley’s roof, and what it has been holding, and for how long, and you are going to tell me the whole of it, because I have spent ten years watching you decide I did not need to know, and I have decided that I do.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “Yes. I think the time for that has come.”

She took the letter back. At the door she stopped, and looked at it once more — *The Cupboard under the Stairs* — and then at him, and her face did the thing it did for students who had done something clever and were about to be told it did not excuse them, except that this time there was something else in it as well, under the severity, that he had not seen there in some years.

“Thirty-first of July,” said Minerva McGonagall. “Nine in the morning. I shall wear the tartan. He will not have seen a witch, Albus, and I should like the first one he sees to look like something.”

“He has seen a witch,” said Dumbledore. “He has seen Arabella.”

“Arabella is a squib in a housecoat and she would tell you so herself.” She opened the door. “I shall look like something.”

*Wisteria Walk, the twenty-second of July*

> *Dear Albus,*
>
> *This will be the last of these, I think, in the old way. I shall write again, of course, from the summers; but not as a watcher. He will be eleven in nine days and the watching is yours after that, and you will do it from closer than two streets, and I hope you do it well.*
>
> *He knows the date. He has known it two years. He asked me yesterday what to pack, and I told him warm things and a good pair of boots, and he wrote it down, in the notebook, in the English one, as if I were somebody. He has a photograph of his mother on a swing that he carries in his inside pocket, from the aunt, and I have seen him take it out when he thinks no one is looking and put it back.*
>
> *You asked me once, years ago, to tell you if he was happy. I did, and it was true. I should like to tell you now the thing I should have said first, seven years ago, and did not, because I did not have it yet. He is kind, Albus. Not polite — he is that too; he was that at three — kind. He mended my hands because he was sorry for them, and then he was sorry he hadn’t noticed sooner, and said so. He gave half his money to the famine at nine and kept the other half for a reason he wrote down. He makes his aunt tea. He asked me what I was so that I would not have to choose between him and you, and he waited two years to ask it.*
>
> *I have not been afraid for him since the hands. I find I am still a little afraid for the rest of you. But less, lately. I think when you meet him you will understand why.*
>
> *The good tin is nearly empty. I shall buy another for the summers.*
>
> *Arabella*

Dumbledore read it in the evening, at his desk, with the castle quiet around him and the lake going gold, and when he reached *kind* he stopped, as he had known he would, and read the word again, and then sat for a long while with the letter in his hand and the window open, looking at nothing, in the way of a man who has asked for one thing for ten years and has just, at the last possible moment, been given it by the one person he trusted to say so.

Then he took the letter, and put it in the file, at the very end, after the green line and the cupboard, and closed the file, and did not open it again until September.
