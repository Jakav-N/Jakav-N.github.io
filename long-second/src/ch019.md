# Chapter 19 — The Thirty-First

He did not sleep, and he had known he would not, and he had planned for it the way he planned for everything, which was by deciding what the year would be for.

He spent the first part of it going through the plan — the whole of it, every pad, from *be small* in a shoebox to *Faster* to the column of months with one left uncrossed — and he did not change a word, and he found when he had finished that there was nothing in it he was ashamed of, which he had not been sure of going in. He spent the second part on the blue notebook, the sentences, all of them, in order, the roses to the feather, until he could have written any one of them in the dark, which he could anyway. And the third part he spent on nothing. Lying under the stairs with the swing photograph propped against the wall and the house ticking round him, in the long quiet that had been the whole of his life, doing nothing at all, on purpose, for the last time in the smallest office in England.

At half past five he heard the kitchen light go on.

He got up. He went through in his socks, and Petunia was standing at the sink in her dressing gown with the kettle not yet on, looking out at the garden, where there was nothing yet but grey, and she did not turn round, and he put the kettle on, and got two cups down, and neither of them said anything until it had boiled.

“They’ll come today,” she said.

“Yes.”

“A letter. It was a letter, for her. An owl, in the morning, on the step, and she screamed the house down.” She took the cup he gave her. “Your uncle doesn’t know what day it is.”

“I’ll manage Uncle Vernon.”

“You always do.” It was not quite a smile. “He thinks it’s a boarding school. He’s been telling the Rotary it’s a boarding school since March. I let him.”

“It is a boarding school.”

“Harry.” She looked at him for the first time that morning, over the cup, with the face with the wall out of it, and under the face, for the first time, he saw the thing that must have been there at eleven, in a town up north, watching a sister read a letter in green ink: not envy. The other side of it. The thing envy is the shadow of. “They’re going to take you somewhere I can’t follow. I know that. I’ve known it ten years. I’m telling you now, before they come, so that I’ve said it once and you’ve heard it: I’d have kept you. If there’d been no letter. If you’d been — ordinary. I’d have kept you, badly, and you’d have gone to Egham, and I’d have put the cuttings in my purse, and it would have been a *life*.” She put the cup down. “I wanted you to know there was a version where I kept you. That’s all.”

He stood in the kitchen with his own cup going cold and found, for the second time in his life, that he had nothing ready.

“I know,” he said. “Aunt Petunia. I know. I’ll come back in the summers. For the roof.” And then, because it was the morning it was: “And for the tea.”

Petunia Dursley made the sound that was not a laugh, and turned back to the garden, and said, “Lay the table. He’ll be down at seven and he’ll want the paper,” and that was all, and it was a great deal.

---

The doorbell rang at nine o’clock exactly, and he had known it would, and he was already in the hall.

Behind him, in the kitchen, Vernon said “Who the devil calls at nine on a—” and stopped, because Petunia had said his name in a voice Harry had never heard her use, low and final, like a door being locked from the inside. Dudley, at the table, went on eating toast. Dudley had been told nothing and had noticed nothing and would, Harry had decided a year ago, be allowed to go on in that condition for as long as possible, because it was kinder.

He opened the door.

The woman on the step was tall, and severe, and dressed from collar to hem in a tartan he did not know the name of, with a pointed hat squarely on her head and square spectacles on her nose, and she was looking down at him with an expression that he had seen before, on Dr Prosser and Mrs Prentice and Professor Carrow and no one else: a professional, declining to be impressed until given cause.

“Good morning,” said the woman. Her voice was Scottish, clipped, and gave nothing away. “My name is Professor McGonagall. I am the Deputy Headmistress of Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. I have come about Harry Potter.”

“Good morning, Professor,” said Harry. “That’s me. Please come in. I’ve been expecting you.”

The eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch. He had learned from Mr Haines what that meant and what it cost.

“*Expecting* me.”

“Since the first of May, 1989,” said Harry. “The date, I mean. Not you in particular — I didn’t know who they’d send. I hoped it would be someone who’d look like something.” He stepped back from the door. “The lounge is on the left. My uncle is going to shout for about ninety seconds and then stop. I’d be grateful if you’d let me handle him; I’ve had four years’ practice and it’ll be quicker.”

Professor McGonagall stood on the step of Number Four, Privet Drive, in her tartan, with a letter in her hand addressed in green ink, and looked at the small boy in the doorway for a long moment — three seconds; most of a morning — and he watched her decide, the way he had watched Carrow decide over the colouring problem, to take the thing she had been given and see where it went.

“Very well, Mr Potter,” she said. “Lead on.”

---

Vernon shouted for eighty-four seconds. Harry timed it.

It was not the shouting he had expected. He had modelled, over four years, a Vernon who roared *we swore when we took him in we’d stamp it out* — Petunia had told him, in April, in the kitchen, what had been said in 1981, and he had built his handling round it. But four years of Rotary dinners had done something to Vernon Dursley that no amount of stamping could undo. He had *boasted*. He had bought eight copies of the *Telegraph*. He had told Marge to take her bad blood to the *Daily Mail*. He had stood in Mrs Bell’s office in the funeral suit and heard a university professor say *the most original mind in twenty-eight years* and had gone home and told the man from the garage. Vernon Dursley had spent four years building, in public, in front of everyone he knew, the character of a man whose nephew was a genius, and he could no more tear it down now in front of a stranger in a hat than he could have returned the frames to the shop.

So the shouting was confused. It wanted to be *get out of my house* and kept turning, halfway through, into *and what sort of school did you say*, and Harry stood in the middle of the lounge carpet between his uncle and the Deputy Headmistress of Hogwarts and let it run for eighty-four seconds, and then said, in the voice he had used on Dudley’s tables:

“Uncle Vernon. It’s a boarding school. It’s very old — older than Eton — and it takes about forty children a year in the whole country, and you can’t apply, you have to be *chosen*, and it chose me. Mr Pargeter’s trust will pay for anything the school doesn’t. You won’t be out of pocket.” He let that land. “And you can tell the Rotary it’s in Scotland. That part’s true.”

Vernon stood in the lounge, purple, with his mouth open, and Harry watched the two Vernons fight it out behind his face — the one from 1981 and the one from the Rotary — and it was no contest, and had not been for three years, and the Rotary won on points inside four seconds.

“*Chosen*,” said Vernon.

“Chosen, Mr Dursley,” said Professor McGonagall, who had been watching this with an expression Harry could not read and was beginning to suspect he would enjoy learning to. “Hogwarts does not advertise.”

“Hm,” said Vernon. He sat down in his chair. He looked at the frames on the wall — the chess cup, the nine A’s, the four A’s, the eight copies of the *Telegraph* folded to page five — and Harry watched him do the sum he had been doing for four years, and arrive, as he always did, at the same comfortable total. “Well. I suppose. If it’s *exclusive*.”

“I have never heard it called anything else,” said McGonagall drily, and Harry understood that he was going to like her very much, and that it was going to take her some time to decide whether she liked him, and that both of those things were correct.

Petunia had not sat down. She stood in the lounge doorway with her arms folded, in the dressing gown still, and looked at the tartan, and the hat, and the letter, and her face did not do the white thing, not once, and when McGonagall glanced at her — a brief look, professional, taking the measure — Petunia Dursley met it and held it and said, flatly:

“You’ll be wanting to talk to him. Not to us. Dudley, go upstairs.”

“But I haven’t—”

“*Upstairs*, Dudley.”

Dudley went, with his toast, looking back at the hat. Vernon, after a moment, with great dignity, took the paper and went into the kitchen, and shut the door. And Petunia stood a second longer in the doorway, looking at Harry, and said, “I’ll be in the garden,” and went, and the lounge was empty but for a witch and a boy and a wall full of frames.

McGonagall looked at the frames for a long moment before she sat down.

“Your uncle,” she said, “appears to have changed his mind about a number of things, Mr Potter.”

“I changed it for him. It took four years.” He sat in the other chair. “He had to be proud of me in public before you came, or he’d have thrown you out. So I made sure he was. I’m sorry about the eighty-four seconds. I’d hoped for sixty.”

“You *timed* it.”

“I time everything, Professor. It’s a habit.” He took a folded sheet from his pocket and smoothed it on his knee. “I have forty-one questions. I’ve put them in order. May I?”

---

She let him ask eleven of them, and answered nine, and the two she did not answer she said so plainly — *that is a matter for the Headmaster* and *I do not know, and I will not pretend to* — and he put a mark beside each and moved on, and somewhere around the fourth question she stopped looking at him like a woman delivering a letter and started looking at him like a woman conducting an interview, which was what he had wanted.

*Was the protection on this house what he thought it was — a thing that depended on his aunt’s roof and his calling it home, and would it hold through the school year if he came back in the summers?* She went still at that. She asked how he knew. He said his aunt had told him, and that he had read everything he could find, and that he had worked out the rest from the fact that a kind man had set a squib to watch him for ten years and had not once come up the path. She said, after a moment, that the matter of the protection was for the Headmaster, and that she had been promised the whole of it herself only three weeks ago, and that she would see to it that he was told. He marked it and went on.

*Who paid?* There was a vault, she said. At Gringotts. His parents had not left him poor. He nodded, and wrote *vault* beside *four galleons*, and she saw him write it and did not yet understand.

*Were there rules about doing magic before school, and what were they, exactly?* There were. She told him: a Decree, a Ministry, a Trace that detected magic near the underage. He said he had read about the Decree in *Hogwarts: A History*, and she said, “You have read *Hogwarts: A History*,” in a voice with no question in it at all, and he said yes, and she wrote something down, and they went on.

*Did she know where his parents were buried?* She did. Godric’s Hollow. A churchyard, in the West Country, with a white stone. She said it quietly, and then she said — not as a Deputy Headmistress; he could hear the difference — that she had known them both, taught them both, and that if he wished, one day, when he was ready, she would take him. He put a mark beside that one that was not like the others, and said thank you, and did not trust himself to say more, and she let it go.

*What happened to the man?* The one from Godric’s Hollow. Did they know? They did not, she said. Nobody did. There were those who thought him dead and those who thought him waiting, and the Headmaster was among the second, and that was the whole of what she could tell him, and she watched his face as she said it, and he let her see it, because he had decided a year ago on a bench that he would let the far side see his face when it mattered.

“You are not surprised,” she said.

“No. The books were careful about it. I read what they didn’t say.” He looked at the list. “It’s why the roof matters. And why Mrs Figg. I worked that out last summer. I’ve put it on a shelf.”

“A *shelf*.”

“For things I’ll deal with when I’m ready and not one day before. It’s a method. It works.” He looked up. “Professor. The next one on the list is the one I most want to ask and I don’t think you can answer it, but I’d like it on record that I asked, on the thirty-first of July, before I’d seen the school. Why did he leave me here? On the step. With a letter and a warming charm and a squib two streets away, and not come. I know he had reasons. My aunt says so. Mrs Figg says so. I want to hear him say them. Will he see me?”

McGonagall looked at him across the lounge for a long time — long for anyone — and he watched her weigh something, and he had the odd sense that it was not his question she was weighing but a conversation she had had three weeks ago in a room full of silver instruments.

“He will see you,” she said. “He has been preparing to see you for ten years. I will tell you frankly, Mr Potter, that I do not think he was preparing for *this*.” She gestured, very slightly, at the list on his knee, the frames on the wall, the whole of the tidy lounge with a boy in it who timed his uncle’s shouting. “And I will tell you also that I asked him the same question, in October of 1988, and did not get the whole of the answer, and that I have decided I am owed it, and that you are owed it rather more. You will have it from him. If he does not give it, you may come to me.”

“Thank you,” said Harry. He marked it. Then he folded the list and put it away, with thirty questions unasked, because the thirty could wait and the one that couldn’t was hers, and he had seen it coming since she sat down.

“You have a question, Professor.”

“I have several. I have been instructed to ask one in particular, and to listen to the answer, and not to arrive with my own in my pocket.” She took the letter from her robes, and turned it, and held it so he could see the green ink of the address, and the second line. “The Quill that writes these does not make mistakes about rooms, Mr Potter. It writes where a child sleeps.”

He looked at the green line. *The Cupboard under the Stairs.* He had wondered, since *Hogwarts: A History*, whether the letter would know, and had decided it probably would, and had decided also what he would say, and he said it.

“It’s mine,” he said. “I took it when I was three. Not — I wasn’t put there, Professor, or not the way that line makes it sound. There was a box room, and there still is, and I could have it tomorrow if I asked; my uncle would give it me with a bow, now. I don’t want it. The cupboard has a door that shuts and a light with its own switch and nobody has opened it in eight years except me. I learned to read in there. I learned everything in there. It’s the only room in this house that was ever mine, and I chose it, and I’d be grateful if you didn’t make it into something it wasn’t.” He stopped. Then, because she had asked properly and was listening properly, and because he had promised himself on a garden bench that he was done with half-truths: “It *was* what it looks like, when I was two. Before I chose it. I won’t pretend otherwise. But I was two for a very short time, Professor, and I’ve been the person who chose it for a great deal longer, and I’d like to be judged as him.”

McGonagall did not say anything for a while.

She looked at the letter, and at him, and then — this surprised him; he had not modelled it — at the frames on the wall again, the chess cup and the nine A’s and the paper from a journal in print since 1865, and he saw her put the two things side by side, the green line and the frames, and hold them, and not resolve them, which he thought was exactly right and which almost nobody could do.

“I will not make it into anything,” she said at last. “I will report what you have said, in your words, to the one person who needs to hear it. He asked me to ask you, and to listen, and I have, and I will tell him that you answered like—” she paused, and he saw her choose “—like a person who had thought about the question before I arrived, which I am beginning to think is going to be the shape of my year.” She put the letter down on the arm of the chair between them. “It is yours. You have not opened it.”

“I wanted to ask the questions first. In case the letter changed them.”

“Open it, Mr Potter.”

He opened it. He read it — the parchment, the crest, *Dear Mr Potter, We are pleased to inform you* — in the time it took her to blink, and then he read it again at the speed of his hand turning the page, for the pleasure of it, and then he looked at the second sheet, the list, *Uniform*, *Set Books*, *Other Equipment*, and he read down the set books, and stopped, and something happened at the corner of his mouth that he did not try to prevent.

“What is it?”

“I have four of these,” said Harry. “The books. *Standard Spells*, *Magical Theory*, *A History of Magic*, and the Bagshot. And a *Prophet* from last July, and a thing from 1911 that isn’t on any list.” He looked up at her. “I should tell you now, Professor, because you’ll find out at the bank and I’d rather you heard it from me. I’ve been to Diagon Alley. Once. Last July, the Saturday before my birthday, on my own, in a cap. I found the pub by standing on the Charing Cross Road until I could feel which way my attention was being pushed, and declining. I followed a family through the wall and watched the bricks. I changed a hundred and eighty-four pounds at Gringotts — a goblin counted it twice to be rude — and I spent all but four galleons at Flourish and Blotts, and I was home for tea, and I’ve read the books eleven times, and I can write every spell in the first three grades in a notation of my own that I don’t think anyone else has, and I know what I am, because I looked myself up in the index.”

The lounge was very quiet.

Minerva McGonagall, Deputy Headmistress of Hogwarts, who had delivered letters to Muggle-raised children for thirty-four years and had believed, until nine o’clock that morning, that she had seen every possible version of the conversation that followed, sat in a chair in a lounge in Little Whinging with her hands folded on her tartan knee and looked at a boy of eleven — eleven today — who had just told her, in order, in a level voice, that he had broken the Statute of Secrecy from the Muggle side, by *paying attention*, and had done it to buy *textbooks*.

“You followed a family through the wall,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And *watched the bricks*.”

“Yes. It’s a sentence, Professor. Written into the wall. A very old one and a very good one. I could see every mark of it. I think I could write it, given a year and a wand.” He hesitated — the one hesitation she had seen from him — and then said the thing under it, because she had earned it. “I didn’t do it to be clever. I did it because I was going to be sent for, and I wanted to have been *somebody* to myself first, before I was somebody to all of you. I wanted to read about my parents off a page, with nobody’s face to read it from. So I went and bought the page.” He folded the letter. “I’m not sorry. But I understand it was against the rules, and I understand that I was lucky, and I won’t do anything of the kind again without asking someone who knows them. I’m telling you so that you know what you’ve got.”

McGonagall was silent for a long time.

Then she stood up, and smoothed the tartan, and looked down at him from her full height with the square spectacles catching the light from the window, and he could not read her face at all, which almost never happened to him, and which he found — he examined it carefully — he did not mind.

“Mr Potter,” she said. “I was asked to come here this morning and find out two things about you, by looking. I have found them both out, and I have found out rather more, and I am going to need to sit down somewhere with a very strong cup of tea before I attempt to put any of it into a report. In the meantime—” and here something happened to the severe mouth that he would spend a good part of the next seven years learning to provoke on purpose “—I believe you have some shopping to finish. You said four galleons.”

“Yes, Professor.”

“You have rather more than four galleons. Fetch your coat.” She looked at the frames one last time, and then at the door through which his aunt had gone to the garden, and her voice changed, very slightly, into something he had heard once before in that lounge, that morning, from the other woman in the house. “And Mr Potter. Many happy returns. I knew your mother. She would have opened the letter first and asked the questions after. You are — I think — rather more like your father in that one respect, and nothing whatever like either of them in all the others, and I find I do not know yet what to make of that, and I intend to find out.”

“Thank you, Professor,” said Harry, and went to fetch his coat, and in the hall, out of sight, he stood for one second — a working afternoon — with his hand on the hook and his eyes shut and the letter in his pocket and the swing in his inside pocket and let the morning catch up with him, and then he opened his eyes and went out to the garden to tell his aunt he was going to the shops.

She was deadheading the roses by the fence. She did not look up.

“Well?” she said.

“Scottish,” said Harry. “Tartan. She knew Mum.”

Petunia’s secateurs paused.

“Go on, then,” she said, to the roses. “Tea’s at six.”
