# Chapter 19 — The Telephone

“How did you travel, the four times?”

“The train, Professor. With Mrs Figg. She sat in the sandwich bar opposite while I found it the first time, and after that she held my hand through the wall, because she said she was sixty-one and hadn’t been in there since her brother died and would like something to hold.”

“Then we shall take the train,” said Professor McGonagall, and did, in the tartan, with the pointed hat in a carrier bag from a shop that had closed in 1979, and sat for twenty-two minutes in a second-class carriage on a Wednesday morning being looked at by every person who got on, and looked back at each of them until they stopped, and Harry sat opposite her with his hands in his lap and did not say a word, because he had decided somewhere around Clapham Junction that this was the most entertaining journey of his life and he was not going to spoil it.

“You are enjoying this,” she said, at Vauxhall, without turning from the window.

“Yes, Professor.”

“Hm.” A pause. “Your mother would have enjoyed it too. She had no respect whatever for a Floo.”

He put that on the shelf with the others. It was a long shelf.

---

The Leaky Cauldron was different without the cap and without Mrs Figg’s hand, and he had known it would be.

Four trips in a football cap pulled to the eyebrows, with an old woman in a mackintosh steering him through the room at a pace that discouraged conversation: nobody in the pub had ever looked at him for longer than it took to see *child*. He walked in now a half-step behind the tartan with his face bare, and the toothless barman looked up from his glass and his rag, and saw McGonagall, and nodded, and saw Harry, and the rag stopped.

“Bless my soul,” said the barman, quite softly. “Professor — is that—”

“It is,” said McGonagall, in a voice that had closed staffroom doors for thirty years. “And he has a wand to buy and a train to catch home, Tom, and I will take it as a personal kindness if the room lets him do both.”

The room did not entirely let him. Chairs scraped. An old woman with a long pipe stood up and simply looked at him, both hands pressed flat to her chest, and said nothing, and sat down again. A man in a purple top hat — *the* man; five years older and exactly as small — came across the floor at a half-run and seized Harry’s hand in both of his and said, “Mr Potter! Dedalus Diggle! You won’t remember — Little Whinging — the bus stop—”

“I remember,” said Harry. “You bowed. My aunt bought my cousin a second ice lolly, which was against the rules of Saturday.” He shook the hand that was shaking his. “Thank you. It was the first time anyone was glad to see me. I was six. I thought about it for a long time afterwards, and I worked out from it that there were people who were glad, and that changed what I did next. So — thank you.”

Dedalus Diggle stood with his mouth open and his hat in his hand and his eyes going wet, and McGonagall said, “*Dedalus*,” in a tone that put him back in his chair as if on a string, and steered Harry through the room with one hand not quite on his shoulder, and out into the little walled yard, and shut the door.

“You remembered his *bow*.”

“I remember everything, Professor. It’s—” he hesitated, because he was closer to the edge of the real thing with her than he had been with anyone on this side, and stepped back from it, because it was not yet time and she would ask *how* “—it’s a habit I have.”

McGonagall looked at him in the yard, with the bins and the weeds and the wall, for a moment longer than the exchange warranted.

“Three up, two across,” she said, and took out her wand, and he watched the wall say its sentence, every brick, for the fifth time, and this time he did not try to hide that he was reading it, and she saw him read, and said nothing, and filed it, and they went through.

The bank was first, and the goblin on the left looked at the tartan and the boy with his face bare, and *stepped back*.

Not a bow. A quarter-pace, the way one steps back for a thing that has a right of way. Harry noticed, and knew why, and found he had preferred the contempt.

The goblin at the counter this time was older, with spectacles on a chain, and McGonagall put a small gold key on the marble and said, “Dr Potter’s vault. He will want to make a withdrawal,” and the goblin took the key and looked at it, and at Harry, and said, “Griphook,” to the air, and a younger goblin appeared, and Harry — who had stood in this hall four times under a cap with scone money in a jam jar — said, before he could stop himself:

“Is Bogrod on the exchange counter today? He changed my Muggle money the first time. He counted it twice.”

The older goblin’s spectacles came up.

“Bogrod counts everyone’s money twice,” he said. “He considers it a courtesy.” And then — the flicker, the professional flicker, the thing Harry had seen once in this hall and had filed as the finest compliment Little Whinging had never paid him — “You changed Muggle money. Four times. With a squib.”

“Yes. A hundred and twelve pounds the first time, from a toffee tin. I have—” he put his hand in his pocket and brought out what was there, which was one galleon and four sickles, the whole of what remained from the fourth trip “—I have this. I’d like to deposit it, please, before I draw anything. It was the first money I ever had on this side, or what’s left of it, and I’d rather it was in the vault than in my shirt.”

There was a silence at the counter. Griphook looked at the older goblin. The older goblin looked at one galleon and four sickles on the marble, and then at the boy, over the spectacles, for a long moment — three seconds — and Harry, who had spent ten years reading the sides of faces, could not read this one at all, and understood that goblins had been doing to wizards for a thousand years what he had been doing to the Dursleys for ten, and that he was in the presence of professionals.

“Noted,” said the goblin, and swept the coins into a drawer, and wrote a line in a ledger, and the line was longer than one galleon and four sickles warranted. “Griphook. The vault.”

The cart went down into the dark at a speed that would have been alarming to anyone who could not see it coming, and Harry could see it coming, every turn, and spent the ride instead looking at the walls — rock, old, and under the rock, faint, the hum of the street outside turned up until it was almost a sound, the whole mountain under London full of sentences a thousand years deep — and when the cart stopped and Griphook opened a door with the key and the green smoke cleared, he stood in the doorway of a vault full of gold and did not go in for a moment.

Mounds of it. Columns of silver. Heaps of little bronze. He had read *vault* on McGonagall’s lips in the lounge and written it beside *one galleon four sickles* and had not, he found, believed it until now.

He thought about a toffee tin under a returns desk. Nineteen pounds and twelve pence, in pencil, on the back of a catalogue card. A hundred and fifteen pounds to a children’s hospital in an envelope, and the whole of a book after it. Mr Pargeter’s bowling-green voice saying *such other expenses as the trustees shall in their discretion deem conducive*. A jam jar of scone money, spent by a woman who had told a great wizard he was wrong for the third time in her life to spend it. Ten years of counting, of *shoes*, of the far-side half kept in a tin for a reason he had said aloud once at eight, and here it had been the whole time, under London, in the dark, waiting for a key.

“Dr Potter,” said McGonagall, behind him. “Is something the matter?”

“No, Professor.” He went in. He took what the list needed and what he had worked out on the train that a year would need, and no more, and counted it into the bag Griphook handed him, and did the sum in sickles aloud so that Griphook would hear him do it right, and Griphook heard, and said nothing, and that was the second compliment of the morning.

“The money problem of my life,” said Harry, on the way back up, mostly to himself, with the dark going past, “just ended in about a minute. I’d planned for it to take until I was twenty-one.” He looked at his hands. “I don’t quite know what to do with the plan.”

“Keep it,” said McGonagall, unexpectedly. “In my experience, Dr Potter, the people who have had to count are the only ones who can be trusted not to.”

---

Ollivander’s was narrow and dim and smelled of dust and something under the dust, like the kitchen floor at Mrs Figg’s turned up loud, and he had been looking forward to it for five years and found, on the step, that his heart was going as hard as it had at the pub.

A telephone. Five years of shouting across a field, and behind this door was a telephone.

The old man came out of the back of the shop like something surfacing, with pale eyes that did not blink enough and a voice like paper, and said, “Ah. Yes. I thought I’d be seeing you soon, Harry Potter,” and then went on about Harry’s mother’s wand and his father’s, willow and mahogany, lengths and tempers, and Harry stood very still and listened to a man describe his parents as a pair of *instruments* — swishy, pliable, good for charm work — and put every word of it on the shelf, because nobody had ever given him *that* before, the shape of their hands.

Then the pale eyes went to his forehead.

“And that is where—”

“Yes,” said Harry. “I know what did it. I read it in a shop a hundred yards from here when I was seven, on a bench, with a squib holding my hand. I’d rather we got on, if you don’t mind, Mr Ollivander. I’d like to find out what mine is before I think about his.”

Ollivander looked at him for a long moment with the unblinking eyes, and something moved behind them that was not quite surprise.

“Yes,” he said softly. “Yes, I think that is the correct order.” And took out a tape measure that began to measure Harry by itself, and went to the shelves.

The trying took forty minutes by the shop’s clock and he loved it beyond reason, and he could not have explained to anyone why, except that it was the first time in his life anyone had handed him *instruments*, one after another, and said *try*. Beechwood and dragon heartstring. Maple and phoenix feather. Ebony and unicorn. He took each one, and lifted it, and Ollivander snatched each one back almost before he had, saying *no, no*, and Harry did not mind, because every one — every *single* one — had done something that nothing in ten years had done.

They *answered*. Badly. Like a telephone with the line crackling, a wrong number, a voice on the far end that was not meant for him. But he felt it each time: the sentence he had been shouting across a field for five years *going somewhere*. Into the wood. Down the grain. Looking for a way out and not finding it, in the wrong wands, and finding a little of one in the nearly-right ones, and he held each nearly-right one a half-second longer than Ollivander wanted and *listened*, the way he had listened to the roses, and heard the shape of the line, and understood what Waffling had been fumbling towards for nine pages and what Blishwick had regretted he could not see and what he himself had written in a biscuit tin at ten under the heading *telephone* without ever once having held one.

The wand was a *channel*, exactly as he had written. And the channel had a *grain*, and the grain had to match the shape of the one sending, or the sentence went down it sideways and came out ragged or not at all, and that was *why* the feathers shot into the ceiling, and that was why it mattered which wand, and he stood in the middle of Ollivander’s shop with his thirty-first rejection in his hand and said, aloud, before he could stop himself:

“They have a *grain*.”

Ollivander, at the shelves, went still.

“I beg your pardon?”

“The wands. They — I’m sorry, Mr Ollivander, I don’t know your words for it; I’ve read Waffling and he hasn’t got any either. But each one has a shape inside it that the — the sending — has to fit. Like a key. The nearly-right ones, I can feel the sending go in and get *stuck*, partway, and come back. The wrong ones it doesn’t go in at all.” He looked at the ebony in his hand. “This one it goes in about a third and stops. It’s very close. It’s just the wrong — turn. I don’t know how to say it. The wrong turn.”

Ollivander did not say anything for a very long time, for a man who had been talking without pause for forty minutes.

“The wrong turn,” he said at last, in the paper voice, very quietly. “Yes. That is — that is not a bad word for it. I have been in this shop for seventy years, Dr Potter, and my father before me, and I have never once heard a customer describe the fit from the *inside*.” He turned back to the shelves, and his hand went, without looking, to a box high up and far back that Harry had the strong sense he had been leaving for last. “I wonder,” said Ollivander. “Yes. Why not. Holly and phoenix feather, eleven inches, nice and supple. An unusual combination. Try.”

Harry took it.

He had been waiting five years for a telephone and had thought he knew what one would feel like, and he did not. It was not a crackle. It was not a *fit*, even, the key-and-lock thing he had felt in the ebony. It was the thing on the kitchen floor the day of the cat: the warmth coming up through him and *finding the way already open* — a line that ran straight from six years old to the tip of a piece of holly, with no field between, no shouting, no loss — and he felt the sentence he had been holding in the back of his mind since the shop door, the simplest one he knew, the candle’s cousin, *light*, go down the grain of the wand like water down a pipe that had been built for it, and he did not let it out.

He *held* it.

He had learned to hold on a feather at a quarter to midnight in a cupboard three years ago, and he held now, with the whole shop watching, the sentence at the tip of the wand like a note in singing, and then he let it out *slowly* — not the shower of sparks that the books said, that the first-years did, that Ollivander was plainly waiting for — but a single steady point of white light, at the tip, that came up out of nothing like the candle had, and stood, and held, and did not waver, and lit the dust in the air of the shop so that every mote of it hung still and separate and bright, for four seconds, which was most of a quarter of an hour of looking at a thing he had made exactly as he had meant to make it.

Then he let it go. The light went out. The dust fell.

Nobody in the shop said anything.

McGonagall, by the door, had one hand flat against the frame. Ollivander stood at the counter with his pale eyes very wide and his mouth a little open, and for the first time since Harry had come in, he blinked.

“Curious,” said Ollivander, and his voice was not paper now; it was something with more in it. “Very — Dr Potter. That was not sparks.”

“No, sir.”

“A first touch *always* gives sparks. Always. The wand answers and the wizard cannot yet shape the answer, and so it comes out as — as *noise*. Joyful noise, but noise. I have seen it ten thousand times. I have never seen it—” he searched “—*steady*.”

“I’ve been practising holding,” said Harry. “Without one of these. For three years. On a feather. I thought if I could hold with nothing, I could hold with something.” He looked at the wand in his hand. It was warm. It had, he thought, been waiting rather a long time. “I was right. It’s — Mr Ollivander, it’s *so much easier* with the telephone.”

“The — telephone.”

“It’s what I call it. Sorry. The channel. You haven’t got a word either; I checked.” He turned the holly over. “Thank you. This is the one. I knew the moment it went in. There wasn’t a turn at all.”

Ollivander came round the counter. He did it slowly, with the eyes still wide, and he stopped in front of Harry and looked at him — not the forehead this time; at *him* — the way Carrow had looked at forty pages at a long table, the way Aldous had looked at page ninety, and Harry stood and let himself be looked at.

“Curious,” said Ollivander again, softly. “Curious. I shall tell you, Dr Potter, because I think it is the sort of thing you would rather know than not, and because I rather think you will find out anyway. The phoenix whose feather is in your wand gave one other. Just one. Thirteen and a half inches, yew. It is very curious indeed that you should be destined for this wand when its brother—” he stopped, and the pale eyes went, briefly, to the forehead and away “—when its brother gave you that scar.”

The shop was quiet.

Harry stood with the holly in his hand and let it land, which took a while by his own clock, and then he did the thing he had learned to do on a bench in the Alley at seven with a reference book open on his knee and a squib’s hand in his: he picked it up, and looked at it carefully, and put it on the shelf marked *when I am ready and not one day before*, and found that the shelf held, as it had held everything he had put on it for four years.

“Thank you for telling me,” he said. “You’re right. I’d rather know.” He looked at the wand, warm, eleven inches, no turn at all. “Its brother did what it did. This one just held a light for four seconds in a shop. I think I’ll judge it on that, for now, and see how it goes.”

Ollivander looked at him a moment longer. Then, to Harry’s considerable surprise, he bowed — a small one, from the neck, the bow of a craftsman to someone who has understood his craft — and said, “Seven galleons, Dr Potter. And I think we may expect great things. I have said that to a great many customers, over seventy years, and I find I have never before said it with so little idea what the things will *be*.”

Madam Malkin’s took ten minutes and he said nothing in it worth recording, except that there was a pale boy on the next stool talking about brooms and houses who looked at Harry’s face and went quiet in a way that Harry filed under *later*. The cauldron, the scales, the phials, the telescope took twenty. Flourish and Blotts he had no need of, and said so, and McGonagall said “I know,” and let him go in anyway, for five minutes, to stand in the back room by the high shelf where a thin grey book had been, and was not now, because it was in a biscuit tin in Surrey with his margin in it, and the young woman with the inky fingers looked up from the till and said, “The *margins* boy — oh. *Oh*,” and did the sum, and Harry said, “I was the margins boy first. I’d rather be that one in here,” and she laughed, and he bought nothing, and McGonagall, behind him, made a small sound that he would spend seven years learning was the nearest she came in public to a laugh.

The owl he chose himself. It was not on the list, and she said so, and he said he had made promises to a physics teacher and a professor of mathematics and a woman with a stamp that he would write, and that there was a post from everywhere and it was only a question of stamps, and she looked at him over the spectacles and said nothing further, and he went into Eeylops alone and came out with a snowy owl in a cage who regarded him with the exact expression of a cat on a wall in Wisteria Walk, and whom he named, that night, out of *A History of Magic*, Hedwig, because the Hedwig in the book had been a woman who could not be told anything, and he had a feeling.

“Dr Potter,” said McGonagall, outside the Leaky Cauldron at half past four, in the Charing Cross Road, with the pointed hat back in its carrier bag and the holly in his pocket and the owl in his hand and the Wednesday crowd going round them like water round a stone, “I shall see you on the first of September. The ticket is in your letter. Platform nine and three-quarters, which does not exist, and you will find it anyway, I have no doubt, by *declining*.” The mouth did its thing. “I have one more question. It is not on anybody’s list.”

“Yes, Professor?”

“The light. In the shop. Four seconds, steady, on a first touch.” She looked at him with the square spectacles catching the afternoon, and her voice was not the Deputy Headmistress’s. “Dr Potter, I have taught transfiguration for thirty-four years and examined some two thousand first-years, and what you did in that shop is not possible. I do not say unusual. I say that I have, until half past twelve today, believed it to be *impossible*, for anyone, at any age, with any wand. I should like to know — not how. I will not ask how. I should like to know whether you *knew* it was impossible.”

He thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how to think about anything by then.

“Yes,” he said. “I read it in a book from 1911. The man said it couldn’t be done and apologised for it. I wrote *not quite* in the margin when I was seven and then I spent three years finding out whether I was right.” He shifted the cage to his other hand. “I’m sorry, Professor. I know it’s going to make your year complicated. I’ve been trying to think of a way to make it less so and I haven’t found one yet. But I promise you I’ll be careful, and I promise you I’ll tell you before I do anything I think might be impossible again, so that at least you’re not finding out in a shop.”

Minerva McGonagall looked at the Boy Who Lived on the pavement of the Charing Cross Road for a long moment, with her hand on the strap of a carrier bag from a shop that had closed in 1979.

“Tea at six,” she said at last. “Your aunt told me. Go and catch your train.”

*The Headmaster’s office, Hogwarts, that evening*

“Albus.”

“Minerva. Sit down. You have the face.”

“I have *several* faces. I have been accumulating them since nine o’clock this morning and I have not yet decided which to wear.” She did not sit. She put a sheet of her own notes on his desk, in green ink, closely written on both sides, and she stood over it with her hands flat on the desk and said: “You asked me for two things. Whether he is happy and whether he is kind. I will give you those first, because they are the simple ones.”

“Please.”

“He is happy. Not content — *happy*. He enjoyed the train. He enjoyed my being stared at. He laughed at a bookseller. He went into a vault full of more gold than he will spend in his life and stood in the door of it for four seconds and the thing on his face was not greed, Albus, it was *grief*, for a toffee tin and a jam jar and ten years of counting, and then he took exactly what a year needs and counted it aloud in sickles so the goblin would hear him do it right.” She breathed. “And he is kind. He deposited one galleon and four sickles before he would draw a knut, because it was the first money he had on this side and he wanted it in the vault and not his shirt. He remembered Dedalus Diggle’s *bow*, from five years ago, and thanked him for it, and told him it had changed what he did next, and Dedalus wept. He bought an owl because he promised three Muggle teachers he would write. He asked Ollivander to tell him about his *own* wand before the one that killed his parents, and when Ollivander told him about the feather anyway, he said — I have it here — *its brother did what it did; this one just held a light for four seconds in a shop; I think I’ll judge it on that.*”

Dumbledore had gone very still at *feather*.

“He knows about the brother wand.”

“Ollivander told him. He put it—” she read from her notes “—*on a shelf*. For things he will deal with when he is ready and not one day before. He has had the shelf since he was seven. He said it had never once failed to hold.” She turned the page. “Now the complicated ones. He asked me, in a lounge, before breakfast, formally, with reasons, whether the Statute of Secrecy would permit him to warm a Muggle children’s hospital with blankets that carry a charm he has designed, pressed by a device he has designed, that nobody has built because nobody could hold the first copy steady, and he can.”

Dumbledore said nothing.

“I told him it does not, as it stands. He said he had thought not. He said it was second on his list of four hundred and three, after the step, and that he wanted to know *before*, not after, because he has a rule.” She straightened. “And Albus. The shop. Ollivander’s. I have left it to last because I do not have words for it and I have been looking since half past twelve.”

“Tell me.”

“Holly and phoenix. His first touch. And he did not give sparks.” She said it very precisely, the way one reports an instrument reading one does not believe. “He held the wand, and he held *whatever it is one holds*, for a second — I saw him do it, I saw him *wait* — and then a single point of white light came out at the tip, steady, and *stayed*, for four seconds, and lit the dust, and went out when he chose. Ollivander *blinked*. Ollivander has not blinked in my presence in thirty years. He said he had seen ten thousand first touches and never once a steady one, and the boy said he had been *practising holding without a wand for three years* so that he could hold *with* one, and then he called the wand a *telephone*, and apologised for the word, and told Ollivander that Ollivander hadn’t got a word either because he had *checked*.”

She stopped. The office was very quiet. Fawkes, on his perch, had his head up.

“And then, outside, on the pavement, I asked him whether he had known it was impossible, and he said yes, he had read it in Blishwick at seven and written *not quite* in the margin and spent three years finding out, and he was *sorry*, Albus, he said he was sorry it was going to make my year complicated and that he would try to warn me next time so that I did not find out in a shop.”

Albus Dumbledore sat behind his desk with his hands folded and the green notes in front of him and the letter from July of 1989 in his inside pocket, where it had been since the twenty-second, and for a long time he did not say anything at all, and McGonagall let him, because she had been letting him for thirty-five years and knew the difference between the silence that was a blind coming down and the silence that was a man standing in front of something with his own eyes open.

“Minerva,” he said finally. “Three weeks ago I said I had underestimated the matter in both directions at once.”

“You did.”

“I think I had not finished underestimating it.” He took his spectacles off. He did not polish them. He simply held them, and looked at the green notes, and she saw the window open and stay open. “He put the feather on a shelf. He deposited one galleon. He is sorry for *your* year. He asked, in order, with reasons, whether he may warm a hospital. And he walked into Ollivander’s and *held a light*.” He put the spectacles back on. “I asked you whether he was kind because I believed it was the only thing that would matter. I find I was right, and I find also that I had not the faintest idea what I was asking, and that I have had five years and a drawer to find out and did not.”

“No,” said McGonagall. “Nor had I. Nor, I think, had Arabella, and she has been *in love* with him for five years and bought his wand-shop books with scone money.” She picked up her notes. “He is going to ask you why you left him on the step. On the first of September, or as soon after as he can get into this office, which I suspect will be the first of September. He has it first on a list of four hundred and three, and he told me he wanted it on record that he had asked before he saw the school, and I told him you would have an answer.”

“I have an answer. It is in my pocket.”

“Then, Albus, I should spend the next month making very sure it is the whole thing.” She went to the door. “Because I have met him now, and I will tell you what Arabella could not, having never crossed the step: he will know the difference. He has spent ten years learning to read the sides of faces for the part that is not being said. He told me so. I think he was being kind.”

She opened the door.

“*Albus*,” she said, from the threshold — and it was not a sentence; it was the whole of the day in a name — and went to find a very strong cup of tea.
