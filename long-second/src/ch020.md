# Chapter 20 — Sixteen Years of August

He did not use the wand. That was the first decision of the month and the hardest, and he made it on the train home from the Charing Cross Road with the holly in his pocket going warm against his leg, and he wrote it down that night so that he would not be able to pretend afterwards that he had not made it on purpose.

*The Decree says no magic outside school before seventeen. McGonagall says the Trace has never once registered at this house. I have a theory about why. The theory says that what I do on kitchen floors does not look, to whatever watches, like what it watches for, because it has no shout in it. The theory does not say anything about a telephone. The moment I send a sentence down holly and phoenix, I am doing exactly what the Trace was built to see, in a Muggle house, under a protection my aunt is holding up with her life, and I promised a woman in tartan on a pavement that I would not do anything impossible without telling her first.*

*So: no. Not for a month. I have waited five years for this and I can wait thirty-one days. The telephone stays on the hook.*

He underlined *on the hook*, and then sat looking at the wand on the blanket beside the blue notebook, eleven inches of holly with no turn in it at all, and felt the month ahead of him the way he had felt the twelve days of a sports hall at six: as a thing with rules, and nothing in it, that he would have to fill himself.

A month was sixteen years. He had never had sixteen years with a telephone he could not use, and he found, lying there with the house ticking, that he was looking forward to it.

---

He found out what a wand was by not using it.

That was the second thing the month taught him, and it was worth, he thought afterwards, the whole of the thirty-one days. He had read Waffling’s nine pages on wands and Blishwick’s two and the enchantment book’s chapter nine eleven times, and he had held thirty-one wrong wands and one right one in a shop, and none of that had told him what the thing *was*. It had told him what it did. He had learned at four that what a thing did and what it was were different questions, and that the second was the one worth asking, and now he had the thing itself on the blanket in front of him for sixteen years and no permission to make it do anything, and so he did the only thing left, which was to *look*.

He held it. That was all. Hours a night, under the stairs, after the broadcasts — he had kept the broadcasts; he would not have known how to stop — with the holly in his right hand and his eyes shut and nothing sent down it, and he listened to the line.

It had a grain. He had known that in the shop. What he had not known, and what sixteen years of holding told him, was what the grain was *for*. It was not a pipe. A pipe carried what you put in it and did not care what. This — he lay in the dark with the wand warm in his hand and read it the way he had read the roof from the step — this *shaped*. The sentence you sent down it came out the far end *more itself*: the pointing sharper, the pushing cleaner, the bracket held in a way that he, with his two hundred to one, could do by hand, and that nobody else alive could, and that the wand did *for* them, a little, without their knowing. That was why they could cast at all. Not because the wand carried the shout; because the wand *tidied* it, on the way through, enough that a ragged sentence sent with the eyes shut arrived at the far end as something the world could read.

And it did it according to its grain. Holly and phoenix tidied one way. Ebony and unicorn would have tidied another, and the other was the wrong turn, and a sentence tidied the wrong way for the mind that sent it came out *less* itself, not more, and the feather went into the ceiling.

He wrote that down, in the blue notebook, in his marks, over four nights, and then in English, for the reader: *The wand is not a telephone. I have been calling it that for five years and I was wrong, or not wrong enough. A telephone carries. This* edits. *It takes a sentence and sands it on the way through, the way a thousand years of hands sanded the Standard Books, and the sanding is the grain, and the grain has to match the hand. They have never known this because they have never once sent a sentence and watched it come out the far end; they shout and the wand tidies and the feather lifts and they think the word did it.*

*I can hold a sentence so steady it does not need sanding. So what does the wand do for* me?

He did not know. He could not find out without sending something, and he had made a rule. But he turned the question over for most of a subjective decade, in the dark, with the holly in his hand, and arrived at a guess that frightened him a little, in the good way, and wrote it down with *provisional* in capitals above it:

*It will do what it does for everyone — carry, and tidy. But there will be nothing to tidy. So the whole of the wand’s attention — if a wand has attention; I think the grain IS its attention — will go into the carrying, and a sentence that arrives at the far end without a mark out of place, carried by a channel that has nothing else to do, will be the first sentence of its kind that anyone has sent since the first wizards, before wands, when people asked.*

*I think the light in the shop was that. I think four seconds steady was what it looks like when the sanding has nothing to do. I think I am going to find out on the first of September what else it looks like, and I think Flitwick — the Charms master; McGonagall says he thinks about this more than most — is going to need a chair.*

---

The Trace theory he sent to McGonagall on the ninth of August, by Hedwig, and it was the first letter he had ever posted by owl, and he stood at the open window of the box room — Dudley’s room, now, by a negotiation Harry had not been party to and had watched with professional pleasure — and watched the white shape go up over the roofs of Little Whinging in the morning and turn north, and thought: *a post from everywhere*.

The letter was two pages, in the round hand, for a reader who was slow and had a department. It said what he thought the Trace watched for and why, and why it had never once registered a cat getting up off a kitchen floor or a pot warmed from forty yards or a feather that held, and it ended: *I have not tested this and shall not. I am telling you because you asked me on a pavement to tell you before I did anything impossible, and I think this may be a thing that is impossible for a reason, and I should like someone who knows the reason to look at it before I am within a mile of a place where it might matter.*

The reply came in two days, in green ink, on the back of his own second page, which he understood was a kind of compliment.

*Dr Potter. Noted. Your theory is, as far as I can judge, correct, and nobody at the Ministry has ever thought of it because nobody at the Ministry has ever met a wizard who did not shout. Do not test it. I have shown it to the Charms master, who has asked me to tell you that he has read your two pages four times and has a chair ready. — M.M.*

He read that eleven times, which was most of an hour, and put it in the biscuit tin with the Second Edition, because it was the first thing anyone on the far side had ever written to him about his work in their own hand, and it was two sentences long and had *a chair ready* in it, and he found he could not stop smiling at the ceiling of the cupboard for some time.

---

The goodbyes took the second half of the month, and he did them in order, because he had learned at five that the things you put off were the things you minded most, and he did not intend to arrive at King’s Cross with anything unsaid to anyone who had held a door.

Mr Pole first, on a Wednesday, in the geography room at Stonewall, over a board, because Mr Pole had lost to him every Wednesday for four years with undiminished pleasure and had earned a last game. Harry lost it. He did it carefully, over fifty-one moves, in a way that Mr Pole would not see and nobody else could have — a knight placed a square too far on the thirty-fourth move, a thing that looked like ambition and was a gift — and Mr Pole sat back at the end with his pipe-smelling jacket open and his face doing a thing it had not done in four years of Wednesdays and said, slowly, “Well, I’ll be *damned*,” and then, after a long moment, looking at the board, “You did that.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Why?”

“Because you’ve lost to me two hundred times and never once minded, and I wanted you to have one you could tell the club about.” He began putting the pieces back in the box. “And because I wanted to find out whether I could lose on purpose to someone who was really looking, and I could, and that’s worth knowing where I’m going.”

Mr Pole looked at the thirty-fourth move for a while longer.

“Son,” he said at last, “I have been playing this game for forty years and I have never seen a man throw a knight with that much *love*.” He shut the box. “Write to the club. Set us a problem a term. The juniors will want to know you’re alive.”

“There’s a post from everywhere, sir. I’ve got an owl.”

“Have you,” said Mr Pole, and did not ask, and shook his hand, and that was that.

Dr Lindqvist and Mrs Osei on the Thursday, in the room with the equipment that had cost more than the building. Mrs Osei said *not like that* once more, for the look of the thing, when he put a probe down the wrong way on purpose so that she could, and then she gave him a thing wrapped in a lab cloth, and it was a small brass instrument from a drawer that had not been opened since the 1950s, a thing for measuring a thing that nobody measured any more, and she said, “It still works. I checked. You never know what you’ll want to measure,” and Dr Lindqvist, from the doorway with his arms folded, said, “Potter. The fourth decimal place. Somebody will build on it one day and act very surprised. I should like you to know I shan’t,” and shook his hand, and Harry walked across the park to the copper roof for the last time with a brass instrument in his pocket and the strong sense that he had been given, by a technician, the single most useful object he would carry up the drive.

Carrow last of the university, on the Friday, in the office with the paper on every surface and the lake going grey outside, and she did not get up, because she did not do that, and she had the offprint of the 1979 paper on the desk between them — it had come that morning; eleven pages again, by a coincidence neither of them remarked on — and she pushed it an inch towards him, which was Carrow for *take it*.

“Scotland,” she said.

“Yes, Professor.”

“A sort of school.”

“Yes.”

“That you will not tell me about, and have never lied to me about, and are about to go off to for seven years with a doctorate and an owl.” She took her glasses off. “Potter. I have supervised nineteen students and I told nine countries you were the easiest. I did not tell them the other thing, because it was not their business, and I am going to tell you now, once, and you will not refer to it again.” She put the glasses back on. “You were the only one who ever made me *better*. Not quicker. Better. I have been a better mathematician since the day you walked up a long table with forty pages than I was in the thirty years before it, because I have had to be, to keep up, and I am fifty-two and I had not expected to get better again at anything.” She pushed a second thing across the desk. It was a stick of chalk, half used, from the board, in a paper sleeve. “That is from the back of the door. The overlap. I kept it. I find I should like you to have it, and I should like you to use it, wherever it is you are going, on whatever board they have, the first time you see a thing nobody else in the room can see. Which I imagine will be the first morning.”

He took the chalk. He could not, for a moment, say anything, and she saw that, and stood to one side of it.

“Write,” said Carrow. “Every term. You promised a physics teacher; you can promise me. And Potter — the comma on page ninety. It was right. Aldous was wrong. I checked.” The knife-face did its thing, one last time, at one corner. “Shut the door behind you. There’s a draught.”

---

Mrs Prentice on the Monday, after four, with the new aluminium ladder standing in the adult section like a stranger and the old one in the returns cupboard where she kept it.

He did not know how to begin, and so he did the crossword, and she let him, and at the end of it there was a seven in the bottom corner that was *farewell* and he did not fill it in.

“Well,” said Mrs Prentice, from the desk, not looking up from the stamp. “That’s that, then.”

“Yes.”

“Six years. You were five. You stood on the step and held up the whole of the Saturday morning and looked.” The stamp came down. “I knew then. I’ve had forty years at this desk and I knew in a second, and I thought: ah. There you are. Somebody’s got to hold the door.” She stamped another book. “And you held it back. The paper. The ladder. I never said. I’m saying now, once, and you’ll not refer to it.”

“No, Mrs Prentice.”

“Good.” She reached under the desk, into the place where the toffee tin had lived, and brought out a thing wrapped in a date-due slip, and it was a pencil. A stub of one. Worn to three inches, with teeth marks at the end, and the lead sharpened with a knife rather than a sharpener, the way a man does who has been sharpening pencils for forty years and does not hold with gadgets.

“His,” said Mrs Prentice. “The margins. *Surely not* and *lovely* and *enough for one night*. It was in the last box. I’ve had it in the tin since 1986 waiting for somebody worth giving it to, and I had decided it would be you by the cuckoo, and I have never once been wrong about a reader.” She pushed it across. “There’s a post from everywhere, I’m told. Only a question of stamps. The stamps are on me. They always were.”

He held the pencil. It weighed nothing. It was the heaviest thing he had ever been handed.

“Mrs Prentice,” he said. “Thank you. For October. For the Hilbert. For—”

“Oh, get on,” said Edith Prentice, who had never once in six years said *get on* to anyone and had plainly been saving it, and turned back to the stamp, and he saw that her hand was not quite steady, and did not refer to it, and went and sat with the crossword a while longer so that neither of them would have to say anything else, and then he went home.

The seven was *farewell*. He filled it in on the train, in the dead man’s pencil, and then sat with the paper on his knee for the rest of the line, not reading it, because he had read it, and because he wanted to sit with the clue.

---

Mr Haines he found by the simple method of going to Stonewall on a Tuesday in the holidays, when the building was empty and smelled of floor polish and the summer, and walking down the corridor to the physics room, where the door was open and a tall stooped man in a cardigan the colour of a wet pavement was writing on a board for nobody, because that was what Mr Haines did in August.

“Potter.”

“Sir.”

“Come to say goodbye, have you. Scotland.” He did not turn round. “The boy I told you about. The one who went. I never said what became of him, because I didn’t know, and I’ve wondered on and off for thirty years.” He put the chalk down. “Last month I found out. He wrote. Out of the blue, after thirty years, three pages, from an address I didn’t recognise, to say he had read about a boy of ten with a doctorate in the *Telegraph*, and it had put him in mind of a physics teacher in Surrey, and he thought he should say thank you while there was time, because he had meant to for thirty years and hadn’t.” Mr Haines turned round. “I don’t know what you are, Potter. I have known since question six that I don’t know. But I know that two boys have walked out of this room to somewhere I can’t follow, and the first one took thirty years to write, and I am too old to wait that long twice.”

“You won’t have to, sir. I’ve got an owl.”

Mr Haines’s eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch, and stayed there.

“An *owl*.”

“There’s a post from everywhere. You said so. It’s only a question of stamps.” Harry put his hand in his pocket and brought out the brass instrument Mrs Osei had given him and held it up. “And I’ve got this. It measures a thing nobody measures any more. I thought you might know what it was.”

Mr Haines looked at the instrument for a long moment. Then he took it, and turned it to the window, and his face did the thing it had done over question six.

“Good God,” he said quietly. “I haven’t seen one of these since 1958.” He handed it back. “Yes. I know what it is. Sit down, Potter. I shall tell you, and then you can go to Scotland, and you will write by the first of October or I shall come and find you, owl or no owl.”

He sat down. He was told. It took forty minutes, and it was the last physics lesson he had on that side of the curtain, and it was about a brass instrument from 1958 and a thing nobody measured any more, and he would not have traded it for the fourth floor.

---

Mrs Figg he saved for the last Saturday, in the garden, because he had saved everything that mattered for the garden since he was five.

The cats were in the attitudes of cats who had never had a bad day. Mr Tibbles sat on the wall with the lecture-hall face — fourteen now, and the hips had never once gone back — and watched the two of them on the bench with the air of an examiner who has turned up for the viva and found it has already been passed.

“Well,” said Mrs Figg.

“Yes.”

“Five years of that café. I shall miss the scones. I shall miss—” she stopped, and looked at the geraniums, and did not finish it, and he let her not.

“Mrs Figg,” he said. “I want to tell you a thing, and I want to say it in the garden because this is where I asked you, and this is where you said the word.” He had built the sentence on the train from Egham, and had thrown it out, and had decided to say it without building. “I was five, and I had been small for three years, and I decided in a cupboard to stop, and I came out and I told Mrs Prentice and I told Mrs Bell and I told my aunt one sentence, and all of those were hard, and none of them were the hardest. The hardest was you. Because you were the one who knew, and the one who was kind, and I was afraid that if I asked, you’d have to choose, and you’d choose him, and I’d lose the one person in Little Whinging who had the good biscuits.” He stopped. “You didn’t choose him. You wrote to him. And then you spent his scone money and told him after. And you held my hand through a wall. And I’ve never — I don’t think I’ve ever said, properly, in a way that wasn’t *thank you for writing* — I don’t think I’d have got to any of it without you. The university, the degree, the books, the street. You held the door the whole time. You just held it from two streets away so I wouldn’t see your hand on it.”

Mrs Figg looked at the geraniums for a long time.

“I was posted here,” she said at last, “to watch for cloaks in the wrong season. Five years I watched. I saw none. And I thought, in the first year, before you could talk: this is a waste of an old woman. Nothing is going to come up this road but the milk.” She made a fist of her right hand, and opened it. “And then a boy of three walked past my gate holding a shopping basket with both arms because he’d said he would, and looked at everything, and I thought: *ah*. There you are. I never once minded after that. Not the café. Not the scone money. Not the letter telling him he was wrong, though I expect I shall hear about that at some length.” She reached down beside the bench, where there was a thing in a carrier bag that he had not noticed because he had been looking at her, and brought it out, and it was the Forth Bridge tin, and it was full.

“For the train,” she said. “And for whoever you sit with. There’ll be somebody. There always is. Give them the good ones first; it’s how you tell who’s worth keeping.” She put the tin in his lap. “And Harry — the summers.”

“For the roof. I promised.”

“Not for the roof. For *me*. I shall want to know what they’re like. The teachers. The man I write to; I’ve never once seen him in a room with a child and I should like to know how he does. The Charms one who’s got a chair ready.” She looked at him sideways, with the gladness, and the pride, and the thing that was entirely steady now and had been for a year. “I shall want it all. In the garden. With the good tin. You can leave out the parts that aren’t mine.”

“There won’t be any of those,” said Harry.

“Oh, get on,” said Arabella Figg, and turned to the geraniums so that he would not see her face, which he had already seen, and would keep.

On the wall, Mr Tibbles looked at Harry with the lecture-hall face, and then — slowly, deliberately, like a man signing a form he had been waiting five years to sign — closed his eyes, and went to sleep in the sun.

---

Petunia did not say goodbye. It was not a thing the house permitted. But on the last night of August she stood in the doorway of the cupboard — she had never once, in eight years, stood in that doorway — and looked at the trunk, and the swing on the wall, and the date pinned beside it with its four lines underneath, and the camp bed with the biscuit tin showing under it, and the blue notebook on the blanket, and said, in the fence voice:

“You’ll want the box room when you’re back. Dudley can have it in term.”

“I’d rather keep this. If that’s all right.”

Petunia looked at the cupboard for a long moment, with the face with the wall out of it.

“It’s all right,” she said. And then, after a while, not in the fence voice: “I never came in here. Eight years. I thought it would be — I thought I’d see something I couldn’t stand.” She looked at the swing. “It’s a *study*.”

“Yes. It always was. From about three.” He stood up. “I’d like to leave the swing on the wall. And the date. If you wouldn’t mind not — if you wouldn’t mind leaving them.”

“I shan’t touch anything.” She turned to go, and stopped, and said, to the hall: “There are sandwiches in the fridge for the morning. And a flask. It’s a long way to Scotland.”

“Aunt Petunia.”

“What.”

“Thank you. For the roof.” He hesitated. “And for coming in. Both times. And for the box with the swing in it. And for—”

“*Harry*,” said Petunia Dursley, and her voice did a thing it had done once before, across a kitchen table, with a shoebox between them, “get some sleep. Your uncle wants to be on the road by eight so he can tell the Rotary about the traffic.”

She went. He sat on the camp bed in the smallest office in England with a trunk at his feet and a wand on the blanket and a dead man’s pencil in his pocket and a brass instrument from 1958 and a stick of chalk from the back of a door and a Forth Bridge tin full of the good ones, and he did not sleep, and he had known he would not, and for the last time under those stairs he took stock, in the blue pen, in English, in the forty-seventh pad.

*Eleven. The better part of two thousand, and I have stopped counting, and I am counting tonight because it is the last night I shall count here.*

*I have a telephone and have not used it, and have found out what it is by not using it, and I think it is going to be more than anyone knows. I have four hundred and three questions in order. I have a theory about the cold that a Charms master has read four times. I have the asking, and the seal, and a design for a press that will not be allowed and may be, and a woman in tartan who said* as it stands.

*I have a swing on a wall, and I am leaving it. I have a woman who held a door and a woman who held my hand and a woman who held a roof, and a man with a knife for a face who got better, and a man with chalk on his elbows who waited thirty years for a letter and will not wait that long again. I have a cousin who said good luck.*

*Tomorrow a wall at King’s Cross is going to push my attention somewhere else, and I am going to decline it, and go through, and sit on a train with a tin of biscuits, and give the good ones first.*

*Then the step. Then the blankets. Then the cold.*

*Then everything else.*

He closed the pad. He looked at the inside of the cupboard door one more time — the date, and *Faster*, and *They shout; I can hold*, and *He listened once; find out who else can*, and *The cold has a grammar* — and he did not add anything, because there was nothing left to add that could be written on a door.

Then he turned the light off, and lay in the dark, and listened to the house tick, for the last time as the only one who knew how long each tick was, and did not mind that at all.
