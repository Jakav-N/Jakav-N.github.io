# Chapter 23 — The Step

The door to the Headmaster’s office was a gargoyle that wanted a password, and he did not have one, and he had known since the train that he would not, and he had decided what to do about it on the stairs.

“I have an appointment,” he said to the gargoyle, at one minute to nine. “It was made in July of 1989 and confirmed on the thirty-first of July this year by the Deputy Headmistress. I don’t know the word. I’d be grateful if you’d tell him I’m here.”

The gargoyle looked at him. He looked at the gargoyle. It was, he decided, a sentence in a thing with a *listening* part, like the roof — a thing that checked what approached and asked it a question — and he stood still inside himself and let it ask, and did not try to answer in any way but the one he had, which was to be exactly who he was and wait.

The gargoyle moved.

The stair behind it went up by itself, which he had read about and had not believed, and he stood on it with his hands at his sides and let it carry him, and read the sentence in it as he went — a small one, old, cheerful, somebody’s idea of a joke in about 1400 — and arrived at a door, and the door was already open, and the room beyond it was full of morning.

---

It was a round room, and it was full of things that moved. He had read about the instruments, in *Hogwarts: A History*, and had not understood what they were for, and standing in the doorway with the sun coming in long and gold across them he understood at once that nobody else did either, because they were not instruments. They were *sentences*. A hundred of them, small and silver, each holding itself, each casting itself over and over on a side table, puffing and turning and ticking, and he could read perhaps a third of them from the door, and the third he could read were all *watching* something, and he thought: *he built a hundred eyes and set them on a shelf, and he sits in the middle of them*, and he understood something about the man before the man had said a word.

The man was behind the desk, in the big chair, with the beard to his belt and the half-moon spectacles, and he was not reading or writing or doing anything with tweezers. He was sitting with his hands folded on an empty blotter, waiting, the way a man waits who has been waiting for ten years and has decided not to pretend otherwise for the last minute of it.

And on a shelf behind him, between a battered sword in a case and a silver dish of something that smoked, sat a hat.

*Good morning, cousin*, said the hat, in the voice it had used in the dark. *He has been sitting like that since half past seven. I have been trying to make conversation and he keeps telling me to hush.*

Harry did not smile. He found it hard.

“Good morning, Professor Dumbledore.”

“Good morning, Dr Potter.” The voice was lighter than he had expected, and older, and had under it the thing he had read off the man’s face across the Great Hall: fear, and gladness, and the two of them not quite agreeing which was to speak first. “Come in. Sit down. There is a chair that has been the right height for a very small Charms master since 1971 and I have borrowed it for the morning. There is tea. It is very strong. I was told that was the requirement.”

Harry came in. He sat in Flitwick’s chair, which was the right height, and looked at the tea, which was very strong, and did not drink it yet.

Fawkes, on his perch, looked at him with a bird’s side-on regard, and then did a thing Harry had not read about in any book, which was to put his head down, briefly, the way Mr Tibbles had closed his eyes on a wall, and Harry thought: *the examiner has turned up and found it already passed*, and then put that away, because it was not the morning for jokes.

“You have a question,” said Dumbledore.

“I have four hundred and three. They’re in order. I’d like to ask the first one and then stop, and then ask the second, and then I think we’ll see.” He put his hands in his lap. “I was told, by two women who know you, that you would have an answer and that it would be good and true and not the whole thing. I asked for the whole thing through Mrs Figg. I’d like to ask for it again now, to your face, so that it’s on record that I did.” He looked at the man across the empty blotter. “Why did you leave me on the step?”

---

Dumbledore did not answer at once. He put his hand inside his robes, and brought out an envelope, and the envelope was not new — the corners were soft; it had been carried — and he put it on the blotter between them and did not push it across.

“I wrote this in July of 1989,” he said. “At midnight, at this desk, with your degree in a tray beside me and a book by a dead friend of mine that you had read. I wrote it because Arabella had told me you would ask, and I knew that when you did, I would be tempted to give you the version of the answer that I had at that moment, which would be better than the one I had in 1986 and worse than the one I have now, and that you would know, because Arabella says you have spent your life reading the sides of faces for the part that is not being said. So I wrote down what I believed before I met you, so that I should not be able to improve it.” He looked at the envelope. “It is the whole thing. It is the whole thing as it stood two years ago, and I have read it eleven times since, and I have not changed a word, and I find that I agree with all of it and that I am ashamed of about a third.”

He pushed it across.

“You may read it now, here, with me in the room, which I should prefer, because I should like to see your face. Or you may take it away and read it alone, which you have every right to. Or I may tell you, in my own voice, which will be worse, because I shall be tempted.”

Harry looked at the envelope. He had been reading sentences since he was six and had never once been handed one that had been *carried*.

“Here,” he said. “With you in the room. I’d like to see your face too.”

He opened it. He read it in the time it took the man across the desk to breathe in, and then he read it again at the speed of his hand, because he had decided somewhere between *Dear Harry* and *Albus Dumbledore* that this was a letter to be read slowly, in front of the man, so that the man could watch, and so that the watching would cost the man what it ought.

*I left you there because your mother died to keep you alive, and her death made a thing that I could use, and the only place it would hold was under her sister’s roof.*

*I left you there because I was afraid of what our world would make of you if it had you at fifteen months.*

*And I left you there because I was tired, Harry, and grieving, and I had made a great many decisions that autumn very quickly in the dark, and this was one of them, and I have never been certain it was right, and I did not go back to check, for eight years, because I was afraid that if I went back I would find I had been wrong and would not be able to bear it.*

*That is the third reason. It is the true one. It is the one I shall find hardest to say to your face.*

He read to the end. *If, when you have asked, you want it — ask for that too.* He had asked for it. Here it was.

He put the letter down on the blotter, face up, and looked at Albus Dumbledore across it, and the man was not looking at the letter. He was looking at Harry, and his face was doing a thing that Harry had seen on exactly one other face in his life, across a kitchen table in Surrey, over a shoebox, in the dark: the face of a person who has been standing in front of a nailed-shut drawer for ten years and has just watched someone else open it.

The office was very quiet. The instruments ticked. The hat, on its shelf, for once, said nothing at all.

“Thank you,” said Harry.

Dumbledore’s face did not move. “For—”

“For the third reason. The other two I’d worked out. I read the roof from the step when I was nine and I knew what the first one was; I read the index on a bench at seven and I knew what the second one was. I didn’t have the third. I couldn’t have. It isn’t the sort of thing you can read off a receipt.” He looked at the letter. “You were tired, and you were afraid you’d been wrong, and you didn’t go back and check. That’s — Professor, that’s the most *ordinary* thing anyone has ever told me. Everyone I’ve ever respected has done it once. Mrs Figg waited two years to let me ask her what she was because she was afraid of the answer. My aunt kept a shoebox for ten years and never once looked in it twice a year because she couldn’t bear what was in it. I made a rule at two and didn’t review it till I was five because I was afraid of what the review would say. The third reason isn’t a wizard’s reason. It’s a person’s.” He stopped. “I came in here with a question. I think I came in expecting to be angry. I had the anger ready; I’ve had it since I was four, in a drawer, the way my aunt had hers. And I find I can’t use it, because you’ve just handed me the one thing that makes it not apply, which is that you were a man in the dark who made a mistake and knew it and couldn’t look. I know what that is. I’ve been it.”

He picked up the tea, which was very strong, and drank some of it, because his mouth had gone dry and because he wanted the man to see him do an ordinary thing.

“I’m not forgiving you,” he said. “I want to be clear about that, because I think you’d like me to and I think it would be a lie, and I don’t lie to my aunt and I’m not going to start with you. It isn’t mine to forgive. It was done to a baby, and the baby isn’t here; I am. What I can do is what a colleague does when another colleague says *I made this mistake and here is exactly how*. I can say: yes. You did. Here is what it cost, in my words, so that it’s on the record in both our hands.” He set the cup down. “It cost a cupboard, which I chose and would choose again, and which the Quill wrote on an envelope and frightened Professor McGonagall, and which was the best room I’ve ever had. It cost ten years of a woman at a sink being afraid of me, which I ended with one sentence at five and could have ended at three if I’d known there was a sentence. It cost a warming charm I couldn’t read till I was eight and a roof I couldn’t read till I was nine. And it cost *you* — I can see what it cost you; it’s on your face; it was on it last night across the hall — it cost you ten years of not knowing, and reading my receipts, and asking me about Dementors through a squib because you’d forbidden yourself the front door.” He looked at the letter again. “That last one I mind about more than the cupboard. You could have *asked* me. At seven. At eight. I had four hundred questions and you had four and we could have had the whole conversation by owl and nobody would have had to be afraid. That’s the cost I’d like on the record. Not the step. The *silence after it*.”

Dumbledore did not say anything for a long time. Then he took his spectacles off, and did not polish them, and held them, and looked at the boy in Flitwick’s chair with eyes that were, without the half-moons, very old and very blue and entirely open.

“Yes,” he said. “Yes. That is the cost. You have put it more exactly than I did in two years of trying.” He put the spectacles back on. “Dr Potter, I have been a headmaster for thirty-five years and have had a great many conversations in this room in which a person told me what I had done wrong, and I have agreed with perhaps four of them, and I have never before been told by someone who had the right to be angry that they could not use the anger because I had handed them the thing that made it not apply. I do not know what to do with that. I shall have to think about it. I suspect I shall be thinking about it for some time.”

“Good,” said Harry. “That’s what the whole thing is for.” And then, because the man had given him the third reason and it had cost what it ought: “Professor. Thank you for the warming charm. I read it from memory when I was eight, in an examination hall, with my pen down. It was the best thing I had ever seen anyone write. I’ve seen the roof since and it’s better, but the charm was first. You wrote it in the time it took to walk down a path with a basket, and it *checked*. It didn’t warm. It asked how cold I was and gave exactly the difference. I’ve built my whole — I’ve built a great deal on that bracket. I wanted you to know that the first sentence I ever heard was one of yours, and that it listened.”

The man across the desk closed his eyes.

“Minerva said,” he said, after a moment, with them still closed, “that you would arrive as a person to whom one had better not come with the answer already in one’s pocket. I had it in my pocket for two years. I find I should have had something else in there as well, and I do not know what, and I think you have just told me.” He opened his eyes. “A handkerchief, possibly.”

“I’ve got one,” said Harry. “My aunt packed four. Would you like it?”

Albus Dumbledore looked at the Boy Who Lived for a long moment across his own letter, and then — Harry saw it start at the corner of the beard and go up — laughed. Not the small smile from the hall. An actual laugh, short and startled and real, the laugh of a man who had braced for ten years against a blow and had been handed a handkerchief instead.

“No,” he said. “No, thank you. I have my own. I have not needed it in this room in some time.” He folded his hands on the blotter again, over the letter, and the fear had gone out of his face, and what was left was the gladness, and the sureness, and the kindness, all three, which Figg had said was what he was like, and Figg had never once been wrong about a face. “The second question. You said there would be a second, and then we would see.”

“The second is the roof,” said Harry. “The whole of it. I read it; I have most of it; there’s a bracket at the heart of it about blood that I couldn’t get into. I’d like to know what it is, and what it’s against, and whether what it’s against is still out there, because the books say *vanished* and I know what careful books are for.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “Yes, I thought that would be second. Drink your tea, Dr Potter. This one takes longer.”

---

It took an hour, and it was the first hour of Harry’s life in which an adult told him everything he asked and did not stop at the drawer.

The roof was Lily. He had known that — he had seen the shape on the swing at the heart of it — but he had not known *how*, and Dumbledore told him how, plainly, with his hands folded, the way a man tells a colleague a result: that a mother had stood between a wand and a child and had been given the choice to step aside and had not, and that the not-stepping had *made* something, a sentence of a kind that no wizard could write on purpose, because it was written in the only ink that could not be bought, and that the sentence said, as near as it could be put, *this one is paid for*. And that it had held — had turned a curse that had never once been turned, and had broken the man who cast it — and had gone on holding, in the child’s skin, and would go on holding as long as the child was a child and had a home with the blood of the one who had paid.

“The bracket you could not read,” said Dumbledore, “is the one I could not write. I did not write the roof, Dr Potter. I *anchored* it. Your mother wrote it. I found it on you, on the step, still warm, and I built a house round it the way one builds a wall round a spring, and the wall is mine and the spring is hers, and the part you could not get into is the spring.”

Harry sat very still in the small chair.

“That’s why it listened,” he said. “The roof. I thought it was your hand. It’s got your hand all over the outside. But the listening part — the part that checks for intent and asks — that’s not yours. That’s *hers*. You built round a thing that was already asking.”

“Yes.”

“She could *do that*.” It was not a question. “The thing I do. The asking. Not the way I do it — not on purpose, not with marks — but once, when it mattered, with everything she had, she asked, and the world said yes.” He looked at his hands. “Mrs Figg said she was the same. *You find the door they already use. Your mother was the same; everyone said.* I thought she meant the people. I don’t think she only meant the people.”

Dumbledore was quiet for a moment.

“No,” he said. “No, I do not think she did. Lily Evans was the finest natural Charms student I ever taught, and Filius will tell you the same, and neither of us ever once saw her *shout*.”

He put that on the shelf. It went in warm, and then it went in deep, and he left it there to do what it would do, because there was more, and the more was colder.

What the roof was against was a man who was not dead. Dumbledore said so plainly, without the careful words, and Harry had known it and found that knowing it and hearing it in that voice in that room were two different things. A wraith. Something that had been a man and had made itself into a thing that could not die in the ordinary way, by means Dumbledore said he was not yet certain of and would tell Harry when he was, and that had been broken on a step in Godric’s Hollow and had gone somewhere — a forest, a mountain, a mind — and was waiting, and would, Dumbledore was as sure as he had ever been of anything, come back. The roof was against *that*. The sentry in the mackintosh was against that. The ten years of not coming up a path were, in part, against that, because a path walked up by Albus Dumbledore was a path that could be watched.

“I should have told you at seven,” said Dumbledore. “By owl. You are right about the silence. I had reasons for it and they were the second kind — the fear kind — and I shall not dress them up for you now.”

“No,” said Harry. “Don’t.” He had his hands in his lap and was looking at the hundred silver sentences on the side tables, the third of them he could read, all watching something. “Those are for him. Aren’t they. The instruments. You built a hundred eyes and set them watching for a man who isn’t dead.”

“Some of them. Some of them watch the roof. One of them—” Dumbledore paused, and Harry saw him decide to go through a door he had been standing beside “—one of them has watched *you*, Dr Potter, since the autumn of 1981. Not what you did. Whether you were alive, and whether you were in the house, and whether the roof held. It is the small one, on the end, that turns clockwise. It has never once stopped.”

Harry looked at the small one on the end. It turned clockwise. He read its sentence, which was short, and simple, and had been cast once in a terrible autumn by a tired man and had held itself for ten years, and it said, as near as he could put it, *is he still there*.

He had thought he had been the only one in the room for ten years. He had not been. There had been a small silver thing on the end of a shelf in Scotland, asking, every second, in a sentence he could read, *is he still there*, and the answer had always been yes.

“All right,” he said, after a while. It came out less steady than he had meant it to, and he let it. “All right. That one I’ll forgive you for. Not the rest. That one.”

“I shall take that,” said Dumbledore gravely, “as the first instalment.”

---

He did not tell the man about the door.

He had decided on the stool, in the dark, with a hat that was the cold’s cousin saying *not alone, and not who you think*, and he had reviewed the decision on the stair at one minute to nine and found it held. The man across the desk had just told him everything he asked. He had told him the third reason and the spring and the small silver thing on the end. He had been, for an hour, the most honest adult Harry had ever sat across from, including a woman with a knife for a face, and the grain behind the door was cold and small and did not like him and was — Harry was nearly sure now, with *wraith* said aloud in the room — was *of* the thing the roof was against, and the man across the desk had a hundred eyes set watching for that thing, and if Harry said *I think there is a piece of it in me*, every one of the hundred eyes would turn, and the man’s face would do a thing Harry did not want to see on it yet, and the door would be opened by the wrong hands at the wrong time with the whole of a castle watching.

*Not alone. Not who you think.*

So he put it back behind the door, and put the door on the shelf, and asked the third question instead, which was the one that had been second on the list since a lounge in Surrey.

“Professor. I asked Professor McGonagall whether the Statute would let me warm a Muggle hospital. She said *as it stands*. She said you sit on the body that decides what it stands as. I have a design for a thing that presses a charm into an object, over and over, exactly, and I think it works, and nobody has built one because nobody could hold the first copy steady, and I can. The first charm I’d press is yours. The one from the step. Into blankets. For a ward in London where a girl of six had her photograph in a newspaper two years ago because the heating was a line in a budget.” He had said it to McGonagall in a lounge and it had carried; he said it now in a round room full of eyes and found it carried further. “I want to know what it would take.”

Dumbledore looked at him for a long time.

“Minerva told me you had asked her that,” he said. “She told me in the voice she uses for things that have frightened her, which I have heard perhaps six times in sixty years. I have thought about it for five weeks.” He unfolded his hands. “It would take a case. Not an argument — I have heard your argument, through Minerva, and it is a good one, and the Wizengamot does not sit to hear good arguments; it sits to hear *cases*. A thing that exists. A charm that has been pressed. A blanket that has been warmed. A Muggle who has been warmed by it and did not know and came to no harm. A reason why the Statute’s own preamble — which you have read, and I have read, and perhaps nine other people now living have read — does not forbid it. And a man who sits on the body to bring the case, in a form it cannot send back for want of a comma.” He put his hands flat on the blotter. “Bring me the case, Dr Potter. Build the thing. Press the charm. Show me a blanket. Give me a Muggle who was warm. Then I shall take it to the Wizengamot, and I shall lose, the first time, because they always lose the first time, and then we shall see.”

“How long?”

“How long do you need?”

Harry thought about a biscuit tin under a camp bed, and a very small man with a chair ready, and a girl who argued with hats.

“Give me a term,” he said.

Dumbledore’s eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch, and Harry thought of Mr Haines, and nearly smiled.

“A *term*.”

“I’ve had the design since March. I’ve had the charm since I was fifteen months old. I’ve never had a wand or a room or anyone who could see the marks, and as of last night I have all three. A term.” He paused. “And I’ll want to use Professor Flitwick. And the hospital wing. And I’ll want Hermione Granger in every room I’m in, because she chases, and because I have been the only one in the room for ten years and I’m told it’s better.”

“You may have Filius; he has been asking me for you since February of 1987. You may have Poppy, if you can persuade her, which you will, because you will ask her properly. You may have Miss Granger in any room she consents to be in, which I suspect will be all of them.” Dumbledore sat back. “And Dr Potter. We shall meet. Here. Once a week, for an hour, at a time of your choosing, and you will ask me whatever is next on your list and I will answer it, and when I cannot I shall say so and say why, and you will tell me what you have built, and when I think it is dangerous I shall say so, and you will listen, and we shall each of us do the other the courtesy of assuming the other has reasons.” He paused. “That is what I do with colleagues. I have not had one who was eleven. I find I should like to try.”

“Thursdays,” said Harry. “At four. I had Thursdays at four for five years with a professor of mathematics and I’d like to keep them.”

“Thursdays at four,” said Dumbledore, and wrote it down, on the blotter, under the letter, in a hand that slanted and was kind.

---

He stood up to go, at ten past ten, with four hundred questions left and a term, and at the door he stopped, because there was a thing he had been not saying for an hour and a half, since the Great Hall, since four seconds at a staff table, and he had decided on the stair that he would say it last, when the man had earned it, and the man had.

“Professor. One more. It isn’t on the list; it’s from last night.”

“Yes?”

“There’s something wrong with Professor Quirrell’s face.”

Dumbledore did not move. But the hundred silver sentences on the side tables did a thing, all at once, that Harry had not seen them do in an hour and a half, which was to go, for a quarter of a second, perfectly still.

“Go on,” said Dumbledore, very quietly.

“I read faces. It’s the only thing I’m actually good at; everything else came from it. I read his for four seconds across the hall and I read it again at breakfast for eleven and I’ve been reading it since, and it isn’t one face. It’s one face being *held* by two people who haven’t agreed how. The nervousness is real; it’s his. There’s something under it that isn’t nervous at all, and isn’t his, and it looks at things — it looked at *me* — the way the small silver one on the end looks at the roof.” He kept his voice level, because the sentence had been built on the stair and he wanted it to carry. “I don’t know what that means. I don’t have a word for it; I have seven languages and none of them have it. I know what a face looks like when two people are in it, because I have been reading one face for ten years that had a woman in it and a fear in it and they never once agreed, and this is like that, except that the second one isn’t a fear. It’s a *person*. And it’s cold.”

The office was silent. Fawkes had put his head up.

Albus Dumbledore looked at the boy in the doorway for a long moment, and his face did not do any of the things Harry had seen it do that morning. It did a new thing. It went *old*. Not tired; old, the way the castle was old, the way a sentence is old that has been holding itself for a very long time and has just felt, for the first time in years, something lean on it.

“Dr Potter,” he said. “Sit down again. Please. I think we had better move Thursday to now.”

Harry sat.

On the shelf behind the desk, between the sword and the dish, the hat said, very quietly, in the voice it had used in the dark, *Oh, cousin. Oh, well done. He has been pretending not to see that for a fortnight and nobody in this castle could tell him so but you*, and then said nothing more, because the man was speaking, and what the man said took the rest of the morning, and it was the beginning of the first thing they ever did together, and it was not on the List, and it was, Harry would think later, exactly the sort of thing the List had been for.
