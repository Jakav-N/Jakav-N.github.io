# Chapter 26 — The Shut Door

The dungeon was cold, and the man at the front of it had a face like a door that had been shut on purpose, and he began the register, and when he reached the P’s he stopped.

“Ah, yes,” said Professor Snape, softly, to the room. “Harry Potter. Our new — *celebrity*.”

The Slytherins at the back made the sound Slytherins made. The pale boy among them did not; Harry noted that, and noted that the pale boy was watching not Snape but *him*, with the face from a stool in a shop, and filed it. The Ravenclaws did not make any sound at all, because they had been in a Charms room on Wednesday and a Transfiguration room after it and had learned, in a week, that making a sound when Harry Potter was addressed was a way of missing something.

Harry said nothing. He had decided on the stairs down — most of an afternoon — what he would do in this room, and the first thing he would do was not answer anything that was not a question.

Snape finished the register. He put the parchment down. He looked at the room with the shut-door face and said the thing about there being no foolish wand-waving here, and about bottled fame and brewing glory and stoppering death, and Harry listened to all of it with a quarter of his mind, because it was good — it was *very* good; it was a man who loved a subject speaking about it in the only register he had left — and with the other three-quarters he watched the face.

He had read it for four seconds across the Great Hall and eleven at breakfast and some minutes since, in corridors, at the staff table, and he had the shape of it now and did not like the shape, because it was not a shape he could do anything about. It was a face that had been shut for a long time, by its owner, from the inside, the way his own cupboard door had been shut, except that this man had not chosen the cupboard. He had been *put* in it, by something, a long time ago, and had decided to stay, and had nailed the door, and the nails had rusted. Harry knew the shape. He had seen it on his aunt for ten years. He had never once seen it on a man.

And under the shut door — he had seen this on the first night, in a quarter-second, and had filed it under *her* and not understood it — there was a thing that happened to the face when it looked at Harry’s *eyes*. Not the scar. The room looked at the scar; Snape did not; Snape looked at the eyes, and the door *flinched*, every time, the way Petunia’s had flinched across a kitchen table at the word *eyes*, and then shut harder.

He did not know what it meant. He was going to.

“Potter.”

It had been eleven seconds since the register. He had expected longer.

“What would I get if I added powdered root of asphodel to an infusion of wormwood?”

The room went still. Hermione’s hand went up beside him and he put his own hand, under the desk, on her wrist, lightly, and she lowered it, and looked at him, and he did not look back, because he was looking at the man.

“The Draught of Living Death, sir. It’s in *Magical Drafts and Potions*, chapter twelve, but the proportions in chapter twelve are for a standard cauldron and the book doesn’t say so, and the standard cauldron it means is a Size Two pewter, which isn’t the one on the first-year list. If you brew it to the chapter in the cauldron on the list you get something that will put a person to sleep for about a week and give them a headache, which isn’t death and isn’t living either.” He kept his voice level and his hands flat. “I don’t know whether you wanted the book answer or the real one. I’ve given you both. I’m sorry if that was more than you asked.”

Snape did not move.

“Where,” he said, after a moment, “would you look if I told you to find me a bezoar.”

“In the stomach of a goat, sir, which is where the book says, and which is true, and which is also useless, because nobody keeps a goat in a cupboard. In the student stores, second shelf, left, in a jar labelled in a hand that isn’t yours; I read the labels on Tuesday when I came down to find the room. There are four. Two are old. I’d use the newer ones; a bezoar loses about a tenth of its — I don’t know your word; its *asking* — every ten years, which the book doesn’t say but the Healing text does, in the fourth chapter, two pages before a dosage table that has its columns the wrong way round and has killed two people.”

Something happened at the corner of the shut door. It was not a flinch. It was the thing Harry had seen on Carrow over page nine and on Aldous over page ninety and had not expected to see here: a professional, given cause, deciding not to show it.

“The *difference*,” said Snape, very quietly, “between monkshood and wolfsbane.”

“There isn’t one, sir. They’re the same plant. It also goes by aconite. The three names are because three different sets of people needed it for three different things and named it for the thing they needed, and the book lists it under all three without saying they’re the same, which I think is the single worst piece of indexing in the Standard series and I’ve read all seven and the two that come after.” He paused. “That one *was* the book answer. I don’t think there’s a real one that’s different. I checked.”

The dungeon was very quiet. Somewhere at the back, a Slytherin who had been about to laugh had decided not to.

Snape looked at him down the length of the room, with the door shut, and the thing at the corner of it that was not a flinch.

“You have read,” he said, “*all seven*.”

“Yes, sir. And Waffling, and the Healing text, and the two Advanced ones, and a thing on the theory of enchantment that the Headmaster told me to read chapter nine of twice. I was told the first-year list when I was seven and I didn’t see why I should stop there.” He had not meant to say that; he had decided on the stairs to answer only questions; but the man had asked three and had meant them, and Harry had learned at a long table that when someone asked three questions and meant them, you gave them the fourth for nothing. “I’ve read the theory, sir. I haven’t *brewed*. I’ve never had a cauldron or a fire I was allowed to use or anyone to say *not like that*. I know what everything on your shelves does and I don’t know what any of it feels like in a ladle. If you want to find out where I’m bad at this, that’s where. I’d rather you found it than didn’t.”

Snape did not say anything for a long time, for him; it was four seconds, which was most of a morning, and Harry spent the morning watching the door, and the door did not open, but something behind it moved, once, like a man in a shut room shifting his weight.

“Then we shall find out,” said Snape. “Pairs. The boil cure. Page four. Mr Potter will work *alone*.”

---

He was bad at it.

Not very. He had Mrs Osei’s hands, which were careful, and Mr Haines’s *put your hand up and I’ll come over*, and sixteen years of holding a wand without sending anything down it, and all of that went into a ladle and a fire and a cauldron that was the one on the list and not a Size Two pewter. But he had never once done it, and the doing was a different thing from the knowing, the way the half of physics that lived in a room had been a different thing, and he found out where he was bad at it in the first ten minutes, which was *heat*.

He could read the book. He could read, for that matter, the *potion* — there was a sentence in it, faint, forming, as the dried nettles went in and the snake fangs after, a thing coming together in the cauldron that had the same spine as everything else and a bracket in the middle that was about *skin*, and he could watch it build, which he suspected nobody else in that room could. But the book said *simmer*, and he did not know what a simmer *looked* like, because he had never stood over one, and he had the flame a quarter-turn too high for a full minute before he saw the sentence in the cauldron begin to go ragged at one edge and understood why.

He turned it down. The sentence steadied. He made a note, in his head, where he kept the notes that had *not like that* in them: *simmer is when the bubbles come up singly and break without sound. Nobody wrote that down because everybody knew.*

Snape came past at the half-hour. He did not stop. He looked into the cauldron for a second — Harry saw him read the colour, which was right, and the surface, which was right now, and the faint ring on the inside of the pewter a quarter-inch above the liquid, which was where it had been for a minute at the wrong heat and which no one who had not stood over ten thousand cauldrons would have seen — and said, without pausing, in the soft voice:

“Too hot. For how long?”

“About a minute, sir. At the start. I didn’t know what a simmer looked like.”

“And now?”

“Single bubbles. No sound. I’ll know next time.”

Snape went on down the row. He did not say anything else. But Harry, who was watching the back of a shut door go away from him between two rows of cauldrons, saw the shoulders do a thing — very small, a quarter-inch, the thing Mrs Figg’s had done at a sink the night of a cup of tea — that was a man putting down, for one step, something he had been carrying a long way, and picking it up again before anyone could see.

The potion, at the end, was correct. Snape looked at it, and at the ring a quarter-inch up the pewter, and said, “Adequate,” and moved on, and Harry understood that the word had cost him something and had been paid anyway, and put it on the shelf beside Carrow’s *hm*.

At the back, the pale boy’s potion had gone orange, and the pale boy was looking at Harry’s cauldron with an expression that had nothing of his father in it at all.

Neville found him in the courtyard at break with the face of a boy who had just been through the same dungeon on the other side of the timetable and had not come out of it.

“He asked me what a bezoar was,” said Neville. He sat down on the bench beside Harry without being asked, which was new, and good, and Harry noted it. “In front of everyone. And I *knew*, Harry, I knew it was the goat, Gran’s got one in a jar, and I opened my mouth and it just — went. And he said *clearly fame isn’t everything, Longbottom, and neither is a famous grandmother*, and Ron said something and lost us five points and I melted the cauldron.” He looked at his hands. “I melted a *cauldron*. On the first day. He said he’d never seen it done in the first ten minutes.”

Harry looked at him. Neville Longbottom, round and frightened and eleven, with a toad in his pocket that Harry had found behind a pipe, sitting on a bench in a courtyard in Scotland having had the whole of himself taken out by a man with a shut door, and Harry had two hundred times as long as anyone else to sit and feel how real it was. Knowing a thing is small does not make it light to the one carrying it. He had written that at seven, about Dudley and a times table, and it was still true, and it was still the only thing in the world worth knowing about teaching.

He thought about the front door, and rejected it. Neville had been through the front door that morning and it had a man behind it with nails in it.

“Neville,” he said. “What’s a bezoar made of?”

“It’s — it’s a stone. From a goat.”

“What’s it made *of*, though. What’s in it. Why does it work.”

Neville looked at him. “I don’t — the book doesn’t—”

“The book doesn’t. Your gran’s jar does. You’ve looked at it, haven’t you. On the shelf. For years.”

“…Yes.”

“What colour is it?”

“Grey. Sort of — layered. Like a — like an onion, if you cut it. Gran said it builds up. In the goat. Round a bit of grass it couldn’t digest.” Neville’s voice had changed, a little, without his noticing, the way Dudley’s had changed over the sixes in a football rhyme. “It’s *plant* in the middle. It’s a plant the goat couldn’t — and then the goat puts stuff round it, year after year, and that’s the stone.”

“And what does a bezoar *do*?”

“It’s an antidote. For most poisons.”

“And what are most poisons?”

Neville opened his mouth, and shut it, and Harry watched the thing happen on his face that had happened on Hermione’s over a light and Terry Boot’s over a match, the *oh* coming up from somewhere under the fright like a plant coming up through a paving slab.

“Plants,” said Neville. “Most poisons are plants. Monkshood. Belladonna. Hemlock. They’re — they’re all *Herbology*, Harry, I know all of those, I know what they *look* like, Gran’s got half of them in the—” He stopped. “It’s a plant stone that stops plant poisons. It’s — the whole thing’s plants. The whole *subject*. It’s Herbology with a fire under it.”

“Yes,” said Harry. “It is. You know more about half his shelves than anyone in that room, including me, because I’ve read them and you’ve *grown* them. He asked you what a bezoar was and you froze because he asked it like a man who wanted you to fail, and you’d never had anyone ask you anything about a plant who wanted you to fail, and it wasn’t the question. It was the door he asked it through.” He stood up. “Next time he asks you a thing, don’t look at him. Look at the jar. In your head. On your gran’s shelf. You’ll know. You always knew. You just didn’t know it was the same shelf.”

Neville sat on the bench looking at his hands, and then at Harry, and the fright was still in his face and would be for years, but there was a thing beside it now that had not been there at breakfast.

“You did that on purpose,” he said slowly. “The questions. You walked me round to it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Harry thought about a kitchen table, and a tennis ball on the stairs, and a cousin who had got to twelve twelves with one wobble and had come home two feet taller and had said *you just have to not be thick about it*, and had, four years later, handed him a cap on a station concourse.

“Because you almost never move a person by the front door,” he said. “You find the door they already use. Yours is plants. It’s a good door. It opens onto more of the castle than you think.” He picked up his bag. “And because I’ve been the boy nobody asked properly, and it’s a cold place, and I don’t leave people in it. I told Malfoy that on the train. I meant it about him and I mean it more about you.”

He went to Herbology. Neville sat on the bench a while longer, and then — Harry saw it from the door — got out a quill and began, in the back of his Potions book, in a round careful hand, to write down the names of the plants on his grandmother’s shelf, and what each one was for.

---

Thursday at four was a round room full of eyes and a very strong cup of tea, and he reported the face.

He had kept a tally. Eleven sightings since Monday: breakfast, three corridors, dinner, a staff-table glance across the Hall, the back of a turban going up a staircase. He gave them in order, with the times, and what each one had shown, in the plain words he would have used for Carrow about a proof: *the second one is more present at meals. The second one looks at the Slytherin table and the first one looks at his plate. On Tuesday at eleven past eight the second one looked at me for a full second and the first one did not know it had happened; I watched the face not know.*

Dumbledore listened with his hands folded and did not interrupt, and when Harry had finished he said, “Thank you,” in a voice that meant it, and then, after a moment: “You have not asked me the fourth question.”

“No, sir. I said a fortnight. It’s Thursday. I’ll ask on Thursday week.” He drank the tea. “I’ve got another, though. It’s on the list; it’s number eleven; I’m moving it up because of this morning.”

“Go on.”

“Professor Snape flinches when he looks at my eyes. Not the scar; everyone looks at the scar. The eyes. He did it on the first night and he did it in the dungeon this morning, twice, and then he shut the door harder. My aunt did the same thing across a kitchen table when she told me I had my mother’s eyes. Mrs Figg said everyone said I had my mother’s eyes. Professor McGonagall got a photograph out on Tuesday of a girl in 1971 with red hair who turned a match back and said *oh*.” He put the cup down. “And my aunt told me there was a boy. Before Hogwarts. From the bad end of their town, with hair that wanted washing, who knew what my mother was before she did, and told her, and my aunt hated him for it for thirty years.” He looked at the man across the desk. “Professor Snape’s hair wants washing. He teaches a subject that is Herbology with a fire under it. He flinches at my eyes and shuts the door. I don’t know what the question is yet. I know there is one. I’d like you to tell me whether I’m reading the right face.”

Dumbledore did not say anything for a long moment. The hundred eyes ticked.

“You are reading the right face,” he said at last. “And I am not going to tell you what is behind it, Dr Potter, because it is not mine. It is his, and it is the one thing in this castle that I have never once pushed through a door, in eleven years of having the right to, and I am not going to start by handing it to his student in the first week.” He paused. “But I will tell you this, since you asked whether you were reading it right. You are. And the door is not nailed from the outside. He nailed it. And he has had the nails in his pocket the whole time, and has never once, in eleven years, taken them out, and I have stopped asking him to.”

Harry sat with that.

“He put the right ones in,” he said slowly. “Nails. From the inside. The way I did a cupboard door at three.” He looked at the tea. “Then it’s a front-door question. He’ll only open it himself, and only if someone stands outside and asks properly and doesn’t push. I know how that works. I did it to my aunt. It took a cup of tea and five years.”

“It may take longer than five years.”

“I’ve got seven here. And I’m — Professor, I’m good at waiting. It’s the one thing I was good at before I was good at anything.” He finished the tea. “I’m not going to ask him yet. I’m going to be *adequate* in his dungeon for a term and let him find out where I’m bad at it and say so, because he found the ring on the pewter this morning and nobody else alive could have, and a man who can see a quarter-inch of heat on a cauldron can see when he’s being handled, and I don’t want to handle him. I want to ask him. When he’s ready. Which I’ll know, because I read faces, and his is the only one in this castle that I can’t read all the way to the back, and I think that’s because there’s somebody standing behind it holding the door, and I think I know who, and I think she had red hair.”

Dumbledore closed his eyes.

“Thursday week,” he said, after a while, with them still closed. “Ask me the fourth. I shall have the answer. And Dr Potter — the ring on the pewter. He mentioned it. At lunch. To Minerva. He said *the boy saw me see it*, in a voice I have not heard him use about a student in eleven years, and Minerva asked him what voice it was, and he did not answer, and I have been thinking about it since.” He opened his eyes. “It was not an unkind voice. That is the whole of what I can tell you about it. I thought you should have that.”

“Thank you,” said Harry, and meant it, and put it on the shelf under *her*, beside a flinch and a photograph and a sentence about an awful boy, and went to Herbology, and did not think about anything else for the whole of the way down, which was a long way, by his own clock, and not nearly long enough.

---

He wrote it in the forty-seventh pad that night, by the window, with the mountains outside and the castle singing, and he wrote it carefully, because it was the first thing he had written in a week that he did not understand.

*A man with a shut door flinches at my eyes and found a quarter-inch of heat on a pewter cauldron that nobody else could have seen, and said "adequate" as if it cost him, and said to McGonagall at lunch "the boy saw me see it" in a voice she had never heard him use. My aunt knew a boy from the bad end of town with hair that wanted washing who told my mother what she was before she knew. The Headmaster says I am reading the right face and will not tell me what is behind it because it is not his, and that the nails are on the inside, and that he has stopped asking.*

*I know what a door nailed from the inside is. I had one. Somebody with a cup of tea stood outside mine and did not push and I came out. It took five years. I have seven.*

*I am not going to handle him. I am going to be bad at his subject honestly, in front of him, every Friday, and let him find where, and say thank you when he does, because he is right every time and because I think nobody has said thank you to that man in a very long while and I would like to find out what his face does when someone does.*

*Neville said oh. Over a bezoar. That is three.*

*The pale boy's potion went orange and he looked at mine with nothing of his father in his face. Four, nearly. Not yet.*

He closed the pad. Hedwig was not on the sill; she was somewhere over Surrey with a letter for the house and one for a librarian and one for a man with chalk on his elbows, and the room was quiet, and the castle sang, and he lay down by the window and did not sleep, and found that the thing he was thinking about was not the List, or the term, or the press, or the cold.

It was a man in a dungeon, with the nails in his pocket, who had seen a quarter-inch of heat and said *adequate*, and had flinched, twice, at a dead woman’s eyes in a living boy’s face, and had not once looked at the scar.

*Her*, he thought. *He looks at her. Everyone else looks at the morning they woke up to. He looks at her.*

He did not know what to do with that yet. He put it on the shelf, next to the photograph, and found that the shelf had got a little warmer, and did not know why, and let it be.
