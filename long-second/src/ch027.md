# Chapter 27 — Two People Holding It

Defence Against the Dark Arts smelled of garlic, and the man teaching it stuttered, and the stutter was real, and Harry had known it was real since the first syllable, and that was the problem.

He had been watching the face for nine days by then — fourteen sightings, each with its time and what it had shown, in a column at the back of the forty-seventh pad, which was for Thursdays now and for the reader in fifty years, under a heading he had not been able to bring himself to make any plainer than *Q.* — and he had the shape of it the way he had the shape of Snape’s door and McGonagall’s pig. Two people. One of them nervous, and thin, and clever in a frightened way, and *there*, in the ordinary sense, the way everyone was there: a man who had gone somewhere and come back wrong and knew it and had built a stutter and a turban and a smell of garlic round the knowing like a wall round a spring. And the other one.

He did not have a word for the other one. He had seven languages and none of them had it. It was not a face. It was a thing that *used* the face, sometimes, for a second, the way you might use a window — a cold, still, perfectly attentive looking-out that borrowed the first man’s eyes and did not borrow the stutter, because it had no need of one, because it was not afraid of anything in the room.

Except — he had seen this on the Tuesday, at eleven past eight, and had written it down and had not told the Headmaster the whole of it — except, perhaps, him.

“The — the — the Dark Arts,” said Professor Quirrell, from the front of a room with the curtains half drawn, “are n-not a *thing*, you understand, not a — a list. They are a — an *approach*. One m-must — one must learn to recognise the approach. Before one can—” He stopped, and swallowed, and looked at his notes, and the first man’s hands shook, slightly, on the lectern.

Harry watched the hands. He had learned at two that hands told you what faces had decided not to. The first man’s hands were afraid. They had been afraid since the man walked in. And then, for a quarter of a second — most of a minute, in the only hours Harry lived in — the hands went still, utterly, the way a cat’s go still on a wall, and the face above them turned, by about four degrees, towards the back of the room where the Ravenclaws sat, and the eyes in it were not the first man’s eyes at all, and they found Harry, and *rested*.

He did not look away. He had decided on the stair at nine o’clock a fortnight ago that he would not. He sat with his hands flat on the desk and let the thing look, and looked back, and read it, and what he read was this: that it *knew* him. Not the way the room knew him — the scar, the story, the morning. The way the small silver instrument on the end of the Headmaster’s shelf knew him. *Is he still there.* Except that this one was not glad.

And behind the door in him — the door he had put on a cold small grain at seven on a train home with a book against his leg, the door the hat had found in the dark and said *do not open alone* — something *moved*.

Not much. A shift, the way a sleeper shifts. The grain behind the door had been still for four years, small and cold and disliking him, and it had never once *moved*, and now, with a thing in a man’s eyes resting on him from the front of a classroom, it turned over, once, like a thing that has heard its name said in another room.

The door held. He had built it at seven and reinforced it at nine and it held, and he sat very still and felt it hold, and the quarter-second ended, and the face at the front was the first man’s again, and the hands shook, and the stutter came back, and Professor Quirrell said, “—b-before one can — can hope to — yes. Page six,” and twenty-six Ravenclaws opened their books, and Harry opened his, and did not read a word of page six, because he was writing, in his head, at the back of the pad, under *Q.*, the fifteenth entry, and the entry said:

*Friday, 9.14. It looked. Thirty seconds, mine. It knows me the way the instrument knows me and it is not glad. And the thing behind my door moved. First time in four years. I do not know what that means. I know that it means the two are not strangers.*

*Thursday cannot come fast enough. That is the first time I have ever written that sentence.*

---

He did not tell Hermione.

He wanted to. He had never once in his life *wanted* to tell anyone anything — there had never been anyone who could be told; he had told a hat and a dead woman in a dream — and he found, walking out of a room that smelled of garlic with a girl beside him saying something about page six, that the wanting was a physical thing, like the hunger had been, and that it had a shape, and that the shape was *her*.

But the Headmaster had said *watch and tell me*, and had said it as a colleague, and Harry had agreed, and the agreeing had been the first thing they had ever done together, and he was not going to break it in the second week because a thing had moved behind a door. So he did the next thing, which he had worked out on the train in August would be the hardest thing about having people in the room, and which he had not expected to need so soon.

“Hermione. There’s a thing I can’t tell you.”

She stopped on the stairs.

“I’m telling you that there is one. So that you know I’m not hiding it *from* you. I’m — it isn’t mine. Someone told me a thing as a colleague and asked me not to, and I said I wouldn’t, and I’m not going to, and I wanted you to know there was a shape where a thing should be, so that when I’m — when I go quiet, in a room, or look at someone too long, you don’t think it’s you.” He looked at her. “I’ve never had anyone in the room to not-tell before. I don’t know how it’s done. I’m doing it badly. I’m sorry.”

Hermione looked at him for a long moment on the stairs with *Defence* under her arm.

“Is it about Quirrell?”

He did not say anything, which was a thing she had learned, in two weeks, to read.

“Right,” said Hermione. “Right.” She started down the stairs again. “Then I shan’t ask. And Harry — that was done fine. I’d rather be told there’s a door than not told there’s a door. I grew up in a house where nobody told me there were doors and I kept walking into them.” A pause. “Thursday’s your day with him, isn’t it. The Headmaster.”

“Four o’clock.”

“Then tell him that if he’s going to have you not-tell me things, he’d better have good ones.” She said it to the stairs. “Because I catch. He should know that. If it matters, I’ll catch it anyway, eventually, and it would be better all round if it was on purpose.”

He put that on the shelf. It went in warm, and then it went in somewhere else, somewhere he did not have a name for yet, and he left it there.

---

Thursday at four was a very strong cup of tea and a hundred eyes and a man who had, Harry saw from the door, already decided to tell him.

“The fourth question,” said Dumbledore, before Harry had sat down. “Ask it. I said a fortnight and it has been a fortnight and I find I have been wanting you to ask since Tuesday, which is new, and uncomfortable, and I should like to get it over with.”

Harry sat in Flitwick’s chair. “What’s in Professor Quirrell’s face?”

“Voldemort.”

He said it plainly, the way he had said *wraith*, and the hundred eyes did the thing they had done a fortnight ago — went still, for a quarter-second, all at once — and the hat on its shelf, which had been about to say good morning, said nothing.

Harry sat with it. He had known. He had known since Tuesday at eleven past eight and had not let himself write the word, and now the word was in the room, in the voice of the only man alive the books said had ever frightened it, and he found that knowing it and hearing it were, as always, two different things, and that the second was colder.

“How,” he said.

“I do not know. That is the honest answer and you shall have it first. I know that a man who was not dead went somewhere in the autumn of 1981 and was *less* — less than a man, less than a ghost, a thing that could not hold itself up — and that such a thing, if it is to go on, must find something to hold it. A place. A creature. A mind.” Dumbledore’s hands were folded on the blotter and did not move. “Quirinus Quirrell went to Albania a year ago, on a sabbatical, to study the Dark Arts at their source, which was foolish, and I told him so, and he went. He came back in July with a stutter he had not had and a turban he had not worn and a smell of garlic he had not needed, and I looked at him across a staff table and saw a frightened man, and told myself that was all I saw.” A pause. “You looked at him for four seconds and saw two.”

“What does it want?”

“A thing that is in this castle, that was not in this castle until the first of September, and that I put here.” He unfolded his hands. “Dr Potter. On the thirty-first of July, while you were in Gringotts depositing one galleon and four sickles, a vault was broken into. It was empty. It had been emptied that morning, by a very large man on my instructions, and the thing that had been in it is now on the third floor of this school, behind a door, in a corridor that I have told the students is out of bounds on pain of a very painful death, and I have done that—” he met Harry’s eyes “—on purpose. So that the thing in Quirrell’s face would know where it was.”

The office was very quiet.

Harry had read *Hogwarts: A History*. He had read the law, and the preamble, and the dissenting pamphlets of 1692. He had read a thin book about a prison in the sea. He had not read anything that prepared him for a kind man with a hundred eyes telling him, with his hands unfolded, that he had put a thing in a school full of children so that a monster would come for it.

“You’ve made a trap,” he said. “Out of the school.”

“Yes.”

“With first-years in it.”

“Yes.”

“That’s—” He stopped. He had not been angry on Monday a fortnight ago with a letter on the blotter; he had had the anger ready for ten years and found he could not use it. He found now that he had a different anger, one he had not known he owned, and that it was *fresh*, and that it was not for himself at all. “Professor. There are two hundred and eighty children in this building. Eleven of them are in my dormitory. One of them melted a cauldron on Friday because a man asked him a question through a shut door, and you have put *that* on the third floor and told them not to go near it in a voice that every eleven-year-old alive hears as an invitation, and you have done it *on purpose*, and you are telling me this as a colleague, and I am eleven, and I have read the Statute and the preamble and I have never once read anything in any book on either side of the curtain that says a headmaster may do that.”

“No,” said Dumbledore. “You have not. Because no book says it, because no headmaster has, because it is not a thing that is *done*. I am doing it anyway.” He did not raise his voice. He did not need to; the hundred eyes had gone still and stayed still. “Shall I tell you why, or would you like to be angry first? You have earned the anger. I should not like to take it from you by explaining.”

Harry sat in Flitwick’s chair with his hands in his lap and the fresh anger in him like a thing he had just been handed and did not yet know the weight of, and he thought about Carrow, who had said *you put your weight where you’ve got weight*, and about a woman in tartan who had said *they will be wrong*, and about a sentence he had said to a cupboard door at five: *Let them deal with it. That is their job. Yours is to stop making it hard.*

“Explain,” he said. “I’ll be angry after. I’d like to be angry about the right thing.”

Dumbledore inclined his head, one inch.

“A wraith,” he said, “cannot be killed, because it is not alive. It cannot be caught, because it is not solid. It can be *driven out* — of a host, of a place — and when it is driven out it goes somewhere else, and finds something else to hold it, and the something else is, as often as not, worse than the last. I drove it out of a forest in 1983 and it went to a mountain. I drove it out of the mountain in 1986 and it went to Albania. I did not drive it out of Albania; it came back on its own, in a man’s head, because it had heard that a thing it wants was in a vault, and it is now in my school, where I can *see* it, for the first time in ten years, every morning at breakfast.” He paused. “If I confront it tonight — and I could; Quirinus is not a difficult man to confront — it leaves him. In an hour it is in a bird, or a snake, or a Hufflepuff, or a man in Hogsmeade, and I have lost it again, and I shall not find it for another five years, and when I do it will have had five years to think. So I have done the other thing. I have given it a reason to *stay*. To stay in one man, in one castle, under a hundred eyes and a staff of eleven who are not fools, and to want a thing badly enough that it will go to the one place in this school where I have arranged for it to be *pinned* — not driven out; pinned — and there, Dr Potter, and only there, I believe I can do something to it that has not been done to it before. I may be wrong. I have been wrong about a great deal. But I have thought about this since 1981, and it is the one plan I have ever had that puts the thing where I can reach it, and I have decided to be sure.”

“And the children.”

“Are in the one building in Britain where a wraith will not harm them, because it needs this building, and a wraith that wants a thing does not spend itself on first-years. It will go to the third floor. It will not go to the dormitories. I know the thing, Dr Potter; I have known it since it was eleven and sat in a chair not unlike yours; it has never once in its life been *wasteful*.” Something moved in his face. “That is the one virtue it has, and I am relying on it, and I am aware of what it says about me that I am.”

Harry sat with that for a long time, by any clock.

He found, when he came to the end of the long time, that the fresh anger was still there and had not gone anywhere, and that beside it, now, was a second thing, which was that the man was *right*. Not right to do it. Right that it was the only plan that put the thing where he could reach it. Harry had read the law and the history and the clerk’s six pages and he knew what the thing had done, in the careful prose of books that went quiet around it, and he had been trying since he was nine to read the cold, and here was a man who had spent ten years getting the cold to come and sit in one chair, and had done it, and it was in a room that smelled of garlic on the first floor.

“I don’t agree,” he said. “I want that on the record. I think a school is the wrong place and I think *sure* is the second kind of reason, the fear kind, wearing a better coat, and I think you know that, because you said *I have decided to be sure* and not *I am sure*, and I read the difference.” He let that land. “And I think you’re right that it’s the only plan that pins it. I’ve been trying to read one of these things since I was nine. You’ve got one to sit still. I understand what that’s worth. I don’t think it’s worth two hundred and eighty children and I don’t think I can stop you and I don’t think I should try, because you’ve been doing this since before my aunt was born and I’ve been doing it a fortnight.” He looked at the man across the desk. “So here is what I’m going to do instead, and I’d like you to agree to it, because I think it’s the thing a colleague would ask for.”

“Go on.”

“Three things. One: I’m not going near the third floor. Not to look, not to test, not for Hermione, not for anything. You have my word and I don’t give it often; I gave it to a woman on a pavement and to my aunt at a sink and that’s the list. Two: I watch, every day, the way I have been, and I tell you on Thursdays, and I tell you *more* — I tell you what the thing does when it looks at me, because it does look at me, and today in a classroom it looked for thirty of my seconds, and it knows me the way the small one on the end knows me, and it isn’t glad.” He stopped. He had decided on the stairs that he would say the next part and had nearly decided, on the stairs, not to, and the deciding had taken most of a day. “And three: Professor Flitwick knows. Not everything. Not the Stone — I don’t need to know what the thing is; you said *a thing* and I’ll leave it at *a thing* until you say otherwise. But Flitwick knows that I can read a face in that classroom that nobody else can, and he knows what the face is doing, week by week, because I tell him on Saturdays, so that there is one person in this castle with a chair ready who knows what I know and is not you. Because you’re sure. And I would like someone in the building who isn’t.”

Dumbledore looked at him for a long moment across the blotter with the hundred eyes gone still and the hat on its shelf not saying anything.

“Filius,” he said at last, “has read your two pages on Dementors four times and burned a letter from the Department of Mysteries on your account, and would, I think, keep a confidence from me if you asked him to, which is a thing I have never once in forty years been able to say about Filius, and I find I am not certain whether to be alarmed or relieved.” He unfolded his hands. “Yes. To all three. And Dr Potter — the thing you said. *I have decided to be sure.* You are right. It is the fear kind. I have had it on for ten years and I had not noticed it was a coat.” He paused. “Thursday. Tell me what it does when it looks at you. All of it. I shall want the thirty seconds.”

“You’ll have them,” said Harry, and did not say the other thing — *and something behind a door moved* — because that was item nine, and the hat had said not alone and not who you think, and the man across the desk with a hundred eyes and a trap on the third floor was, Harry had just decided, in that chair, with fresh anger in him and a kind man being right about the wrong thing, precisely who he thought.

---

Flitwick heard it on the Saturday, in the small warm office, with Hermione sent to fetch a book from the common room that did not need fetching, and he heard it in the chair, and when Harry had finished he did not say anything for a long time, and then he said:

“Albus has put *that* on the third floor.”

“Yes, sir. A thing. He didn’t say what and I didn’t ask.”

“I know what.” Flitwick’s small face had gone a colour Harry had not seen on it. “I helped him move it. He said it was for *safekeeping*. He asked me to — never mind what he asked me to. Dr Potter, that *man*—” He stopped. He took his short dark wand out and looked at it and put it away again, which Harry understood was what Flitwick did instead of swearing. “Two people. In Quirinus’s face. And it looks at you.”

“For thirty seconds, yesterday. Mine. It knows me.”

“Of course it knows you. It—” Flitwick stopped again, and Harry watched him arrive at the edge of a thing and decide, as the Headmaster had, not to hand a fear to a boy for Charms. “Saturday. You will tell me every Saturday what it did. I shall write it down. If it ever — if the face ever does a thing you have not seen it do before, you will not wait for Saturday. You will come to this office, at any hour, and if I am not in it you will go to Minerva, and if she is not in hers you will go to the Headmaster, and if he is not in his you will go to *Severus*, and I know what you are going to say about Severus and you will go anyway, because he was in that war and I was not and he knows what that face is from the inside in a way none of the rest of us do.” He looked at Harry. “Promise me.”

“I promise. Sir — Severus?”

“*Severus*,” said Flitwick. “Any hour. Now. Hermione will be back in two minutes and she has read your face for a fortnight and she will know something has been said, and you had better decide before she opens that door how much of a shape you are going to let her see, because that girl could find a door in a wall with her eyes shut, and I should know, I have been teaching her to for a fortnight and she is *catching*.” The beam came back, with an effort Harry could see. “Page thirty-one. The pointing part. Where were we.”

Hermione came back with the book that did not need fetching and looked at the two of them and said nothing, and sat down, and opened the Second Edition at the pointing part, and read the first mark aloud in a careful voice, and got it right, and Flitwick said “*Yes*,” with real pleasure, and Harry sat on the first-year stool and watched a girl who caught learn to read a language with one speaker, and thought about a face with two people in it, and a thing behind a door that had moved, and a man who had decided to be sure.

*Severus*, he thought. *Any hour.*

*Not who you think.*

He did not know yet. He put it on the shelf, next to a flinch and a photograph, and went back to the pointing part.

---

That night he wrote the fifteen sightings out fair, in the forty-seventh pad, and under them, in English, for the reader in fifty years, the thing he had decided in a chair with the fresh anger in him.

*He has made a trap out of a school. He is right that it is the only plan that pins the thing, and wrong that it is worth the building, and he knows both, and has decided to be sure, which he admits is a coat. I have told him I do not agree. I have told him I will not go near the third floor. I have told Flitwick. Those are the three things a colleague can do when he cannot stop the senior man and does not think he should try, and I have done them, and I am still angry, and I think the anger is right and I am going to keep it where I can see it.*

*The thing in the face looks at me. It knows me the way the instrument knows me. And the thing behind my door moved, once, when it looked, like a sleeper hearing its name. I have not told him that. I have not told Flitwick. The hat said not alone and not who I think, and I have just found out who I think, and it is the man with a hundred eyes who has decided to be sure, and I have decided — this is the decision; I am writing it so I cannot pretend — that he is exactly who the hat meant. Not him. Not yet.*

*Flitwick said Severus, any hour. I do not know what that means. I have put it on the shelf next to the others. The shelf under* her *is getting crowded.*

*Hermione read the first mark aloud today and got it right. Flitwick said yes. I have been the only speaker of a language for five years and as of this morning there are two and a half.*

*Fourteen days of a fortnight were spent on this. I would spend them again. But I came here for the blankets, and the cold, and the table, and I have a term, and a fortnight of it is gone on a face, and I am going to spend next week on a bronze disc with Flitwick and a girl who catches, because the man upstairs is sure and I am not and the only answer I have ever had to a thing I could not stop was to build something else beside it.*

He closed the pad. He did not sleep. He had not expected to. He lay by the window with the castle singing one long note under him, and somewhere on the first floor a man with a stutter was asleep in a room that smelled of garlic with a thing in his head that was not asleep at all, and somewhere behind a door in Harry Potter a small cold grain that had been still for four years was still again, and he felt it be still, and did not trust it, and kept the door shut, and waited for morning, which was a long time coming, and came.
