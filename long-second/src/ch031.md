# Chapter 31 — Interlude: The Staff Room

*The staff room, Hogwarts, the evening before Hallowe’en, 1991*

“It doesn’t pull left,” said Rolanda Hooch, to the fire, for the third time.

Nobody answered her, because everybody in the room had heard it twice already, and because it was the kind of sentence that did not want an answer so much as a witness. She was standing with her back to the hearth and a school broom in her hands — the oldest one, the one with half its twigs gone and a crack in the handle that she had been meaning to have mended since 1979 — and she was turning it over the way a woman turns over a thing she has owned for twelve years and has just discovered has a room in it she never knew about.

“Twelve years,” she said. “Twelve years it pulled left and I told every first-year who got it to lean right and think nothing of it. Filius borrowed it on a Saturday and gave it back on a Tuesday and it flies *straight*. Straight as a — I took it up myself. Round the pitch twice. Not a hair.” She turned it over again. “And I can’t find what he did. The lift’s the same. The steering’s the same. I’ve had a wand over every inch of it and the only thing I can find that’s different is *this*.” She held up the handle, where the crack was, and under the crack, cut into the wood so small that Pomona Sprout had to put down her tea and come over and put her nose nearly against it, there was a line of marks. Forty, perhaps. Tiny. Clean. Like nothing anyone in the room had ever seen.

“That’s not a rune,” said Sprout.

“No.”

“It’s not *anything*. It’s not Ogham, it’s not Futhark, it’s not the Egyptian — Rolanda, I did Ancient Runes to NEWT and that is not a script.”

“No,” said Hooch. “It isn’t. And the broom flies straight.” She put the handle down across her knees and looked at the room. “Filius. You were in the room. Don’t tell me *Charms club*. I have known you since 1961 and you have never once in thirty years had a Charms club that cut marks into my brooms.”

Filius Flitwick, in the chair that had been the right height for him since 1971, with a cup of tea he had not drunk, looked at the forty marks under the crack for a long moment with an expression that the room had been learning, over eight weeks, to recognise, and that Pomona Sprout had privately named *the Potter face*, because she had seen it on four of her colleagues and on herself, once, in a greenhouse, over a boy who had answered a question nobody had asked.

“It is a language,” said Flitwick. “With five speakers. One of whom can write it, and one of whom can cut it, and three of whom can read it slowly, and I am the slowest of the three, and I am sixty-two.” He put the tea down. “The cushioning charm on that broom was written about sixty years ago by somebody who, in Dr Potter’s phrase, was *shouting*. It is ragged. It has been arguing with the steering charm for sixty years, and the argument is a pull to the left. He read all three charms at once — Rolanda, there are *three*, layered; did you know there were three? I did not know there were three — and rewrote the third one clean, in his own notation, and cut it under the crack where it would not show, and gave it back, and said *don’t tell her which one, see if she can tell*.” He picked the tea up again. “You could not tell. Nor could I. Nor, I suspect, could the man who wrote the original, if he were alive, which he is not, because he was shouting.”

Hooch looked at the forty marks.

“He *rewrote* a cushioning charm,” she said slowly.

“He rewrote it *better*. In a week. On a Saturday. In a room that slopes. And then he said no to your Seeker’s berth because he could *see the Snitch*, and Davies came to me very nearly in tears, and I told Davies that in forty-one years I had never once seen a first-year refuse a thing he was the best in the school at because it would not be fair to Hufflepuff, and that Davies should go and find someone who wobbled, which is what Dr Potter told him, and which is, I find, exactly right.” He drank the tea at last. “That is what is in your broom, Rolanda. A boy who can see the Snitch and will not catch it. Written very small, where it would not show.”

The staff room was quiet.

Minerva McGonagall, at the long table by the window with the Hallowe’en seating plan in front of her and a quill she had not used in ten minutes, said, without looking up: “He turned a match into a needle and back in my classroom. Four times. And then told me, in front of twenty-five first-years, that I held two brackets at once when I did the pig and did not know it, and that it was the cleanest holding he had seen since *the roof*, and that he had decided not to mind being rude about that one thing.” She put the quill down. “I have been teaching for thirty-four years and I did not know I held two of anything. I have been to my own classroom twice since and done the pig and tried to *watch*, and I cannot, and he can, and on Tuesdays he tells me what he saw and I am as rude back as I please and it is—” She stopped. “I got out a photograph. From 1971. I had not got it out in twenty years. He looked at it for forty minutes and then told me what his mother had been doing when she turned her match back, and he was right, and I had been wrong about it since the day she did it.”

Nobody said anything.

“The Longbottom boy,” said Sprout, after a while, in the voice of someone adding a thing to a pile because it would not stay in her hand, “answered a question in my greenhouse last week that I had not asked. I was explaining why Devil’s Snare dislikes light and I had not got to the *why* and Neville Longbottom — *Neville*, Minerva, who melted a cauldron on his first day and has not said a word in my class in eight weeks — said *it’s the same as the bezoar, isn’t it, Professor, it’s a plant that does the opposite of what you’d think because of what’s underneath*, and I said yes, and he went a colour, and afterwards I asked him where that had come from and he said *Harry walked me round to it*.” She picked up her tea. “Walked him round. His words. I asked what it meant and he said *you find the door they already use*, as if quoting, and went red again, and I have been thinking about it for a week and I believe it is the single best description of teaching I have ever heard, and it came out of an eleven-year-old Gryffindor who was quoting an eleven-year-old Ravenclaw who got it, I am told, from a *cat*.”

“Mr Tibbles,” said Flitwick.

“I beg your—”

“Arabella Figg’s cat. He fixed its hips at six. Albus has the letter.” Flitwick looked at the fire. “The boy learned to teach from mending a cat, Pomona. He learned that you cannot make a thing come by wanting it, only by minding about it properly, and that the same is true of people, and he has been applying it to Longbottom and Granger and Boot and a *prefect* for eight weeks in a room that slopes, and the results are in your greenhouse and my classroom and Rolanda’s broom.” He put the cup down. “And Poppy’s ward. Though Poppy will not say so.”

Poppy Pomfrey, in the corner, with her arms folded, had said nothing for the whole of the conversation, and said nothing now for a moment longer, and then said, to her own folded arms, in the square voice:

“Cadwallader had a hairline in the radius.”

The room turned.

“An inch proximal. Non-displaced. I set the ulna and did not look at the radius because the ulna was the thing that showed. The boy stood twenty feet away and asked the arm a question with a wand and told me where it was and I put two fingers on it and the child said *ow*.” She unfolded her arms. “Thirty-one years. I have set ten thousand bones. I would have taken the sling off in three weeks and he would have leaned on it and it would have gone and I would have set it again and wondered. I did not see it. I said so. In front of the boy. In front of Cadwallader.” She looked at Flitwick. “You were there.”

“I was there.”

“Then you will confirm that I said *I did not see it* and did not qualify it.”

“You did not qualify it, Poppy. It was the bravest thing I have seen in this castle, and I told Dr Potter so, and he said he had seen a man admit a trap, and I did not ask him what he meant.”

Pomfrey looked at the fire for a while.

“Wednesdays,” she said. “Eight o’clock. He brings the girl to write it down and you to sit in a chair. He has cast that — *asking*, he calls it — on four patients since, with my hand on the bed and my word first, and it has been right four times, and twice it has been right about a thing I had not looked for.” A pause. “He gave me two names. For a drawer. From the Compendium. The fourth chapter. He found the table when he was eight in a *cupboard*, Minerva. I knew the second name. I was a trainee.” She did not say anything else about that, and nobody asked, because the square face had done a thing square faces were not built for and had done it in front of colleagues, and the room let her have it.

“He has not,” said Severus Snape, from the shadow by the door, where he had been standing for some minutes without anyone having quite seen him arrive, “mended anything.”

The room turned again.

He was leaning against the wall with his arms folded and the shut-door face on, and he had plainly been listening since Hooch’s second *it doesn’t pull left*, and he said it the way he said everything, soft and dry and with the nails in his pocket.

“Four patients. A broom. A cup, I am told. He *asks*. He does not *do*. He finds the hairline and hands it to Poppy. He finds the ragged charm and cuts a clean one under a crack and gives it back to Rolanda and does not say which. He walks Longbottom round to a thing and lets Longbottom think he found it.” The dark eyes went round the room. “Have any of you noticed that the boy with a doctorate and a wand that lit steady in Ollivander’s has not, in eight weeks, done one single thing in this castle *himself* that he could have had someone else do?”

Nobody answered.

“I have,” said Snape. “I have been watching for it. I expected—” He stopped, and the door shut a fraction harder, and when it opened again the voice had something in it that McGonagall had heard once, at lunch, in September, and had not been able to name. “I expected a boy who would *show*. The father showed. The father could not turn a chair leg into a snake without an audience. This one turns a match into a needle and back four times and then spends the rest of the lesson helping Boot hold his wand stiller and does not look up when the room gasps.” A pause. “He put a ring on a pewter cauldron on his first Friday by having the flame a quarter-turn high for a minute, and when I told him so he said *about a minute, sir, I didn’t know what a simmer looked like, I’ll know next time*, and he has known every time since, and he has never once been *more* than adequate in my dungeon, and I have been teaching for ten years and I believe he is doing it on purpose and I cannot find the seam.”

“He is,” said Flitwick quietly. “He told me. He said he would be adequate for a term so that you could find where he was bad at it and say so, because you found the ring and nobody else alive could have, and he wanted to be *found*, not handled.” He looked at Snape. “His word, Severus. *Found*.”

Snape did not say anything for a long moment.

“And the eyes,” said McGonagall, from the table, not looking up from the seating plan, in a voice that was perfectly level. “He has noticed that you look at the eyes, Severus. Not the scar. He told the Headmaster so on the second Thursday. He told him he was reading the right face and asked whether it was. Albus said yes and would not say what was behind it.” She put the quill down. “I am saying this in front of you because he is going to ask you himself, eventually, properly, through the front door, and I think you had rather hear from a colleague that it is coming than find out in a corridor. He does not push. He waits. He waited five years for his aunt and she came out. He has told Albus he has seven for you.”

The room had gone very still, in the way staff rooms go when a thing has been said that was not supposed to be said in them.

Snape looked at McGonagall across the room with the door shut all the way and the nails in his pocket, and she looked back, and did not look away, and after a moment he said, very quietly:

“Seven years.”

“His words.”

“He is *eleven*.”

“He is eleven and he has a doctorate and he has decided to be adequate in your dungeon so that you will find where he is bad, and he has noticed the eyes, and he is going to wait.” McGonagall picked the quill up again. “That is the whole of what I know, Severus, and I have said it, and I shall not say it again, and you may be as rude to me about it as you please on any day but tomorrow, because tomorrow is Hallowe’en and I have a seating plan.”

Snape did not say anything. But his hand went, once, to the pocket where McGonagall had decided years ago the nails were kept, and stayed there a moment, and came away empty, and he turned to the door.

“The b-b-boy.”

He stopped.

Quirinus Quirrell had come in. Nobody had seen him do it; he had a way, since July, of being in a room before the room knew, and he stood just inside the door in the turban with the smell of garlic coming off him and the stutter in his mouth and his hands shaking very slightly at his sides, and he was looking at the broom across Hooch’s knees, and then at Flitwick, and then — a quarter of a second; nobody in the room could have measured it but one person who was not in the room — at nothing, at the middle distance, with eyes that were not quite his.

“The P-Potter boy,” said Quirrell. “You’re all — everyone is t-talking about him. I only — I have him for Defence. Tuesdays and F-Fridays. He is — he is very—” The hands shook. “He *watches*. Doesn’t he. He sits at the b-back and he watches. I find it quite — one finds it quite—”

“He watches everyone, Quirinus,” said Flitwick, in a voice that had no bounce in it at all and a great deal of something else. “It is what he does. He watched Rolanda’s broom and found three charms in it. He watched Poppy’s patient and found a hairline. He watches *me*, on Saturdays, and tells me what my face did when I saw a girl read a mark.” He did not look at Snape. “It is nothing to do with you.”

“No. No, of c-course.” Quirrell’s eyes went to the broom again, and to the forty marks under the crack, and stayed there a second longer than a nervous man’s would have, and then came away. “Only — one does w-wonder what he *sees*.”

“Hallowe’en,” said McGonagall, loudly, to the seating plan. “Tomorrow. Pumpkins. Quirinus, you are at the end by Pomona, as you asked; you said the candles gave you a headache in the middle.”

“Yes. Yes, th-thank you, Minerva. The candles.” He put a hand to the turban, and the hand shook, and he went to the tea urn, and stood with his back to the room pouring a cup he did not drink, and the room talked about pumpkins with great determination for four minutes, and Snape stood by the door with his arms folded and did not go through it and did not take his eyes off the back of a turban, and when Quirrell left — abruptly, with the cup still full, murmuring about marking — the room let out a breath it had not known it was holding, and Sprout said, “That man gets *worse*,” and nobody answered her.

Snape waited until the door had been shut for a count of ten. Then he crossed the room, not to the fire, but to Flitwick’s chair, and bent, and said, very low, so that only Flitwick heard it and McGonagall, who was nearest, half did:

“He looks at the boy.”

“I know,” said Flitwick.

“Not *watches*. *Looks*. In the Hall. I have seen it. The—” the voice dropped further “—the thing under the stutter. It looks at Potter the way—” He stopped.

“I know, Severus. The boy knows.”

Snape went absolutely still.

“The boy *knows*.”

“The boy has been reading that face since the first night and has a tally of every time it has looked at him, with the times, and reports it to Albus on Thursdays and to me on Saturdays, and he told Albus on the second of September that there were two people holding it and the second one was cold, and Albus has told him what the second one is.” Flitwick looked up at the shut door bent over his chair. “He is eleven. He sits in that classroom twice a week and lets it look at him and counts the seconds. He has given Albus his word not to go near the third floor and he has kept it. And he has told me — Severus, he has told me that if the face ever does a thing it has not done, he is to come to me, and if I am not in my office to Minerva, and if she is not in hers to Albus, and if Albus is away—” He paused. “I told him to go to you. I told him you knew what that face was from the inside. He did not ask what I meant. He put it on a shelf. He has a great many shelves.”

Snape did not move for a long moment, bent over a small chair in a staff room with his hand flat on the arm of it.

“You told the boy,” he said, “to come to *me*.”

“Any hour. Yes.”

“*Why*.”

Flitwick looked at him. The beam was entirely gone, and what was under it was sixty-two years old and had thought about a thing for forty-one of them and had been shown the sea by a child two months ago, and was not afraid of a shut door.

“Because of the eyes, Severus,” he said. “Because you look at the eyes and not the scar. Because you said *the boy saw me see it* at lunch in a voice Minerva had never heard. Because he is going to wait seven years for you to open a door that you nailed from the inside, and I do not think you have seven years, and I do not think *he* does, not with that thing looking at him twice a week from the front of a classroom, and I would like there to be one person in this castle he will go to at any hour who knows what it is to be looked at by that — and have it *know* you — and have lived.” He picked up his tea, which was cold. “That is why. You may be as rude to me as you please. It is Hallowe’en tomorrow and I have a Charms club.”

Snape straightened.

He stood for a moment in the middle of the staff room with the door shut all the way and his hand at the pocket, and the room pretended, with great skill, to be discussing pumpkins, and then he turned, and went out, and did not slam anything, which McGonagall, who had known him eleven years, noted as the single most alarming thing he had done all evening.

“Well,” said Sprout, after a while. “*That* went—”

“It went,” said McGonagall, “exactly as it should. He will think about it. He thinks about everything for a very long time and then does it all at once. It is the only thing he and the boy have in common and I am not going to be the one to tell either of them.” She finished the seating plan. “Rolanda. The broom. May I?”

Hooch handed it over. McGonagall looked at the forty marks under the crack for a long moment, with the square spectacles catching the fire, and then — the room watched her do it; nobody said anything — put one finger on them, lightly, the way a woman touches a photograph she has not got out in twenty years.

“A language with five speakers,” she said. “One of whom can cut it into wood where it will not show, and give the thing back, and say *see if she can tell*.” She handed the broom back. “Albus has asked me whether I think the boy is happy. I have told him yes. I have not told him the other thing, because Albus did not ask it and I am not certain he is ready for the answer.”

“What other thing?” said Sprout.

“Whether *we* are,” said Minerva McGonagall, and picked up her seating plan, and went to bed.

*The sloping room, the same evening, later*

Harry wrote the staff room down without having been in it, which was a thing he could do, and did, in the forty-seventh pad, for a reader who would not have been in it either, by the light of a steady white point at the tip of a holly wand, turned down to a cigarette-glow so as not to wake Terry.

He had not been in it. But he had been in the Great Hall at dinner, and had watched eleven faces at a staff table for eleven minutes, and had come out with a tally that was not of Quirrell for once, and the tally said:

*Hooch has flown the broom. She knows it is straight. She does not know which charm. She has shown someone the marks under the crack; Sprout, I think; Sprout had the face of a woman who has looked at a thing very closely and not known it.*

*Flitwick has said something to Snape. Not at dinner; before. Snape came in late and did not look at Quirrell and then looked at nothing else for nine minutes, and looked at me once, for two seconds, and the look was not the flinch. It was the other one. The one from the pewter. He has been told something and he is deciding what to do with it and he does not decide quickly.*

*McGonagall has said something to Snape as well. She had the face she has on Tuesdays when she has been as rude as she pleases and is not sorry.*

*Pomfrey did not come to dinner. She has four patients and a drawer.*

*Quirrell sat at the end by Sprout, where the candles do not reach, and the second one looked at Snape twice and at me three times, for a total of forty seconds, and the door did not move. I am counting the door now as well as the looks. The door is the thing I care about. Thirty-one days since it moved. I do not trust the number.*

*Tomorrow is Hallowe'en. Ten years. There will be pumpkins. I have decided not to think about Godric's Hollow at a feast and I know already that I will fail and I have decided to let myself.*

He looked at that for a while, in the cigarette-glow. Then he added the last line, because the reader in fifty years would want it, and because it was the thing he had worked out at dinner watching eleven faces and had not expected to work out at all.

*They are talking about me in a room I am not in, and the talking has the shape of people who have been shown a thing and are deciding whether they are afraid of it. McGonagall said on a step that the house I went to would judge me first and be wrong. She did not say the staff would judge me second and be* right*, and I think they are, and I think the thing they are right about is the thing the hat said: that I will be the most interesting seven years of its existence, and that it has had Dumbledore. I did not come here to be interesting. I came for the blankets. But I think I had better accept that I am going to be interesting whether I like it or not, and spend the interest on the blankets, the way I spent the Advertiser on a stepladder.*

*Snape looked at me for two seconds and it was not the flinch. I do not know what it was. I have put it on the shelf under* her*, and the shelf is nearly full, and I think something on it is about to fall off, and I think I had better be in the room when it does.*

He turned the light out. It went out exactly, the way a lamp does, because he had told it to, and the room was dark, and the castle sang, and somewhere on the first floor a man with two people in his face was awake with the candles giving him a headache, and somewhere in a dungeon a man with a shut door was awake with his hand in his pocket, and Harry Potter lay in a bed by a window and did not sleep, and counted the door, and it did not move, and he did not trust it, and the night went on, and ended, and it was Hallowe’en.
