Harry Potter and the Speed of Thought

Chapter 1: Two Hundred to One

The last thing that happened to Harry Potter at the ordinary speed of the world was his mother carrying him up the stairs.

She had him under one arm, which she never did. Normally she carried him on her hip, facing out, so that he could see where they were going and point at things. Downstairs his father had shouted one word, very loud, and then there had been a flash of green under the door and nothing else. Now the stairs were going past much too fast, and his mother was breathing in a way he had never heard. She put him in the cot without turning on the light. She pushed the cot back against the wall with her hip and turned round. There was a man in the doorway, and the man was Voldemort.

Harry was fifteen months old and had been standing for three of them. He stood now, holding the rail, because the cot was the best place in the room to see from. He had never seen Voldemort before. He saw a tall shape with the landing light behind it, so that the face was dark, holding something thin.

What his mother said, Harry kept. He kept it the way he would later keep everything, completely, and for a long time it was the only thing he had from before, so that it sat in his memory like a single line of writing on an otherwise blank page. She said, “Not Harry, please, not Harry.” She said, “Take me instead.” Voldemort answered in a voice that sounded bored. She said it again. She was between him and the cot now. He said two words Harry did not know, there was another flash of green, much closer this time, and Lily Potter was on the floor.

Harry started to cry then, because Voldemort had turned round and was not his father.

Voldemort looked down at him. Harry had no name for the expression on his face, and would not have one until he was nearly three, when he found it in a dictionary under curiosity and decided the word was not big enough. Voldemort raised the wand and said the two words again, carefully, the way you repeat something to a child who did not hear it properly the first time.

The green came again. This time it was for him, and it had not finished arriving when the world changed speed.

He would spend a lot of his life trying to describe what happened next, mostly to himself, and never get it right. The nearest he came was this. The light touched his forehead and the pain started, and then it kept on starting. It did not get worse. It just never reached the point where pain stops arriving and settles in, and he was left there waiting for it, for what felt like a whole long afternoon. He had time to notice things. The green had gone out of the room. Voldemort had thrown his head back and was making a sound, and the sound came very slowly, a single note that climbed and climbed and did not stop. The wall behind him was coming away from the ceiling. It did this at the speed of something falling in a dream. A dark line opened between the plaster and the ceiling. Dust came out of it, and did not fall; it hung there in the landing light, every speck of it perfectly still. Voldemort’s outline went ragged. Harry would read a great many words later and not find a better one for it: he frayed, like cloth, from the edges inward, and the note he was making thinned along with him, until the thing in the doorway was a smear that was no longer a man and the sound was a thread. Then the thread pulled away, out through the hole where the wall had been, and was gone. The dust began, very slowly, to come down.

When it was quiet he found that he was crying, and listened to it.

That was the strangest part of the first night, stranger than Voldemort or the dust. He could hear his own crying begin. He could hear the breath go in and the sound set out across the room like something walking, and go on, and go on, until he got used to it and found he could think about other things while it carried on without him. He thought about his mother. She was on the floor beside the cot, on her side, with her hair over her face. She did not move. This did not surprise him, because nothing in the room was moving. The dust took a very long time to settle, and he watched it. He was cold, because the wall was gone and there was a piece of sky where it had been, with stars in it. The stars did not move either.

How long it was before Hagrid came, he could not have said. He had nothing to measure with, and would not have for a year.

The doorway filled. Hagrid was so big that for a moment Harry took him for the wall coming back. He was making a sound, low and continuous, like a hill moving, and it was a long time before Harry understood that the sound was words, and longer before he understood that the words were meant for him. The hands that came down to him were the size of his whole body. They took about a minute, by his reckoning, to close round him, and he had time while they did to look at the fingernails, black with something, the hair on the backs of the fingers, a white scar across one knuckle, and to decide that he was not afraid of the hands, because they were shaking. He was lifted and pressed into a coat that smelled of dog and woodsmoke and rain. The coat heaved. Hagrid’s voice broke into pieces, and Harry knew what that was, because he had been doing it himself.

There was a second man after that, and Harry knew him. It was Sirius Black, who came on Sundays and made the wooden dog bark by pointing at it, and whose name Harry knew as the sound the sea made in his picture book. Sirius came in through what was left of the door and stopped dead. Then he moved faster than anyone Harry had seen move that night, which was about as fast as a man wading. He had his hands in his hair. He said things to Hagrid, and Hagrid said things back. Harry understood none of the words and all of the shape of it, because he had wanted things himself and been told no: Sirius wanted Harry, and Hagrid would not give him up. It went on a long time. At the end of it Sirius put his face into Hagrid’s coat beside Harry’s, close enough that Harry could see one eye, very wet. Then he went away. When he came back he gave Hagrid his motorbike.

Harry slept after that, for what Hagrid would one day tell him was most of a day, and remembered none of it. He remembered waking in the air.

There was wind, and a drone under him that he could feel in his teeth. Hagrid’s arm lay across him like a fallen branch. When he turned his head under it he was looking down at what he took for the sea, except that it was lights: thousands of them, orange, in lines and curves and clusters, going on to the edge of the world, and not one of them moving. It was Bristol, though he would not know that for years. Above him the stars did not move either. He looked at them for what felt like a whole night and they stayed exactly where they were. Somewhere in that night he came to a conclusion about the world that he would not have words for until much later, and the words, when they came, made it less true rather than more. The world was a still place. Things happened in it only occasionally. The happening was the rare part, and the good part.

Hagrid talked the whole way. Harry could not follow a word. He listened to the voice rise and fall under the wind in long slow swells, and after a while he could tell when Hagrid was sad, which was most of the time, and when he was trying to be cheerful for Harry’s sake, which was the rest.

He was set down on something hard and cold, and half woke. He was wrapped in a blanket. There was stone under the blanket, and three voices above him.

One was Hagrid’s, still full of weather. One was a woman’s, clipped, each word cut cleanly off from the next; that was Professor McGonagall. The third was a man’s, old and unhurried, and it was the one the other two waited for; that was Dumbledore. Harry could not understand any of it. He was fifteen months old, on the second night of the strangest thing that had ever happened to anyone, and he was not equipped to. But he kept it, the way he had kept his mother’s words, because it was the only other thing that had happened, and over the next two years he would go through it so many times that the sounds wore grooves. There was a name McGonagall kept using, which was Dumbledore’s. There was a sound Dumbledore made, gently, which was Harry’s own name. There was a name McGonagall would not say and Dumbledore said anyway, in the tone of someone picking up something hot to show that it can be done, and that was Voldemort’s. And at the end, in Hagrid’s voice, cracked right across, there was a short run of sounds that Harry would finally work out at the age of two and a half, sitting in the dark under a staircase, and would turn over for a long time afterwards: the boy who lived.

A hand touched his forehead where it hurt. It was Dumbledore’s, warm and dry and very old. After it the cold went out of the stone, and he slept.

The number, when he was old enough to measure it against a kitchen clock, would turn out to be about two hundred. For every second the world took, he got two hundred of his own. That was all it was. He would have given a great deal, in the years that followed, to have had someone tell him that on the doorstep, in those words. Nobody could have. Nobody knew. He spent the next two years working it out from the inside, with nothing to compare it to, and two years at two hundred to one is a long time.

In the morning there was a scream.

He had been asleep, or doing whatever sleep was going to be from now on, and he came up out of it into a sound that started low and climbed and kept climbing, the way Voldemort’s had, so that for a moment he thought Voldemort was back. Then there was a face above him. It was Petunia Dursley’s, and it was the wrong one.

He was going to spend a great deal of the next two years looking at this face, and he looked at it now with his whole attention, because it was all there was. It was long and pale. The eyes were set close together and were a colour that had nothing to do with his mother’s, and the whole face was doing several things at once that he had never seen a face do together. Later, when he had words, he would sort them out: fear, mostly; under the fear a fury with nowhere to go; under that something older and sadder that Petunia would have denied to her last breath. At the time he saw only that the face was moving, which almost nothing did, and he watched it the way he had watched the dust.

Petunia had a piece of paper. She read it standing on the step with him at her feet, and he watched her read. It took her a long time by his reckoning, the whole of a long afternoon, with the cold coming up through the blanket. Her eyes went along the lines and back and along. He could see, because there was nothing else to look at, the exact moment each line reached her, because her face did something small each time. He did not know what the paper said. He did not know what paper was. But by the end of it he knew that Petunia had decided something, and that she had hated deciding it. When she picked him up it was the way you pick up something that has to be carried and might be dirty.

Inside the house there was a man who was wide rather than tall, with a moustache. This was Vernon Dursley, and he said a great many things in a voice that was the loudest thing Harry had heard since Hagrid, and he said them for a long time. There was another child, Dudley, who was Harry’s size and a good deal rounder. He sat in a high chair with a spoon and looked at Harry with the frank, enormous interest that only a very small child can bring to another one. Then, having finished his assessment, he hit Harry with the spoon.

Harry saw it coming. He saw the arm go back and the spoon come round in a long curve, and he had, by his own reckoning, several minutes to think about it. What he did not have was any way of getting out from under it. He told his head to move. His head, which had always moved when he told it to, thought about the request, and the spoon arrived, and then his head moved. That was the first he knew of the second problem, which was that whatever had happened to his thinking had not happened to the rest of him. His hand, sent toward a cup, set off like a boat leaving harbour. He watched it go, unable to hurry it. When it got there it had forgotten what it was for, and knocked the cup over. He had been walking for three months. That morning, set down on the kitchen floor, he fell over, and he kept falling over for ten days.

“Backward,” Petunia said to Vernon, at the end of the first day. “Well. It’s no wonder, is it.”

The day was the longest thing that had ever happened to him, and he had no way of knowing that every day from now on would be the same length. The house was still. Petunia moved through it like a figure in a painting that has been asked to pose slightly differently every quarter of an hour. Vernon went out, and the front door took so long to close behind him that Harry watched the light in the hall change three times as it swung. Dudley was put in a playpen and stayed there, breathing, which Harry found he could watch: the chest going out, pausing, coming in, pausing, like the sea if the sea were very patient. In the whole long bright silent stretch of that first day there were exactly three things in the house that moved at a speed he found natural. There was a fly on the kitchen window, which crossed the glass in a graceful, unhurried curve, landed, and washed its face. He loved it. There was the second hand on the kitchen clock, which crept, and which he would come to know better than any other object in the world. And there was the tap, when Petunia turned it on. The water came down in a rope. Where the rope met the sink it broke apart, and the breaking was the most complicated and beautiful thing he had ever seen. She turned it off before he had finished looking.

He learned two things about Petunia that day. Her face had two settings. There was one for Dudley, which was soft and moved a great deal and made sounds that went up at the ends, and one for Harry, which was flat and still and made sounds that went down. And she was frightened, and whatever she was frightened of was in the room whenever he was.

He learned one thing about Vernon, and he learned it that night, through the wall. Vernon had been loud all evening. Now he was quiet. The quiet voice went on for a long time, low and level, with Petunia’s voice coming in under it in short pieces. Harry lay in the dark and listened to it, and knew, without any words at all, that Vernon’s quiet voice was the most dangerous thing in the house.

They had put him in a small room at the back, in a cot that smelled of Dudley, among things nobody wanted: boxes, a lamp with no shade, Vernon’s golf clubs, and, along one wall, the twelve red volumes of an encyclopaedia that had belonged to Petunia’s father, with gold letters on their spines. The letters were the last thing he saw before Petunia turned the light out and the first thing he saw in the morning. He would see them every day for the next fourteen months without knowing what they were, and they would turn out to matter.

The dark was long. The hour before sleep came was, by his own reckoning, about a week, and nothing happened in it at all.

He waited for his mother. He did not know that he was doing it, any more than he knew he was breathing. Every time the house made a sound he turned his head toward the door. The turning took a long time, and the door stayed a door. By the time a thing happened that he would later think of as the first thing he ever worked out, he had turned his head toward the door perhaps two hundred times, waited after each one, and waited, in total, longer than any fifteen-month-old ever had.

The door was Petunia. Every time it had opened that day it had been Petunia, or Vernon, or Dudley carried. It had not once been his mother, and the number of times it had not been her had got so large that it had stopped being a number and become a fact about the door. He did not have the word never. He had the fact, which is the larger part of the word. He turned his head away from the door, slowly, and looked at the red spines instead. He had stopped waiting, not because he had decided to but because the waiting had run out.

That left the sounds. All through the long bright day, in the still house, the sounds had been the only things that changed. Petunia’s voice to Dudley and Petunia’s voice to him. Vernon’s three volumes. The television in the front room that talked and sang and had people in it. Hagrid’s voice and McGonagall’s and Dumbledore’s, which he went through again now, lying in the dark, and found that he could, that they were still there. He did not decide anything. He was fifteen months old. But if anyone could have seen into the small back room that night and seen what the child in the cot was paying attention to, with the whole of a mind that had suddenly been given more time than it knew what to do with, they would have seen him listening. The house breathed. The boiler ticked. Downstairs Vernon’s quiet voice went on. Harry listened to all of it, sorted what he could, kept the rest against the day when he could, and at some point, without noticing, he slept.
