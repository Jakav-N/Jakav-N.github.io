Harry Potter and the Speed of Thought

Chapter 1: Two Hundred to One

The last thing to happen to Harry Potter at the ordinary speed of the world was his mother carrying him up the stairs.

She had him under one arm, which she never did. She carried him on her hip as a rule, facing outward, so that he could see where they were going and point at things. Downstairs his father had shouted a single word, very loud, and then there had been a flash of green under the door and nothing else, and now the stairs were going past much too fast and his mother’s breath was coming in a way he had never heard it come. She put him in the cot. She did not turn on the light. She pushed the cot against the wall with her hip and turned round, and there was a man in the doorway.

Harry was fifteen months old and had been standing for three of them, and he stood now, holding the rail, because the cot was the best place in the room to see from. He did not know the man. The man was tall, and the light from the landing seemed to go round him rather than onto him, and he was holding something thin.

What his mother said, Harry kept. He kept it the way he would later keep everything, which is to say completely, and for a long time it was the only thing he had kept from before, so that it lay in his memory like a single line of writing on a page otherwise blank. She said, “Not Harry, please, not Harry.” She said, “Take me instead.” The man said something in a voice that sounded bored. She said it again, and she was between the man and the cot now, and the man said two words that Harry did not know, and there was the green again, close, and his mother was on the floor.

He began to cry then, because the man had turned round, and was not his father.

The man looked down at him with an expression Harry had no name for, and would not have one for until he was nearly three and found it in a dictionary under curiosity and saw that the word was not big enough. The man raised the thin thing. He said the two words again, carefully, as though to a child who had not heard them properly the first time.

The green came, and this time it was for him, and it had not finished arriving when the world changed speed.

He would spend a great deal of his life trying to describe what happened next, mostly to himself, and never be satisfied with it. The nearest he came was this. The light touched his forehead and the pain began, and went on beginning. It did not get worse, but neither did it arrive at the point where pain stops starting and begins to be; it stayed there, poised, for what felt like the whole of a long afternoon. In that afternoon he had time to notice things. He noticed that the green had gone out of the room, and that the man in the doorway had thrown back his head and was making a sound, and that the sound was coming very slowly, a single note that climbed and climbed and showed no sign of stopping. He noticed that the wall behind the man was coming away from the ceiling. It did this at the speed of something falling in a dream: a line of dark opened between plaster and plaster and widened, and dust came out of it and did not fall but hung, each mote picked out by the landing light and perfectly still. The man’s shape frayed. There was no better word for it, and later Harry would read a great many words and not find one. It thinned at the edges, and the note it was making thinned with it, until the thing in the doorway was a smear of something that was no longer a man and the sound was a thread, and then the thread drew away, out through the hole where the wall had been, and was gone, and the dust began, very slowly, to come down.

When it was quiet he found that he was crying, and listened to it.

That was the strangest part of the first night, stranger than the man or the dust: that he could hear his own crying begin, hear the breath drawn in and the sound set out across the room like something walking, and go on hearing it, and go on, until he grew used to the sound and found he could think about other things while it continued. He thought about his mother. She was on the floor beside the cot, on her side, with her hair across her face, and she did not move, and this did not surprise him, because nothing in the room was moving. The dust took a very long time to settle. He watched it. He was cold, because the wall was gone, and he could see a piece of sky through the gap, and the sky had stars in it, and the stars did not move either.

How long it was before the giant came, he could not have said. He had nothing to measure with, and would not have for a year.

The doorway filled. The thing in it was so large that for a moment he took it for the wall coming back. It was making a sound, low and continuous, like a hill moving, and it was a long time before Harry understood that the sound was words, and longer before he understood that the words were for him. The hands that came down to him were the size of his whole body. They took, by his reckoning, about a minute to close round him, and he had time, as they did, to look at the fingernails, which were black with something, and the hair on the backs of the fingers, and a white scar across one knuckle, and to decide that he was not frightened of the hands, because they were shaking. He was lifted, and pressed into a coat that smelled of dog and woodsmoke and rain. The coat heaved; the hill-sound broke up into pieces; he knew what that was, because he had been doing it himself.

There was a second man after that, and Harry knew him. It was the one who came on Sundays and made the wooden dog bark by pointing at it, the one his mother called by a name that began with the sound the sea made in his picture book. He came in through what was left of the door and stopped dead, and then he moved faster than anyone Harry had seen move that night, which was to say about as fast as a man wading. He had his hands in his hair. He said things to the giant, and the giant said things back, and Harry, who understood none of the words, understood the shape of it perfectly, because he had wanted things himself and been told no: the known man wanted Harry, and the giant would not give him up. It went on for a long time. At the end of it the known man put his face into the giant’s coat beside Harry’s, so that for a moment Harry could see one of his eyes very close and very wet, and then he went away, and came back, and gave the giant something that gleamed and had two wheels.

He slept, after that, for what the giant would one day tell him was most of a day, and he did not remember any of it. He remembered waking in the air.

There was wind, and a drone under him that he felt in his teeth. The giant’s arm lay across him like a fallen branch, and when he turned his head under it he was looking down at what he took for the sea, and it was lights. There were thousands of them, orange, laid out in lines and curves and clusters, and they went on to the edge of the world, and none of them moved. Above, the stars did not move either. He looked at them for what felt like a whole night and they stayed exactly where they were, and he came to a conclusion about the world that he would not have words for until much later, and which the words, when they came, made less true rather than more: that it was a still place, in which things happened only occasionally, and that the happening was the rare and precious part.

The giant talked the whole way. Harry could not follow a word of it. He listened to the voice rise and fall under the wind, long slow swells of sound, and after a while found that he could tell when the giant was sad, which was most of the time, and when the giant was trying to be cheerful for him, which was the rest.

He was set down on something hard and cold, and half woke. He was in a blanket, and there was stone under the blanket, and three voices above him.

One was the giant’s, still full of weather. One was a woman’s, clipped, each word cut cleanly off from the next like something sliced. The third was a man’s, old and unhurried, and it was the one the other two waited for. He could not understand any of it. He was not, at fifteen months and on the second night of the strangest thing that had ever happened to anyone, equipped to understand it. But he kept it, as he had kept his mother’s words, because it was the only other thing that had happened, and over the following two years he would go through it so many times that the sounds wore grooves. There was a sound the clipped voice made, often, that would turn out to be the old man’s name. There was a sound the old voice made, gently, that would turn out to be Harry’s. There was a name that the clipped voice would not say and the old one said anyway, in the tone of a man picking up something hot to show that it can be done. And there was, at the end, in the giant’s voice, cracked right across, a short run of sounds that Harry would finally decode at the age of two and a half, sitting in the dark under a staircase, and would turn over for a long time afterwards: the boy who lived.

A hand touched his forehead where it hurt. The hand was warm and dry and very old, and after it the cold went out of the stone, and he slept.

The number, when he was old enough to measure it against a kitchen clock, would turn out to be about two hundred. For every second the world took, he had two hundred of his own. That was the whole of it, and he would have given a great deal, over the years that followed, to have been told it that night, in those words. Nobody could have. Nobody knew. He spent the first two years working it out from the inside, with no help and nothing to compare it to, and that is worth bearing in mind when considering how long those two years were.

In the morning there was a scream.

He had been dreaming, or doing the thing that was going to be dreaming from now on, and he came up out of it into a sound that started low and climbed and kept climbing, the way the man’s sound had, so that for a moment he thought the man had come back. Then there was a face above him. It was a woman’s, and it was the wrong one.

He was to spend a good deal of the next two years looking at this face, and he looked at it now with the whole of his attention, because it was all there was. It was long and pale and the eyes were set close together and were a colour that had nothing to do with his mother’s, and it was doing several things at once that he had never seen a face do together. Later, when he had the words, he would sort them: fear, mostly, and under the fear a fury that had nowhere to go, and under that something older and sadder that she would have denied to her last breath. At the time he saw only that the face was moving, which almost nothing did, and he watched it the way he had watched the dust.

She had a piece of paper. She read it standing on the step with him at her feet, and he watched her read. It took her a long time by his reckoning, the length of a long afternoon, with the November cold coming up through the blanket. Her eyes went along the lines and back and along, and he could see, because he had nothing else to look at, the exact moment at which each line reached her, because her face did something small each time. He did not know what the paper said. He did not know what paper was. But he knew by the end of it that the woman had decided something, and that she had hated deciding it, and when she picked him up it was the way one picks up a thing that has to be carried and may be dirty.

Inside the house there was a man who was wide rather than tall, with a moustache, and who said a great many things in a voice that was the loudest thing Harry had heard since the hill-sound, and said them for a long time. There was another child. He was Harry’s size and a good deal rounder, and he sat in a high chair with a spoon and regarded Harry with the frank, enormous interest that only a very small child can bring to another, and then, having completed his assessment, hit him with the spoon.

Harry saw it coming. He saw the arm go back and the spoon come round in a long curve, and he had, by his own reckoning, several minutes to consider it. What he did not have was any way of getting out from under it. He told his head to move. His head, which had always moved when he told it to, considered the request, and the spoon arrived, and then his head moved. That was the first he knew of the second problem, which was that whatever had happened to his thinking had not happened to the rest of him. His hand, sent toward a cup, set out like a boat leaving harbour, and he watched it go and could do nothing to hurry it, and when it got there it had forgotten what it was for and knocked the cup over. He had been walking for three months. That morning, set down on the kitchen floor, he fell over, and he went on falling over for ten days.

“Backward,” the woman said to the wide man, at the end of the first day. “Well. It’s no wonder, is it.”

The day itself was the longest thing that had ever happened to him, and he had no way to know that every day from now on would be the same length. The house was still. The woman moved through it like a figure in a painting that has been asked to pose in a slightly different position every quarter of an hour. The wide man went out, and the front door took so long to close behind him that Harry saw the light in the hall change three times as it swung. The round child was put down in a playpen and stayed there, breathing, which Harry found he could watch: the chest going out, pausing, coming in, pausing, like the sea if the sea were very patient. And there were, in all the long bright silent stretches of that first day, exactly three things in the house that moved at a speed he found natural. There was a fly on the kitchen window, which crossed the glass in a graceful, unhurried curve and landed and washed its face, and he loved it. There was the second hand on the kitchen clock, which crept, and which he would come to know better than any other object in the world. And there was the tap, when the woman turned it on: water coming down in a rope, and the rope going into the sink and breaking, and the breaking was the most complicated and beautiful thing he had ever seen, and she turned it off before he had finished looking.

He learned two things about the woman by the end of the day. The first was that her face had two settings. It had one for the round child, which was soft and moved a great deal and made sounds that went up at the ends, and it had one for Harry, which was flat and still and made sounds that went down. The second was that she was frightened, and that whatever she was frightened of was in the room whenever he was.

He learned one thing about the wide man, and he learned it that night, through the wall. The man had been loud all evening. Now he was quiet, and the quiet voice went on for a long time, low and level, with the woman’s voice coming in under it in short pieces, and Harry lay in the dark and listened and knew, with a certainty that owed nothing to words, that the quiet voice was the most dangerous thing in the house.

They had put him in a small room at the back, in a cot that smelled of the round child, among things nobody wanted: boxes, a lamp without a shade, a bag of long thin sticks with heavy heads, and, along one wall, a shelf of twelve red books with gold marks on their spines. The marks were the last thing he saw before the woman turned out the light and the first thing he saw in the morning, and he would see them every day for the next fourteen months without knowing what they were, and they would turn out to matter.

The dark was long. The hour before sleep came was, by his own reckoning, about a week, and nothing happened in it at all.

He waited for his mother. He did not know that he was doing it, any more than he knew that he was breathing. Every time the house made a sound he turned his head toward the door, and the turning took a long time, and the door stayed a door. He had turned his head toward it perhaps two hundred times, and waited after each one, and the waiting had added up to something no fifteen-month-old had ever waited through, when a thing happened that he would later think of as the first thing he ever worked out.

It was this. The door was the woman. Every time it had opened that day it had been the woman, or the wide man, or the round child carried. It had not once been his mother, and the number of times it had not been her had grown so large that it had stopped being a number and become a fact about the door. He did not have the word never, but he had the fact, which is the larger part of the word. He turned his head away from the door, slowly, and looked at the red spines instead, and he stopped waiting, not because he had decided to but because the waiting had run out.

There remained the sounds. In the still house, through the long bright day, the sounds had been the only things that changed. The woman’s voice to the child and the woman’s voice to him; the wide man’s three volumes; the box in the front room that talked and sang and had people in it; the hill-voice and the clipped voice and the old one, which he went through again now, lying in the dark, and found that he could, that they were still there. He did not decide anything. He was fifteen months old. But if anyone could have seen into the small back room that night, and seen what the child in the cot was attending to with the whole of a mind that had been given more time than it knew what to do with, they would have seen him listening. The house breathed. The boiler ticked. Downstairs the quiet voice went on. He listened to all of it, and sorted what he could, and kept what he could not sort against the day when he would be able to, and at some point, without noticing, he slept.
