Harry Potter and the Speed of Thought

Chapter 7: Scope

By Thursday the telephone had been taken off the hook so often that Vernon rang the exchange and had the number changed, and the new one was not in the book.

The street had not been like this before. There were vans at both ends of it with dishes on their roofs, and cars Harry did not know parked in front of houses whose owners stood in their doorways and looked at the cars. Men with cameras sat on the low wall outside number six eating sandwiches. Mrs Polkiss had been at her gate in her dressing gown on Wednesday and was at her gate in a frock on Thursday, and on Thursday afternoon she came across and rang the bell and asked Petunia, in a voice that carried, whether there was anything she could do, and Petunia, who had spent eighteen months making certain that Mrs Polkiss never once set foot inside the house, let her in and made her tea.

Harry watched this from the stairs and found it interesting. Petunia’s position in the street had been fixed, as far as he could tell, for as long as he had been in the house: she was the woman at number four with the clean windows and the fat little boy and the sister nobody mentioned. By Thursday she was the aunt of the boy who had been on the news. She hated it. She also could not quite bring herself to hate all of it, and he watched the two things go across her face as she poured, and thought that he had probably done her a kind of harm he had not reckoned on, and a kind of good she would never thank him for, and that he did not know yet which was larger.

The calls, before the number changed, had come from everywhere. The Mail again. Two other papers. A man from a company that made toys, who wanted to know if Harry would like to try something. A woman from a programme in New York, at half past three in the morning, who had not thought about the time difference and apologised for several minutes. Three publishers. Two universities, one of which was Cambridge. Vernon took all of them, standing in the hall with his free hand in his pocket, and Harry, from the cupboard, heard him get better at it as the week went on. By Thursday evening he was saying things like we’d have to consider that and I’ll need that in writing in a voice that Harry had never heard him use and that Vernon had evidently been waiting his whole life for an occasion to use.

The books had started arriving on Wednesday.

Mrs Ashworth’s box came first, with a note that said she hoped these were the kind of thing, and they were, every one of them, chosen by someone who had watched him read for a month. Dr Whitlock’s box came on Thursday, heavier, with the statistics textbook’s brothers and sisters in it and a note that said only You asked. On Friday a crate came from a bookshop in Oxford, with straw in it and a card from the manager, who had seen the news and thought the shop might as well be the first. And in among these came parcels from strangers, a dozen a day by the weekend, some with letters and some without: a Bible from a woman in Kent, a set of Dickens from a man who said he had no one to leave them to, children’s books from people who had not understood, a Latin grammar, a history of the Royal Navy, a book about bees.

The cupboard could not hold them. By Thursday night there were books in the hall, and Petunia was stepping over them.

He put it to them on Friday morning, at breakfast, before anyone from outside could put it first. He had thought about how to say it and had decided that the only honest way was the plain one.

“People are going to ask where I sleep,” he said. “The man from the Mail nearly did, on Monday. Somebody will. I’d rather, when they do, that the answer were a bedroom. I think you’d rather too.”

Vernon looked at Petunia. Petunia looked at the toast. Dudley, who had understood the word bedroom, began to say something and was told to eat.

“The box room’s got his things in it,” Petunia said.

“They could go in the garage,” said Harry. “There’s room. I measured it.”

He had. Nobody asked how.

It was done by Saturday, and it cost Dudley a day of screaming that Harry could hear from any room in the house and did not enjoy. He had not wanted to take anything from Dudley. He had wanted a room; there was one room; Dudley had been using it to keep a tricycle in. He told himself this several times during the afternoon, while Vernon carried the tricycle and the Big Trak and the plastic garage and the boxes of cars down the stairs and out through the kitchen. It was true. It did not make the screaming any more pleasant, and he noticed that it did not, and decided that it was right that it should not. A thing could be fair and still cost someone something. He had read that somewhere this week. He had read a great many things this week.

By Saturday evening he had a bed, which was Dudley’s old one, a chest of drawers, the window, the twelve red volumes back on their shelf where they had always been, and against every wall the books, in stacks, more than he could read in a month at any speed he could manage with his hands. He stood in the middle of the floor and turned round once, slowly, and had no words for it, in any of the languages he had so far.

On Friday a crew came from Japan and another from Germany, and on Saturday morning Breakfast Time came and set up its lights in the kitchen and filmed him eating cereal with a book propped against the milk jug. Frank Bough, whom Harry had watched every morning since January and who turned out to be shorter than the television made him, sat at the other end of the table and asked him questions and was charmed. Petunia made the tea. Her hands shook so that the cups rattled on the tray, and Harry saw Frank Bough see it and look away, kindly, and thought that there were worse people to have in your kitchen.

He read.

He had been reading twelve hours a day since Tuesday, in the cupboard and then in the hall and then in the room, with the door shut whenever he could shut it, and for the first time in his life he was choosing. That was the thing he kept coming back to. For a year he had read whatever was in reach, the Argos catalogue and the car’s handbook and the Bible, because they were there. Now there were two hundred books in his room and he could pick up any one of them. The picking turned out to be a skill, and he was not good at it yet. He read the first book in Mrs Ashworth’s box because it was on top. He read the second because it was next. By Wednesday night he had stopped doing that and started, instead, with a question, and letting the question choose.

The question was the one he had been carrying since the library: how big was the world, what was wrong with it, and how much.

Dr Whitlock had brought him the answer to the first part, because he had asked her for something true about the world and she had taken him at his word. It was a report from an organisation in Geneva, grey, with tables. The tables said how many children died each year before they were five, where, and of what. The number was a little under fifteen million. He read it, did the division, and read it again. Forty thousand a day. Seventeen hundred an hour. He had been in the room with the report for an hour. He sat for a while with that.

Then he did a thing that he would do many times afterwards and that he did for the first time that evening without quite knowing he was doing it: he tried to feel it, found that he could not, and looked at the fact that he could not.

He could feel one. He knew exactly what one was; he had one, on the floor beside a cot, with her hair across her face. He could feel two, perhaps, or ten. He could not feel seventeen hundred, and he could certainly not feel fifteen million, and when he tried, what he got was a kind of grey hum that was not grief and not anything, and that would have let him turn the page. He turned it over for a long time. The number was real. The report was the kind of document that did not get things wrong by more than a little. Each one of the fifteen million had been exactly as real as the one on the floor. So the feeling was the thing that was broken, not the number, and it was broken in a particular way: it stopped counting somewhere around a dozen and did not tell you it had stopped. If he was going to think about the world at all he would have to do it with the numbers and not the feeling, know that he was doing so, and not trust the hum.

He read the Brandt report, which said that the rich half of the world could fix a good deal of this and had chosen, on the whole, not to. He read a history of the twentieth century, which took him a day and a half and which he would not have described to anyone as a pleasant day and a half. There was a chapter in it that he read and then stopped reading. He sat with his hands flat on the closed book for a real ten minutes, which was most of a day and a half of his own, and then opened it and read on, because he had decided some time ago that he did not look away from things, and this was a thing. He had been through the night ten thousand times. This was the night, done to six million people, by men who went home afterwards and ate their dinners, and it had been arranged with forms.

He read the Mail, which was still coming, and read it differently now. There was a drought in Ethiopia that the paper gave four paragraphs to on page eleven. There was a bombing in Beirut. There was a man called Andropov who had replaced a man called Brezhnev and about whom nobody seemed to know anything, missiles coming to a place called Greenham Common that people were sitting down in front of, an illness in America that the paper wrote about in a tone he did not like, and three million people in Britain who had no work. He read all of it with the grey report open beside it, and began, without meaning to, to put numbers against things: how many people, how badly, for how long, whether anyone was doing anything, and whether the thing being done was the thing that would work.

On the Saturday night he read the book from the library, the one with the philosophers in it, because Mrs Ashworth had put it in the box with a note that said you’ll want to argue with this, and she was right.

Most of it he read as he read anything, fast and arguing. Then he came to a short piece by an Australian, written ten years before, about a child drowning in a pond. The argument was simple. If you were walking past a shallow pond and a child was drowning in it, you would wade in and pull the child out, and you would do it even though it ruined your shoes, and anyone who did not would be a monster. The child in the pond was no different from a child dying somewhere you could not see, except in the seeing; the shoes were no different from the money you spent on things you did not need; and so you were, most of you, most of the time, standing on the bank. That was all it said. He read it in eight looks and then he read it again in his head, slowly, the way he had once had to read everything, looking for the place where it was wrong.

He could not find it. He found a dozen places where he wanted it to be wrong. He noticed that he wanted it, and made himself look harder at each of those places than at the others, because wanting a thing to be false was not evidence that it was. The replies in the book were by professors, and they were long. When he had finished them he thought that none of the professors had found the place either, and that two of them knew it.

He did not sleep much that night, in the sense that mattered. He lay in Dudley’s old bed with the stacks of books around him in the dark and thought, and the night was long, the way all nights were, and for once he was glad of it.

By Monday he had read everything in the room that bore on the question, and he had an answer, or the beginning of one, and he took it apart that night the way he took everything apart, to see whether it would stand.

It was not complicated. He had expected it to be complicated, and it was not.

What mattered to him was that things went well for people. Not for him particularly; for people, the fifteen million and the three million and the one on the floor. He had looked for something that mattered more and had not found it, and he had looked for a reason why it should matter less and had not found that either, and he had been looking, one way and another, since the doorstep. That was the first thing.

The second thing was that almost nothing in the world was arranged to make it happen. The grey report was full of things that could be done and were not. The Brandt report said so in so many words. The century he had read on Thursday was a long account of people arranging things for other reasons entirely; the reasons were mostly small, and the results were mostly not. Nobody was in charge of this. There was no one whose job it was. That had taken him longer to accept than the first thing, because it seemed to him that there ought to be someone, and there was not.

The third thing was that he had something nobody else had. He did not know what it was or where it had come from. He knew that he had two hundred seconds for every one the world had; that he forgot nothing; that he had learned to read from a television in a fortnight and had read two hundred books in a week with his own small hands turning the pages; and that he was two years and ten months old. He did not know what that was worth. He thought it was probably worth a great deal in the right places; that he had been given it for no reason; and that the only sensible thing to do with a thing you had been given for no reason was to use it for the thing that mattered.

That was the whole of it. He did not call it anything. He did not promise anything to anyone, since there was nobody to promise to, and he distrusted the kind of feeling that went with promises; he had read enough history that week to know where that feeling led. It was a conclusion. He had reached it the way he reached conclusions, and he would hold it the way he held any conclusion, which was until something better came along, and he did not expect anything better to come along.

Then, because he had just read a century’s worth of what happened to people who decided they knew what mattered and had been given the means to pursue it, he spent the rest of the night on rules.

He would not lie. He had decided this already, somewhere between the library and the letterbox, and had found in the fortnight since that it made things simpler rather than harder, but he looked at it again now and saw more in it than convenience. Everyone in the century who had gone wrong had started by lying, to others and then to themselves, and the second kind was the one that did the damage, and the surest way he could think of never to lie to himself was never to lie at all. There was a corner of this he had not looked at before. He had been talking at Dudley’s level in front of adults for fifteen months. He had never said a false word doing it, but he had done it in order to be believed to be something he was not, and he could not now see the difference between that and a lie, except in the mechanism. He saw also that it had stopped on the Saturday at the library, without his deciding, and that it was not coming back.

He would not steal. He would not harm people. He would keep the law when the law was good, and when it was not he would count the cost of breaking it honestly, including the cost to everyone else’s reasons for keeping it. These were not hard. They were the rules everyone was told and most people mostly kept. The point of them was not that they were clever. The point was that they were cheap, and that he could see, from the century, exactly what they insured against, and that the premium was nothing next to the claim.

He lay in the dark and went through the rules once more, and the conclusion once more. Then, since it was still early by his clock, he went through the day.

Interlude: The Great Hall

The post came in at breakfast on Saturday, as it did every day, in a storm of wings under the enchanted ceiling, and the Daily Prophet landed by Albus Dumbledore’s plate between the marmalade and his left hand, folded, with the front page uppermost.

He looked at it for a long time without unfolding it.

The headline was in the Prophet’s largest type, which it reserved for deaths and Quidditch. THE BOY WHO LIVED, AMONG MUGGLES. Beneath it, in the type reserved for everything else: Potter, 2, called “genius” by Muggle scholars. Raised by Muggle relatives in Surrey. Ministry “monitoring.” There was a photograph. It had been copied from a muggle newspaper and the Prophet’s charms had set it moving, so that a very small boy on a step in a garden looked up from a book, down again, and up again, with a patience that had nothing of a child in it. The boy had his father’s hair and his mother’s eyes.

Dumbledore unfolded the paper and read it. He read it twice, which those who knew him would have found more alarming than anything in it, because Albus Dumbledore did not read things twice.

Rita Skeeter had had the name on Thursday, from the Daily Mail, the photograph on Friday, and the front page on Saturday, and she had done her work thoroughly. The muggle papers were quoted at length. A librarian. A retired engineer. A psychologist from something called a county council, who had declined to comment on the boy’s scores and whose declining had been quoted anyway. The boy himself, through a letterbox. The boy himself, on a sofa, on something called the BBC, which the Prophet explained in a footnote was a kind of wireless with pictures: it’s wrong about one thing in chapter two, but he puts it right in chapter five. The address was in the fourth paragraph, because it had been in the Mail.

On Tuesday morning Arabella Figg’s owl had been one of eleven at his plate. It had said that the boy was in the local paper, something about reading, and that she had thought he ought to know. He had read it, and thought good; he is clever; Lily’s son, and gone to the Wizengamot. He thought about that now.

Minerva McGonagall had been reading over his shoulder, which she did not do. “Albus,” she said.

“Yes.”

“He learned to read from the television.”

“So it appears.”

“At two.”

“At two years and eight months. The article is specific. Miss Skeeter, whatever her faults, can copy a date.”

“Albus.”

“Yes,” Dumbledore said. “I am going.”

Down the table Filius Flitwick asked for the paper to be passed, and it went along the staff. The Great Hall, which had been doing what it did at breakfast on the last Saturday of term, began to go quiet from the top table outward as the students saw the staff see it. Pomona Sprout read it with her hand over her mouth. Severus Snape read it in silence, as he read everything, and then looked at the photograph for rather longer than he had looked at the text, and then put the paper down and rose and left the table by the side door without a word. Dumbledore watched him go and said nothing, and went on saying nothing for some time after the door had closed.

At the far end of the table a voice much too large for the room said, “Does it mean I can go an’ see him, Professor?” It was Hagrid, who had the paper now and was weeping into his beard with the open happiness of a man who has waited two years for a piece of news and been given it. The whole Hall heard him, and the whole Hall, for a moment, looked at the headmaster.

“Soon, Hagrid,” Dumbledore said. “Not yet. But soon.”

He folded the paper, put it in his robes, and finished his tea. There was no sense in letting good tea go cold, and he was thinking, and over a century he had found that he thought best with a cup in his hand. Then he rose, and the Hall rose with him out of habit. He went out by the main doors, and McGonagall, who knew him, saw that he had not touched the marmalade.

On Tuesday morning Harry was at his window, because there was a Landau volume on the sill and the light was better there, and he happened to look down into the street.

It was the longest day of the year, and the street was full of the kind of light that comes at midsummer in England, flat and gold and going on forever. The vans had mostly gone. A man was walking up Privet Drive from the Wisteria Walk end.

He was tall, and old, with a long white beard and a long white face, and he was wearing a suit. Harry had seen a great many suits in the past week, on reporters and publishers and the man from the toy company. He knew what a suit looked like in 1983, and this was not it. It was a suit from some other time, dark and narrow, worn perfectly correctly by someone who had evidently learned how suits were worn a long time ago and seen no reason to learn again. He walked without hurrying. He walked past the one van that remained, and the two photographers on the wall at number six, who looked at him and looked away, which was odd, because the photographers had looked at everything for a week; and he came to the gate of number four and stopped, and looked at the house.

Harry had seen that look before. He had seen it every day for twenty months. It was the look the Dursleys gave him, the look of someone regarding a thing that was in their keeping and was dangerous and could not be put down. He had never seen it on anyone else, and he had never seen it directed at a house.

He stood very still at the window, with Landau forgotten in his hand, and felt something arrive in him that he would later be able to name as the jolt of a probability going from very small to nearly one in a single step. He had not heard the man speak. He did not need to. He had been carrying three voices on a doorstep for twenty months, and he had been carrying the knowledge, since the bottle, that there was something in the world that the Dursleys were afraid of and that had to do with him, and he had told himself for a week that the attention he had asked for might reach it. Here it was, in a suit from the wrong decade, opening the gate.

He put the book down and went to the top of the stairs. Below him, in the hall, Petunia opened the front door, and he heard her breath go in, and heard the old man say, in a voice he had last heard say his name on a cold step with a hand on his forehead:

“Mrs Dursley. We have corresponded.”
