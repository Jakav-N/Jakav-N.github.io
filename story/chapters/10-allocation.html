Harry Potter and the Speed of Thought

Chapter 10: Allocation

By the second week of August the days had a shape, and the shape was his.

He woke at half past six without being woken, which was a thing his body had decided on its own. He came down to a kitchen in which Vernon had already left and Petunia was at the sink, and he ate cereal with Breakfast Time on, because it was still the fastest way to find out what had happened in the world overnight, and because he had been watching it since January and had not yet broken the habit. At seven he went upstairs and read.

He read until twelve. He had a system now, which he had arrived at in July after a fortnight of reading whatever was on top of the nearest stack: the subjects took turns, so that no two days running went to the same one, and each had a week of his own time to settle before he came back to it. Monday was physics and chemistry. Tuesday was mathematics, which had turned out to be the subject in which thinking fast was nearly the whole of the game, and which he therefore rationed, because he could have done nothing else. Wednesday was the body and its diseases. Thursday was money and how people arranged themselves. Friday was history, and the week’s Economist, which came on Fridays and which he read cover to cover in forty minutes and then thought about for most of a subjective month. Saturday was philosophy and the books that did not fit anywhere. Sunday was the eight books from the trunk, the two green-inked ones, and the table.

At twelve he stopped, whatever he was in the middle of, and went into the garden for an hour.

That had taken some working out. He had not wanted to. There were books in the room and an hour was two hundred hours and he could feel every one of them going. But he had read enough about the body that summer to know that his did not care in the least how fast he thought, and that a child who sat on a bed for twelve hours a day would grow up with the bones and the lungs of a child who sat on a bed, and that the wanting was, in this case, exactly what it had been with the bottle: a feeling, to be noticed and set beside the facts. The trust had bought a climbing frame. He climbed it. He ran round the lawn until he was out of breath, which took longer each week. He ate what Petunia put in front of him and asked for more, and she gave it to him, since June, without comment. He was thin, and he had been thin for twenty months because food had been a thing he was given, and he had decided in July that it was going to be a thing he got.

At one on Tuesdays and Fridays Rachel came.

She had arrived on the eighth, in a Renault with a dent in the wing, in answer to an advertisement Pell had put in the university’s newsletter. She was twenty-four, in the second year of a doctorate on something to do with the surfaces of metals, and she needed the money and had said so in the first minute, which Harry had liked. Pell had interviewed her in the living room and Harry had sat on the stairs and listened and had sent down, at the end, via Dudley, a note that said yes.

The job was to drive him to the university library in Guildford on Tuesdays and Fridays, where a letter from the man in Cambridge had got him a reader’s ticket that the librarian had issued with the expression of someone who did not believe what she was typing; to fetch whatever the branch library had got in for him on inter-library loan, which by August was a crate a week; and to type what he dictated, because Pell’s typist in Guildford had other things to do and had begun to ask questions.

The first thing Rachel had said to him, on the eighth, standing in his room looking at the stacks, was: “Right. What do you want to read?”

He had given her the list. It was eleven typed pages, four hundred titles, in order of priority within subject, and he had dictated it over two evenings from what the Economist’s reviews and the bibliographies of the books he already had told him existed.

She read the first page. She read the second. She looked at him over the top of the third and said, “The library’s not going to have half of these.”

“The half will do,” Harry said. “For now.”

She looked at him for a moment longer, and then she laughed, not the laugh people laughed on television but a short one, the laugh of someone who has just been given a problem she can see the shape of, and said, “Fair enough,” and folded the list into her bag, and that was the whole of it. He had been talked to by a great many adults since June. Dr Whitlock had talked to him carefully, as a thing to be found out about. The man from Cambridge had talked to him as a result. Mrs Ashworth talked to him kindly and Pell talked to him as a client and Dumbledore talked to him as a problem he had not yet solved. Rachel, from the first afternoon, talked to him the way she presumably talked to the other people in her laboratory, which was to say briskly, about the work, with the assumption that he knew what he was asking for and would say if he did not. By the end of the first week he had noticed how much easier everything was when one person did that, and had begun to wonder what it would be like if several did.

The garden had another use, which he kept for the end of the hour, when he was tired enough to be still.

He had moved the practice out of his room because the room had a window onto the street, and a man with a camera had stood on the pavement for most of July. The garden had a fence, Petunia’s borders, and a corner behind the shed where he could sit with his back to the house and not be seen from any window. It was there that he did his half-hour, with a feather, and then a pencil, and then, in the second week of August, a book.

The feather was one in two by the middle of the month. He had stopped being surprised by it. The pencil, which was heavier and which he could roll along the paving stone from one crack to the next about one time in six, taught him that weight mattered, or that his picture of weight mattered, which was not the same thing and which he put in the table as a question. The book taught him more than either.

He had brought it out because of an idea that had been sitting in the back of his mind since the glasses. A page took him five seconds to look at and a second and a half to turn. The turning was now a third of the whole, and his hands were not going to get faster. So he set the book on the paving stone, open, and made the picture: the top right corner of the page lifting, the page coming over, the page lying down. It took him most of the half-hour to get the first one. By the end of the week he had it one time in four, and he sat behind the shed with the book in front of him and watched a page turn over by itself, slowly, like a leaf deciding, and saw the whole of what it meant and the whole of what was wrong with it in the same second.

What it meant was that the hands were not the limit. If he could turn a page one time in four by looking at it, he could learn to turn it every time, and then faster, and then he would be reading at the speed of his eyes and nothing else. What was wrong with it was his eyes. The eyes were five or six looks a second, and that was the retina and the muscles and not anything he could think his way round, and he had known for months that the eyes were the wall behind the hands, and had let himself not think about it because the hands were the wall in front. He thought about it now. He did not have an answer. He put it in the table, in the fifth column, under the thing about speed, and underlined it twice.

The thing about speed held. He tested it every day, because it was the strangest thing in the table and he did not trust strange things until they had been tested every day. A picture made at anything like his own pace did nothing, however exact. A picture made at the pace of a hand did what the picture said. He varied everything else he could vary, the size of the thing and the distance and the light and whether his eyes were open, and none of it changed the rule. After a fortnight he was as sure of it as he was of anything: whatever was on the other side of the picture took the picture in at a fixed rate, and the rate was not his.

The table had forty typed pages by the end of the month. Rachel typed it on Tuesday and Friday evenings from the cassettes, and on the second Friday she came up the stairs with the pages in her hand and said, “What is this?”

“A table.”

“I can see it’s a table. It’s sixty-one rows of Latin that isn’t quite Latin, with hand movements. Somebody called Goshawk is cited on every row and I can’t find a Goshawk in the catalogue. What’s it for?”

Harry had thought about this moment since July, because he had known it would come with anyone who typed for him. He had decided what he would do about it, and it was the thing he had decided about everything.

“I can’t tell you yet,” he said. “I’m sorry. It’s not that I don’t trust you. It’s a thing I’ve been asked not to talk about, and I think the person who asked has a reason, and I’d rather not say even that much, but you asked, so.”

She looked at the pages and then at him.

“That’s fine,” she said. “You’ll tell me when you can?”

“If I can.”

“Fair enough,” said Rachel. She put the pages on the desk and asked him about the second column instead, which was the one with the hand movements in it, and whether he wanted the diagrams redrawn, because hers were terrible.

He had noticed something in the table that week, on the Sunday, going over it, and it was in the fourth column. Of the sixty-one spells in the first book, four had inventors. He had the second-year book now, with seventy more, and nine of those had inventors, and every one of the thirteen named men and women had lived in the last three hundred years. The ones without names were the old ones. The lifting charm and the lighting charm and the unlocking charm had no maker and no date. Neither book said so, and he had begun to suspect that nobody had ever said so, because nobody had ever put the sixty-one in a column and looked down it. He did not know what it meant. He thought it probably meant something, and he put it at the top of the list for Saturday.

Dumbledore came on the last Saturday in August, in the afternoon, and Harry gave him the list.

It was sixty pages. Rachel had typed it. It had everything he had wanted to ask since June that he had not been able to ask anyone else, in order of how much the answer would change, and he had expected Dumbledore to take it away. Dumbledore sat down in Vernon’s chair and read it.

He read it in about ten minutes. Harry sat on the sofa and watched him do it, and it was the hardest ten minutes he had spent since the cupboard, because the old man read the way everybody read, down the page and over, at the speed of the world, and Harry had sixty pages in his head and knew what was on each of them and had to sit and watch a page that he could have taken in with twelve looks take a hundred and twenty seconds, which was six and a half hours of his, sixty times. He did not fidget. He had decided in the first minute that he would not, and he thought he managed it, and Dumbledore, turning the last page, looked up and said, “That was very hard for you,” and Harry said, “Yes,” and did not ask how he had known.

“I can answer perhaps one in ten of these today,” Dumbledore said. “The rest need books I do not have with me, and some of them need books that I am not sure exist. I will bring what I can.” He turned back through the pages. “This one. The speed.”

Harry told him about the feather, the pace of a hand, and the fortnight of testing. He told him about the page turning, what it meant, and what was wrong with it. He did not leave anything out, including the counts, and Dumbledore listened with the stillness Harry had come to recognise, which was the stillness of a man putting a thing he had just heard next to things he had known for a long time and watching them fail to agree.

“It is known,” he said, when Harry had finished. “In a general way. It is why the fastest duellists in the world cast perhaps three spells in a second and no one has ever cast four, and why that has been true for as long as there have been records. It has never been explained. It has barely been asked. I think you may be the first person in history for whom it is a practical problem rather than a curiosity.” He was quiet for a moment. “I had hoped to find you had not been practising.”

“I said I would. In June.”

“You did. I had hoped anyway. I am going to ask you to keep to three rules, and I think you will tell me they are already yours. Nothing on anything living. Nothing you cannot undo. Nothing where someone who does not know what you are could see it.”

“Those are my rules,” Harry said. “I’d add one. I don’t do it when I’m angry. I haven’t been, much, but I noticed in the cupboard that it worked better that way, and I didn’t like what that would do to me if I let it.”

Dumbledore looked at him for a while.

“Yes,” he said. “Add that one.” And he took out his wand, and went to the back door, and did something to the garden from the step that Harry could not see and that he said would make the corner behind the shed a place that the neighbours’ eyes would slide off, and came back, and sat down, and said, “Now. The rest.”

The rest took the afternoon. There was a law about children doing magic, an office that enforced it, and a way the office knew, which was a kind of mark laid on a child with the letter that invited them to the school. Harry had no letter and so no mark, and would not until he was eleven, and Dumbledore said this with the air of a man who had thought about whether to say it and had decided that not saying it would be a kind of lie. There was the post. Four hundred and some letters had come for Harry Potter since June, to the school, where Dumbledore had asked to have them sent, and where they had been read.

“I’d like a summary,” Harry said. “Once a month. Who’s written, and about what, roughly. And I’d like every letter from anyone who knew my parents. Not a summary. The letter.”

“Agreed,” said Dumbledore, after a moment, and Harry noticed that the moment had been for deciding and not for refusing, and was glad of it.

There was the money, which Dumbledore had an account of in his pocket, and which Harry read in two looks and put aside to think about on Thursday. There was Hagrid, who would come in September, and a woman called McGonagall, who had been on the doorstep and had asked to come, and Dumbledore said she would come in September too.

“And the friend who isn’t in a position to visit,” Harry said. “You said that in June. Who is he?”

“His name is Remus Lupin. He was at school with your parents, and was one of their closest friends, and he has reasons, which are his to tell you, for not being able to come to you yet. He would like to. I have asked him to wait.”

“Who were the others? My parents’ close friends. You said one of.”

Dumbledore did not answer at once.

“There were three,” he said. “Remus Lupin. A man called Peter Pettigrew, who is dead. And a man called Sirius Black, who is in prison, and will be for the rest of his life.”

“Why?”

“Because it was he who betrayed your parents to Voldemort, the night they died. He was the one person who knew where they were, and he told. And when Peter Pettigrew found him the next day and accused him, in a street full of people, he killed Pettigrew and twelve others with a single curse, and laughed, and was taken.”

Harry sat very still.

He had the night. He had been through it four thousand times and had it exactly, from the stairs to the stone, and he knew who had come through the broken doorway after the giant: a face he had known, the Sunday face, the one that made the wooden dog bark. It had been in pieces. It had wanted him, and been refused, and given the giant the motorbike, and gone. He turned it over now, with Dumbledore watching him, for a real second, which was long enough to go through it twice and to look, carefully, at each of the places where what he remembered met what he had just been told.

“A man came to the house that night,” he said. “After the big man. After Hagrid. I knew him. He came on Sundays and he made my wooden dog bark by pointing at it. He had his hands in his hair. He wanted to take me and Hagrid wouldn’t let him, and at the end he gave Hagrid the motorbike. Who was that?”

Dumbledore was silent for long enough that Harry heard the kitchen clock.

“That was Sirius Black,” he said.

“He was in pieces.”

“Grief and guilt look alike from the outside, Harry. Especially at night, especially to a child. I do not say that to dismiss what you saw. I say it because it is true.”

“I know it’s true,” Harry said. “I’m not saying it means anything. I’d like to read the record of his trial.”

“I will bring you what the Ministry has.”

Harry noticed the shape of that. It was not I will bring you the record. He had been listening to this man choose his words for two months, and he knew the sound of a sentence that had been built to be true, and he did not ask what the difference was, because he would be able to read it for himself when the pages came.

“Thank you,” he said. “I’d like to come back to this.”

“Yes,” said Dumbledore. “I rather thought you might.”

Interlude: A Newsagent’s

The town was in the north and it did not matter which. He had been in it since March, in a room over a shop, doing the kind of work that asked no questions and paid on Fridays, and he had been well enough that week to do it, which was not true of every week. It was three days after the full moon. He walked slowly.

The newsagent’s was on the corner. He went in for cigarettes, which he could not afford and bought anyway. The magazines were in a rack by the door, and one of them had a child on the cover.

He knew the hair first. Then he looked at the eyes, and stood in the doorway of a newsagent’s in the north of England with his hand still out for the cigarettes and could not, for a moment, do anything at all.

He bought it. He read it standing on the pavement outside, in the thin northern light, with people going round him, all of it, the subtitles and the library and the man called Hobday and the psychologist who had declined to comment, and the photographs, of which there were six, and in four of them the boy was reading and in two he was looking at the camera, and the two were the ones that Remus Lupin could not look at for long.

He took it home. He put it on the table in the room over the shop, next to the letter that had come in June, which he knew by heart and read again anyway. Not yet, Remus. When it is safe. He is well, and he is remarkable, and I will tell you more when I can. I know what I am asking. Albus always knew what he was asking. It was his gift.

In the drawer of the table there were eleven sheets of paper, each beginning Dear Harry, each stopped somewhere on the first side. He took them out and read them in order, which he had not done before. They were all the same letter, and the place where each one stopped was the place where he would have had to say what he was.

He took a twelfth sheet and wrote it through to the end, and did not stop at the place. Then he addressed it to Albus Dumbledore, at Hogwarts, with a note that said to be given to him when you judge it right, or kept, as you think best; I trust you in this as I have in everything, which is my failing and I know it, and he went out in the dusk to find an owl, which in a town like that took some time.

The Economist came on the last Friday of the month, with a Landau that Rachel had found in Guildford and said she had never seen anyone look so pleased about. He had them both on the kitchen table on the Saturday morning with his cereal. Breakfast Time was on. He looked up at it, reached over, and turned it off.

Petunia, at the sink, turned round at the silence.

He did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that would have made sense to her. The box in the front room had taught him to talk. It had given him the Falklands and Countdown and the subtitles and Russell Grant, and he had sat in front of it for twenty months because it was the only thing in the house that moved. It was still moving. It was simply that there was, for the first time, something better in the room, and he turned back to it.

That night he had too much day.

He had noticed it coming for a fortnight. The going-through, which had been the spine of every night since July of the year before, took as long as the day had been full, and the days were fuller now than they had ever been: twelve hours of books, each page of which he had, the library, Rachel, the garden, Dudley’s twenty minutes, the table. He had begun, without deciding to, to go through some of it quickly, the cereal and the stairs and the drive, and he had noticed that he was doing it. He lay now in the dark with the night half gone by the house’s clock and the day not yet finished by his, and thought about what he was losing, and whether it mattered, and how he would know.

He found that he was almost pleased. It was the first limit he had come up against since the night that was not a door or a bolt or a person. It was arithmetic. He had two hundred seconds for every one of the world’s, and that was not, after all, an infinite number, and he had finally filled it. He did not know what to do about it yet. He thought that was going to be the shape of a great many things from now on, and that it was a better shape than the one he had started with, and he went on going through the day, slowly, as if he had all night, and did not.
