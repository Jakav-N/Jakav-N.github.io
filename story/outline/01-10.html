Harry Potter and the Speed of Thought

Outline: chapters 1 to 10

This block runs from the night of Saturday 31 October 1981 to the end of August 1983. Harry goes from fifteen months old to three years and one month.

By the end of it he has a bedroom full of books, a trust with a few thousand pounds in it, a postgraduate who drives him to a university library twice a week, a cassette recorder, a mentor he half trusts, a secret that two people know, a daily hour of wandless practice that has just run into a ceiling he cannot explain, and a reading programme that would take a university a decade. He has stopped watching the television that taught him to talk. He has also met the first problem of his life that is not about other people: there is more day than there is night to go through.

The months were written first and stand on their own. The chapter map and the chapter notes at the end are carved from them afterwards, so that the months are never squeezed to fit chapter boundaries. The chapters take perhaps a fifth of what is here. The rest exists so that what is shown has weight behind it, and so that later chapters can refer back to things the reader never saw.

Month by month

November 1981. Fifteen to sixteen months.

Harry begins the month as an infant whose brain has just started running two hundred times faster than his body. He knows perhaps fifty words and can say five, all from before the night, and for the first week he cannot make his body do anything he intends.

The night itself is Saturday the 31st of October. Lily puts him to bed; James shouts downstairs and is cut off; Lily carries him up the stairs and says, at the cot, the words he will keep for life. Voldemort kills her and turns the wand on Harry. The pain begins and does not finish beginning. The room comes apart slowly: plaster drifting, dust hanging, Voldemort’s body going to pieces with a scream that thins into something that leaves. Then the first silence, and his own crying, which he hears start and goes on hearing until he gets used to it.

Later that night Hagrid fills the doorway, lifts him in hands that take a subjective minute to close, and cries into a coat that smells of dog and smoke and rain. Sirius Black arrives: Harry knows him as the man who comes on Sundays and makes the wooden dog bark. Sirius is frantic, which at Harry’s speed looks merely brisk. There is a long argument in which Sirius wants Harry and Hagrid will not give him up, and at the end of it Sirius hands over his motorbike. This memory is a seed. When Harry is told in August 1983 that Sirius Black betrayed his parents, he has a fact the official story does not account for: the man came to the ruin that night and was in pieces.

On Sunday the 1st of November Hagrid has him. Where is not shown. Harry sleeps most of the day, concussed and exhausted, and wakes in the air at night over Bristol: stars that do not move, a sea of orange light, the engine a steady drone, Hagrid talking the whole way in long slow sounds. He sleeps again.

Late that night he is set down on the Dursleys’ doorstep and half wakes. Three voices: Hagrid’s, still sobbing; McGonagall’s, clipped; Dumbledore’s, calm and unhurried, the one the other two wait for. He understands none of it but keeps all of it, and over the next two years he replays it so often that he can eventually pick out the names, Dumbledore’s and his own and Voldemort’s, and the phrase in Hagrid’s voice that ends “the boy who lived.” Dumbledore’s hand touches his forehead. The stone goes warm and he sleeps.

On Monday the 2nd Petunia opens the door and screams. Her face is the wrong face. He watches her read Dumbledore’s letter, which takes her a subjective hour, and sees her go through fury and fear and grief and settle on the expression she will mostly wear around him for the next twenty months. Inside there is Vernon, at full volume, and Dudley, who looks at him with enormous interest and then hits him with a spoon. Harry sees the spoon coming for several subjective minutes and cannot get his head out of the way. That night, through the wall, he hears Vernon’s quiet voice for the first time and knows it for the most dangerous sound in the house.

In the first week, from the 2nd to the 8th, his body will not do what he tells it. He has been walking since twelve months and now he cannot. His reaches miss. His babble comes out as drawn-out lowing that frightens Petunia a little, and she tells Vernon he makes a noise like a cow. Her verdict is that he is backward, and no wonder. He sorts the house’s noises within days: the boiler’s sequence, the fridge, the clock, the television’s two notes, the Hoover. He finds the three things that move at a pace he can follow, which are the fly on the kitchen window, the second hand on the clock, and the tap. He learns to put his attention somewhere other than the pain in his forehead, because the pain is continuous and he has no choice. He waits for his mother every time the door opens, and on the first night stops waiting, because the number of times the door has not been her has become a fact about the door. He discovers that nights are short and days are long.

In the second week, from the 9th to the 15th, he recovers crawling by working out the trick: decide, send the command, wait, watch the limb arrive. He gets his first word back, which is Dudley’s name, because it is said a hundred times a day and comes with a face. He gets no, which comes with a different face and is mostly for him. He learns the rising shape of a question and the pause after it. A Lincoln’s Inn solicitor’s envelope arrives with his birth certificate and NHS card and a covering letter that says nothing else, and Petunia files it in the bureau. Mrs Figg moves into Wisteria Walk two streets away, on Dumbledore’s instructions, and Harry does not know. Dudley hits him daily, and Harry begins watching Dudley’s arm.

In the third week, from the 16th to the 22nd, he is standing, cruising along furniture, and taking his first steps again, still falling. He has a dozen words receptively, and he notices that Petunia’s voice to Dudley contains good boy and that her voice to him contains nothing of the kind. Petunia takes him to be registered with the GP, which is the minimum she can do. A stranger’s hands, a cold instrument, and the first adult who looks at him properly, for about a minute. He has his first outing in the pushchair and sees the street: cars that cross his view over a subjective minute each, trees, a dog, the sky. It is too much input at once, and he learns that this is a thing that can happen.

In the fourth week, from the 23rd to the 30th, he crosses the kitchen on his feet, planning the route and carrying it out. He has about twenty words. He knows the television’s schedule by ear: the Play School tune means morning and the news theme means evening. The scar settles to an ache. It is the first week in which he is bored rather than overwhelmed, which is the start of a long project, finding things to think about.

Vernon will not say the boy’s name; it is “it” for a fortnight, then “him,” then “the boy.” Petunia cleans more than she used to. Dudley is told he is a good boy for hitting. Harry sleeps in Dudley’s old travel cot in the box room, between Vernon’s golf clubs and twelve red volumes with gold letters on their spines.

On the wizarding side, Dumbledore installs Mrs Figg and gives her her instructions: watch the boy, and do not be so kind to him that the Dursleys stop sending him round. Sirius is in Azkaban without a trial. Pettigrew’s finger has been buried. Crouch is prosecuting hard. The Longbottoms are attacked around now. Snape begins his first full year teaching. Dumbledore does not visit and has no plan to.

The muggle world reaches Harry as the Nine O’Clock News through Vernon, Crossroads and Coronation Street through Petunia, and Play School through Dudley. None of it is words yet. All of it is sound.

Chapter 1 takes the whole month.

December 1981. Sixteen to seventeen months.

In the first week, on Thursday the 3rd, the bottle slides. Dudley has a cold and Petunia is upstairs with him; Harry has not been fed; the bottle is made up on the kitchen table, four feet from the cot. He looks at it for a real half-minute, which is an hour and a half of his, with a picture of it in his hands so complete he can feel its weight. It slides along the cloth, tips off the edge, and lands in the cot. Petunia sees the slide from the doorway and screams. That night Vernon stands over the cot and the quiet voice goes on for a long time. Nothing is done, because there is nothing to be done, but the house is colder for a week. Harry does not understand what he did. He keeps the sequence: he wanted, he pictured, the bottle came, Petunia was afraid.

In the second week, from the 7th to the 13th, it snows heavily and stays bitterly cold; this is the real winter of 1981. Each flake takes a subjective minute to fall past the window and he watches for hours. It is the first beautiful thing he has had time for. His fine control is coming back, and he can hold a cup in both hands. He has about thirty words, and he has found that the children’s programmes name things slowly and repeat them.

In the third week, on Wednesday the 16th, Petunia takes him to Mrs Figg’s for the first time, because she wants to do her Christmas shopping and two pushchairs are too many. The house smells of cabbage. There are four cats, part-Kneazle, who look at him with an attention he recognises and does not get from people. Mrs Figg, who has been told not to be kind and has compromised by being boring, reads him the cat photograph albums caption by caption for an hour. He has never been talked to for an hour. It is the best day since the night. On Petunia’s shopping trip into town he sees shop signs, which are shapes. He has begun trying to make sounds himself and they come out slow and strange.

In the fourth week Marge arrives on the 23rd with Ripper, who is young and already unpleasant. At lunch she asks what “that one” is and Vernon says “Petunia’s sister’s. Car crash,” in a flat quick voice Harry has not heard before, and Petunia goes stiff beside him. He keeps the two words with the stiffness attached to them. Dudley’s presents fill the living room. Harry is given a rattle he watched Dudley drop behind the sofa in November, and a jumper Marge bought for Dudley that came out too small, which she says is a joke. He notices that the kitchen’s fluorescent tube breathes, a slow pulse once every two seconds by his count, and that the tree lights do not. He hears Vernon drunk and loud and then Vernon drunk and quiet, and learns that the quiet voice has a kind with no anger in it.

In the last days of the month he wants the light on in the dark box room with the whole of himself, and nothing happens. He has no framework in which this is informative, but he keeps it. On New Year’s Eve there are fireworks, heard and not seen, long low booms.

Petunia takes the bottle as the beginning of the end. Vernon says “the orphanage” out loud twice; Petunia says “we can’t” and does not say why. Marge recommends a firm hand.

Mrs Figg sends her first owl to Dumbledore: the boy is there, he is thin and quiet, and they do not like him. Dumbledore reads it and finds it as expected. The Lestranges and Crouch’s son come to trial around the turn of the year.

January 1982. Seventeen to eighteen months.

The first ten days are the coldest in living memory; Braemar touches minus twenty-seven on the 10th and there is snow across England. His room is the coldest in the house, and he learns that the boiler’s sequence of clicks predicts warmth half a subjective day ahead. He tries to climb and fails. He can now tell Petunia’s telephone voice, which is for Yvonne, from her Dudley voice and her Vernon voice. There are three Petunias.

On Thursday the 14th the health visitor does the eighteen-month check on both boys. Harry walks well now, and says Duh-ee and no and more. Nothing is noted. Petunia’s hand tightens on his shoulder when she is asked how he is settling in, and Harry notices that the health visitor looks at him properly, which almost nobody does. The same week Vernon’s company Granada arrives, and Harry learns the words Ford and Granada and the tone of gloating.

In the third week he works out that the television talks in the same system as the people. Petunia leaves BBC1 on in the mornings because Dudley likes the music, so Harry gets Play School and then the schools programmes. Words and Pictures sounds out words with a magic pencil, and Harry, at eighteen months, files the words as shapes. The lunchtime news follows. He learns dozens of words a day now, because the television repeats, speaks clearly, and talks about what it shows. He also wants Dudley to stop screaming, with the whole of himself, and Dudley eventually stops, as Dudley always does, and Harry registers, without words, that this proves nothing.

On Friday the 29th he climbs out of the travel cot. Petunia finds him on the landing at five in the morning looking at the stairs, and buys a bigger cot with higher sides. He will be out of that one by March. He has also noticed something about the night: the doorstep voices stay clear because he goes through them, and he goes through them every evening whether he wants to or not.

Vernon says they should put him somewhere. Petunia says there is nowhere. Her telephone calls with Yvonne become a regular source for Harry: gossip, the neighbours, the Polkisses, the woman at number seven, and Mrs Figg, who is “a bit odd, all those cats.”

Mrs Figg’s letters to Dumbledore say “fine.”

Chapter 2 takes this as summary.

February 1982. Eighteen to nineteen months.

In the first week Petunia moves him, for the daytimes, to a playpen in the front room, because she has begun to feel she needs to see him. From the playpen he can see the television. He has more than two hundred words receptively, and at night he has begun to practise making sounds in earnest, because he can hear exactly what goes wrong with each attempt and correct it.

On Thursday the 11th the television comes on. Dudley is asleep upstairs and Petunia is hoovering. The set is off. Harry wants it on with the whole of himself and pictures the picture coming back, and it comes on at full volume, in the middle of the lunchtime news. Petunia comes down the stairs faster than he has ever seen her move, looks at the switch on the set, at the stairs, at him, says nothing, turns it off, and carries the playpen into the kitchen and sets it facing the wall, where there is nothing to want. That night the quiet voice goes on longer than it ever has. Harry hears Vernon say they should put him somewhere and Petunia say there is nowhere.

The rest of the month is the kitchen. The tap is often in view. He watches Petunia cook and learns what cooking is, as procedure. She keeps a radio on in the kitchen, which gives him music and the news on the hour in a second clear voice. Laker Airways collapses and Vernon is pleased about it at length. The Six O’Clock News becomes a fixture because Vernon is home for it.

By the end of the month Harry understands most of what Petunia says to Dudley, nearly all of children’s television, and the gist of the news: about four hundred words receptively, five aloud. Petunia hears him “talking to himself” through the wall and tells Vernon it is not normal. Harry has two observations now, and both had looking in them. He has modelled Dudley: hits when bored, bored most of the time.

March 1982. Nineteen to twenty months.

In the first week he has most of the consonants and the vowels are still wrong. Petunia goes on complaining about the mumbling.

On Friday the 12th Dudley takes a red block, and Harry, who has been waiting for an occasion, says clearly, in a toddler’s voice, “Dudley has the red one.” Petunia, at the sink, turns round and drops a plate. Harry watches her face and understands, over the next ten real minutes, that she has filed the sentence with the bottle and the television. He decides to talk the way Dudley talks when she is in the room and to say what he likes when she is not. He is nineteen months old, and it is the first strategic decision of his life.

In the third week he talks to Dudley when they are alone, and Dudley, who has forty words, starts acquiring more. Petunia thinks Dudley is advanced. South Georgia is on the news from the 19th, and Vernon begins watching every bulletin.

On Saturday the 27th Harry climbs out of the second cot. Petunia gives up on cots and puts a mattress on the floor of the box room with a stair gate across the door. From the mattress he can reach the bottom shelf, and on the 28th he pulls down the first volume of the encyclopaedia. It is heavy and full of small marks and some pictures. He cannot read it. He looks at every page anyway over the following weeks, tearing eleven of them before his hands learn how pages turn, and nobody notices, because nobody has opened these books since 1975. He finds the map of Africa, the aardvark, and the page headed ALPHABET, which shows a dozen scripts in parallel and which he looks at more than any other page without knowing why. He begins to see that the small marks are a small set of shapes repeated.

April 1982. Twenty to twenty-one months.

Argentina invades the Falklands on Friday the 2nd, and from then on the television is on for news most of the day. Vernon watches everything. Harry gets hours of clear adult speech with maps, ship names, place names, numbers, and the ideas of a country, a government, an enemy, and being killed. He learns to tell the news from Vernon’s commentary on it: the newsreader says what happened and Vernon says how he feels about it, and the two have different grammars. The task force sails on the 5th.

On Easter Sunday, the 11th, the Dursleys go to Marge’s and Harry goes to Mrs Figg’s, where she gives him a hot cross bun. It is the first thing anyone has given him on purpose, and he will remember the taste for the rest of his life, which in his case is literally true. By now he can turn a page without tearing it.

In the third week he is on volume 2. He has all twenty-six shapes, has paired the capitals with the small letters by their positions on the ALPHABET page, and knows the order from the spines, but he does not yet know that the shapes are sounds. He matches the encyclopaedia’s map of the South Atlantic to the television’s, which is the first time two sources have agreed about something he can see.

South Georgia is retaken on the 25th. He has about fifteen hundred words receptively and can follow the news. In the last days of the month Petunia takes Dudley to the playground and Harry watches other toddlers from the pushchair, taking a sample of what two-year-olds do, for calibration.

Vernon is patriotic, Petunia is jumpy, and Dudley is nearly two and has learned the word want.

Chapter 2 takes this as summary, with one sharp detail: a casualty list read aloud.

May 1982. Twenty-one to twenty-two months.

The Belgrano is sunk on the 2nd and the Sheffield hit on the 4th. The Mail on Sunday launches on the 2nd and Vernon takes it, so there are now two papers in the house on Sundays. From the gap between “a British ship has been hit” and the names three days later, Harry learns that information arrives in pieces and that adults wait for more of it too.

On Wednesday the 12th Petunia has a dental appointment and Harry goes to Mrs Figg’s. Mr Tibbles sits on his lap for an hour, and the purr is a slow deep pulse. From a shelf of Agatha Christies Mrs Figg produces a child’s atlas, and Falklands and Argentina, which he knows as sounds, become shapes on a map.

The landings at San Carlos are on the 21st. By now the sound project is complete in outline: he understands ordinary English, and what is left is vocabulary, which comes with input. Dudley, nearly two, has learned to hit with objects, and does.

On Thursday the 27th Dudley hits him above the ear with a wooden engine. The pain lasts a subjective quarter of an hour before Harry can get his attention off it, and in that quarter hour he wants Dudley not to be able to do it again and pictures the engine somewhere Dudley cannot reach. The engine is on top of the wardrobe. Dudley howls. Petunia comes, looks at the wardrobe, and looks at Harry, who cries, which is also true, because his ear hurts. She takes Dudley out without a word. That night there is no quiet voice, because she has not told Vernon; Harry hears the gap at dinner where it would have gone, and learns that she is afraid of Vernon’s reaction as well as of Harry. Goose Green is the next day.

He has three observations now. Each time he wanted from inside a feeling that filled him; each time he saw the thing exactly, as if it were already so; each time it was so; each time Petunia was afraid rather than angry. He also knows that three is not many.

June 1982. Twenty-two to twenty-three months.

In the first week he turns over what it means that Petunia carried the engine rather than handing it to Vernon. He notices that she looks at him when she thinks he is not looking, and that he is always looking.

In the second week he has the week’s structure mapped: bins on Tuesday, Yvonne on Wednesday, Tesco on Thursday, Vernon’s golf on Sunday. He has measured that Petunia is kinder to everyone, himself included, while Vernon is at golf.

Argentina surrenders on Monday the 14th. Vernon opens a bottle and gives Dudley a sip, and Harry watches the bubbles rise at a pace he can count. The same week he puts his situation into a summary, with a two-year-old’s logic and an adult’s time to think it over: he is not Dudley; Petunia is not his mother; his mother was the voice in the night; the people on the doorstep put him here on purpose; the people here do not want him. He will have to arrange things himself, though he does not yet know what things.

Prince William is born on Monday the 21st and Petunia buys the commemorative magazine, which has photographs with captions. Dudley’s second birthday is Wednesday the 23rd. Yvonne and her boy come, and the Polkisses, and the woman from number seven. There are eleven presents, and Harry counts them. He is sent to Mrs Figg’s for the afternoon so that Dudley’s day is not spoiled, and is not sorry. She reads him the albums again, and this time, when she says Mr Tibbles and points, Harry sees that the marks under her finger are the same marks under every photograph of the same cat. It is the first time the shapes have meant anything. He finds the marks on the next page before she turns to it, and knows what she will say.

In the last days of the month he begins matching captions to cats from his memory of the two visits, and notices that he remembers the second visit better than the first. This is the first hint of what he works out in July.

Chapter 2 takes the captions as a short scene.

July 1982. Twenty-three to twenty-four months.

The first half of the month is warm. Petunia puts both boys in the paddling pool in the garden. There is the fence, the neighbours’ voices, a radio next door playing Radio 2 and then the news, and a bee, which moves at a pace Harry adores; he watches one for a subjective hour and learns everything about how a bee lands on a flower. He is dry by day from the middle of the month. He understood the whole business in February, and the body has caught up. Petunia, who has fought Dudley over the potty for months, is briefly pleased and then disturbed.

In private his speech now has adult syntax in a toddler’s mouth, and he drills pronunciation silently at night. Dudley’s vocabulary is accelerating because of him, and Petunia is pleased with Dudley.

The IRA bombs Hyde Park and Regent’s Park on Tuesday the 20th. Petunia is frightened of London for a month. Harry hears bomb and IRA and soldiers and opens a new category, people who kill on purpose, and connects it to the night.

On Wednesday the 28th, going through the night as he always does, he notices that the doorstep voices are clear and yesterday’s breakfast is not, and asks himself why. The answer is that he has gone over the one and not the other. That night he goes through the whole day in order, twice, which takes him two or three hours and the house half a minute. In the morning he has it all. He tries it on the 29th and the 30th and it holds. From now on nothing that happens to him need be lost. An ordinary person who tried this would run out of night; Harry runs out of day.

Saturday the 31st is his second birthday. Nobody says anything. He does not know the date. He knows that Dudley’s birthday gets a cake and his does not. Petunia looks at him oddly at breakfast, and in April 1983, reading his birth certificate, he will understand that she knew.

Chapter 2 ends on the memory discovery and the birthday.

August 1982. Two years to two years one month.

On Tuesday the 3rd the health visitor does the two-year check. Harry produces two-word phrases on request. She is pleased, Petunia is pleased, and it is the last time Harry deliberately performs below himself, though he will not know that for ten months. Lebanon is on the news; Vernon is not interested; Harry is.

In the second week he glimpses the folder in the bureau with his name on it while Petunia is on the telephone. He cannot read it. He memorises the shapes of every word on the top sheet anyway. At dinner Vernon talks about Grunnings, drills, the Japanese, his boss, and the Birmingham office, and this is the first business education Harry gets.

In the third week he is on volumes 7 and 8 by pictures and now by captions: LION under a lion, which he knows from Mrs Figg’s albums and from the television. One word at a time, by pictures, with captions in capitals that do not match the body text. Twenty words by the end of the month.

Marge comes for the bank holiday, from Saturday the 28th to Monday the 30th, with Ripper. Ripper corners Harry in the garden. Harry watches the dog’s weight shift forward over three or four subjective minutes and steps aside at exactly the right moment, three times, until the dog sits down confused. Marge says dogs know a wrong ‘un and Vernon laughs. Harry files that he can do this with anything slow enough, which is everything.

Mrs Figg’s letters say “fine.” Dumbledore spends the summer on Flamel and the Wizengamot, and a thought for the boy in Surrey goes no further than the thought.

Chapter 3 opens with Ripper.

September 1982. Two years one to two months.

In the first week Petunia buys Dudley’s things for playgroup, and Harry hears through the kitchen door that he is not going and why: Dudley needs other children, the other one doesn’t need anything, and she isn’t having him showing off in front of other people’s mothers. Harry has not, that he can remember, shown off. He has hidden carefully since March. It seems to be possible to show off by sitting still.

From Monday the 6th Dudley goes to playgroup on Mondays and Thursdays. On those mornings Petunia puts Harry in the box room with the gate across and gets on with the house, and for three hours, which is a subjective month, nobody looks at him or hits him or needs anything. These mornings are the best time he has had. He uses them for the encyclopaedia and for thinking.

Princess Grace dies on Tuesday the 14th and Petunia is upset for days; Harry learns what a famous person is. On Thursday the 16th Petunia cuts his hair to the scalp with the kitchen scissors, for no reason he can find. He hates it all day, and that night, going through the day, he dwells on his hair as it was, every piece of it, with a two-year-old’s full feeling. On the Friday morning it is back. Petunia screams, and Vernon is told that evening, and for the first time there is a consequence: Vernon carries Harry downstairs and shuts him in the cupboard under the stairs for the night to think about what he has done. The dark is total. He finds the light cord by feel after a couple of real minutes, sees the spiders and the shoes and the underside of the stairs and a rusted bolt on the inside, and is told through the door to turn that light off. He revises his table to four observations and finds that the fourth breaks two of his rules: his eyes were shut, and he was remembering rather than wanting. He does not know what to make of that.

In the last ten days he stops practising speech aloud, because Petunia hears, and practises silently instead, which works nearly as well because he can hear what he would have said. He finishes volumes 11 and 12 by pictures. The cupboard night has given him an idea, and he tries, for the first time, to make the thing happen on purpose, and fails.

October 1982. Two years two to three months.

On Tuesday the 5th Dudley climbs on a kitchen chair for the biscuit tin and the chair tips. Harry is in the doorway. The fall takes him two subjective minutes to watch. He sees where the back of Dudley’s head will meet the floor and does not want it, and pictures Dudley landing on the chair’s cushion, which is tied to the seat and travelling the other way. Dudley lands on the cushion; the chair lands three feet off with nothing tied to it. Petunia, at the sink, has seen all of it. She picks Dudley up and looks at Harry for a real quarter-minute, which to him is an hour, and says, quietly, “Go in the other room.” For nine days she is less cold. Harry measures it: she says his name twice, and gives him Dudley’s portion once. On the tenth day it resets. He learns that it works on people and on moving things, that she is afraid of it even when it helps, and that in her, gratitude does not survive fear.

On Tuesday the 12th the Falklands victory parade is on television and Vernon is moved to tears, which Harry has not seen before and studies. By the middle of the month he has finished the encyclopaedia by pictures and starts again at volume 1 with about sixty caption-words.

In the dark mornings of the third week he tries on purpose again, small things, and fails every time, and notes that every success so far had feeling in it that he cannot summon to order.

The clocks go back on Sunday the 31st and the box room is dark by five. He finds that at one angle the landing light reaches the page. The 31st is also the anniversary, which nobody knows about but him, roughly: the dark, the cold, and the first fireworks going off early for Guy Fawkes, which to him are long low booms. He goes through the night more than usual that night.

November 1982. Two years three to four months.

Channel 4 opens on Tuesday the 2nd at a quarter to five, with Countdown. Vernon has opinions about Channel 4. Petunia watches Countdown anyway, because it is on while she makes the tea and because, Yvonne says, she fancies Richard Whiteley. Harry gets, every weekday, nine letters chosen one at a time and named aloud, a clock, people saying words, and the words spelled out on the board; then a numbers round with the working written up. Within the first fortnight he has the names of all twenty-six letters and the idea that a word is its sounds in a row. By the end of the month he can do the numbers round in a subjective minute, having inferred adding and multiplying from the written working. He cannot do the letters round, because he does not know enough words by shape.

On Friday the 5th the Dursleys go to the Polkisses’ bonfire and Harry goes to Mrs Figg’s, who lets him watch the fireworks from her bedroom window. The rise of a rocket is the most beautiful motion he has seen. She says, watching his face, “You like those, don’t you, dear,” and then catches herself and says, “Well, don’t get used to it.”

On Wednesday the 17th he hears Petunia on the telephone to Yvonne, through the open box-room door. “He watches, Yvonne. He just watches. Dudley’s a normal little boy, he runs about, he shouts. That one sits there with my dad’s encyclopaedias open on the floor like he’s reading them. He’s two.” Then: “No. No, I don’t want anyone looking at him.” He learns that his looking has been noticed, and that Petunia will not let anyone from outside look at him, so that anything he wants that the Dursleys will not give him will have to come by a route that goes round her rather than through her. He does not know how to do that yet.

And the rules he will keep, no lying, including by performance, no stealing, no harming, and the law when the law is good, are cheap insurance against the ways he has just read that people with power go wrong. It is not a vow. It is a conclusion. He also decides that night that he has been talking at Dudley’s level in front of adults for fifteen months and that this is over.

July 1983. Two years eleven months to three years.

He reads twelve hours a day, by subject, in rotation, so that no subject is read twice running and each has a subjective week to settle before he comes back to it. With his glasses, which arrived on the 28th of June, a paperback page takes him five or six seconds and a book about half an hour, and the slowest part is his own hands turning the pages, so that he gets through something like fifteen books a day and over four hundred in the month. The books come from the Advertiser’s appeal, which brings hundreds of donations, mostly useless and a few of them gold; from Blackwell’s crate; from Mrs Ashworth, who arranges a special loan of fifty at a time from the county system; from a reading list the Mail pays for; from the trunk Dr Lowry brings from Cambridge on the 12th; and from Dumbledore. By the end of July he has mathematics to about second-year undergraduate level, and has found it the one subject in which thinking fast is nearly everything; physics through Feynman’s lectures; chemistry; the beginnings of molecular biology; economics from Samuelson through Friedman to Keynes himself, and the Economist weekly from the 15th; the twentieth century properly, including a hard week on the Holocaust; philosophy through Mill, Singer, Rawls and Hare, from which he works out roughly where he stands and why; statistics, where he is happiest; and medicine through Harrison’s and the WHO reports, including the eradication of smallpox three years earlier, which is the one thing in the medical books that reads like a victory, and he reads it twice.

The magical books are the seven first-year texts and Hogwarts: A History. He reads them in an evening and goes through them again in his head over the following days. Waffling’s Magical Theory is not a theory. The field has recipes and no model. Spells are treated as a given inventory with inventors’ names attached to a few of them. The incantations are Latin-ish and the Latin sometimes fits the effect and sometimes does not. Wand movement, incantation and intent are described as three ingredients and nobody has asked which does what. Transfiguration is described as pure intent with a wand. A History of Magic records the Statute of Secrecy in 1692 and says nothing about the fact that the muggles wizards hid from have since been to the moon. The beasts book is a taxonomy without a family tree. Nobody keeps data on anything. He begins a table of every spell mentioned anywhere in the eight books, with incantation, movement, effect, inventor if named, and page, and dictates it, and it runs to forty pages by the end of the month. His questions for Dumbledore run to sixty.

He practises for half an hour morning and evening with the door shut. The feather lifts one time in twenty by the 5th, one in five by the 10th, and one in three by the 20th. He moves on to a pencil, and then to lifting the corner of a page of a closed book. What works is a picture complete in every particular, held, with feeling behind it, and he has learned to supply the feeling from the night, and then, in July, from the WHO figures, which is a thing he notices himself doing and thinks about. What does not work is anything he cannot picture exactly, and anything hurried. On the 20th he discovers the ceiling: he tries running the picture faster, because he can, and nothing at all happens; slowed to about the pace a hand would take to make a wand movement, it works. He has no idea why. He records it. It is his first real datum about magic that did not come out of a book.

The muggle machinery assembles itself around him. Dr Whitlock’s report to the county recommends nothing the county can do, and recommends a trust, a university attachment, and that he be left alone to read. Geoffrey Pell, a solicitor in Guildford, approaches through Whitlock on the 4th, free of charge and for the publicity, and is kept because he is competent and because he laughs when Harry corrects his draft. Pell buys the cassette recorder on the 6th, and from then on a typist at his office transcribes what Harry dictates, and dictation becomes Harry’s way of producing anything for anyone else. Social services visit on the 6th and Harry answers honestly: they feed him and keep him clean, they do not like him, he has a room now, he used to sleep under the stairs. The report says satisfactory, with concerns noted. Petunia is humiliated at being inspected, and afterwards Harry says, “I’m sorry about this,” and she does not answer. Dr Peter Lowry comes from Cambridge on the 12th with a trunk and a colleague, tests Harry for a day, goes quiet in the afternoon, and offers Cambridge’s resources. Harry says books by post are as good as Cambridge and he will not travel weekly, and that a reader’s ticket at a university library within reach would be better; Lowry writes to the librarian at the University of Surrey. The trust deed is executed on the 14th: the Harry Potter Educational Trust, with Pell, Whitlock, and Vernon as trustees, Vernon insisting and being given a minority. Harry dictates a three-page letter of wishes, which Pell reads with an expression. On Saturday the 9th Harry is on Wogan, live, and it is the television moment of the summer: a very small boy on a very large sofa reading through the applause, and Wogan asking what the book is, and Harry telling him, and Wogan asking if it is any good, and Harry saying the author is wrong about the second chapter but puts it right by the fifth. The audience laughs for a long time. Harry, who did not mean it as a joke, works out why they laughed, and files it. The American networks are declined, because Dumbledore has asked that Harry not leave the protection and because Harry’s own view is that travel is time; the Japanese and German crews film him in the garden instead. Three publishers come to the house. He chooses the one whose editor asked the best question, and over three evenings, from the 28th to the 30th, dictates thirty-one thousand words for parents on how he learned to read, built on paired sound and spelling, with a method anyone could use with a subtitled television and a patient afternoon. The advance is fifteen thousand pounds. He reads the typescript for errors himself, in an hour.

Vernon is businesslike and has started saying “the boy” instead of “it.” Petunia avoids Harry more than before, now that Dumbledore has been in her house. Dudley comes for his twenty minutes every day and is beginning, in a small way, to be a brother. The bolt is gone and the cupboard is a cupboard.

Mrs Figg has been told by Dumbledore that she may be kind now, and comes round on the 2nd with a cake and is kind in a way that suggests two years of wanting to be. She tells Harry she is a friend of Professor Dumbledore’s. He understands that she has been watching him for two years, and is not angry, because what else could she have done, and asks her about the cats, and she tells him what a Kneazle is, which is his first magical creature.

Sunday the 31st is his third birthday and the first one that is a day. Dumbledore comes for the third time, with Lily’s own annotated Charms and Potions textbooks from the Potters’ effects held at Hogwarts: her handwriting in the margins, her corrections to the authors, and a drawing in the Potions book of a professor who is unmistakably Slughorn. He also brings a cake from Hagrid, with a note that Harry reads aloud, which says that Hagrid carried him to his aunt’s and that Professor Dumbledore says he can come and see him soon. Harry says, “I remember him. He has a beard. He was crying.” Mrs Figg comes. Petunia gives him a paperback she bought at Tesco without reading the title, and it is The Hobbit, which he has read, and he thanks her and means it. Vernon gives him a pound. Dudley gives him a drawing of a dinosaur and takes it back.

Hagrid asks every week. McGonagall says she would like to meet him, and will in September. Snape says nothing. The Improper Use of Magic Office opens a file on Harry Potter, finds nothing to put in it, and closes it. The Prophet runs a follow-up and a letters page. Lucius Malfoy reads both, files the facts that the Potter boy is in Surrey, famous, and warded by Dumbledore to the teeth, and concludes that there is no hurry about anything.

August 1983. Three years to three years one month.

The routine is set. He is up at half past six and has breakfast with Breakfast Time still on, because it is information. He reads from seven until twelve. Then there is the body hour: he has worked out that his muscles and bones do not care how fast he thinks and need what any child’s need, so there is the garden, the climbing frame the trust bought, and running, and he is thin and eating more now that food is a thing he gets rather than a thing he is given. He reads from one until seven. Dudley has his twenty minutes. From seven to eight he dictates: the question list, letters, notes for Pell and Whitlock, the spell table. He is in bed at eight. The night’s going-through, by the end of the month, has more day in it than there is night, so that he has begun skipping the mundane, and has noticed that he has begun to skip, and wonders what he is losing.

Rachel Dunne is hired on the 8th. She is twenty-four, a physics postgraduate at the University of Surrey, and she answered Pell’s advertisement. On Tuesdays and Fridays she drives Harry to the university library in Guildford, where Lowry’s letter has got him a reader’s ticket; she fetches inter-library loans from the branch; she types. On her first day she asks what he wants to read and he hands her a list of four hundred titles, dictated, in priority order by subject, and she says the library will not have half of them, and he says the half will do. Within a week she is talking to him as a colleague, which no adult has done, and he notices how much easier everything is when someone does.

In August he finishes Feynman by the 10th and starts Landau. He goes deep and fast into mathematics: Spivak, Rudin, the beginnings of algebra and measure theory. He reads Fisher and Savage, and derives what he can of Bayesian decision theory for himself from the encyclopaedia’s article on Bayes and the Economist’s book reviews. He reads chemistry, Watson’s molecular biology and the new Alberts, the first serious microeconomics, and accounting, which he reads as a system for not lying to yourself about money. He finishes the twentieth century and begins the nineteenth. He reads Popper, Kuhn and Lakatos in a week and argues with all three. He finishes Harrison’s and begins epidemiology.

The practice hour is folded into the body hour, in the back garden out of sight of the road, once Dumbledore’s dampening charm goes up on the 27th; until then he keeps to his room. The feather is one in two by the middle of the month. A pencil rolls. A page turns, one time in four, and he sees the application at once and the problem with it at once, which is that his eyes still have to look at the page. Nothing on living things, and nothing anyone can see. The ceiling holds: nothing faster than about a second of imagining per effect, however clearly he holds the picture. He varies what he holds constant and finds that the pace is the thing.

The spell table is complete for the eight books, and he notices that the inventors named are all recent, that the oldest spells have none, and that nobody remarks on this. Rachel types it and asks what it is. “I can’t tell you what it’s for yet. I’m sorry.” She says that’s fine. This is how the Statute will be handled with every muggle colleague for the next eight years: by declining, and never by lying.

On Saturday the 27th Dumbledore comes for the fourth time. He brings the second-year books and a few more, among them Spellman’s Syllabary and a Charms theory text. He reads Harry’s sixty-page question list on the spot, in ten minutes, while Harry watches him read at a two-hundredth of Harry’s speed and finds it nearly unbearable. He answers a tenth of it and says the rest needs books he will bring. Harry tells him everything about the wandless practice, including the ceiling, and Dumbledore is alarmed and visibly fascinated, and says that the ceiling is known in a general way and not explained. His conditions are nothing on living things, nothing Harry cannot undo, and nothing a muggle could see, and Harry says that these are his rules already. Dumbledore explains the Statute properly, and the Improper Use office, and the Trace, which Harry does not have, because it is laid with the letter. He tells Harry what he has done about the owl post, and Harry sets his terms: a monthly summary, and every letter from anyone who knew his parents. He mentions the Ministry’s interest and how he has deflected it, and promises an accounting of the Gringotts vault by September. Harry asks about Hagrid, and a visit is arranged for September; about McGonagall; and about the friend who is not in a position to visit, and Dumbledore gives the name Remus Lupin and says he has reasons. Harry asks who his parents’ friends were, and Dumbledore gives him three names, and says that one of them is in Azkaban, and why. Harry asks to read the record of the trial. Dumbledore says he will bring what the Ministry has, and Harry hears that this is not the same sentence as I will bring the record, and files it, and says he would like to come back to this. He learns that there was no trial in September, from the file itself, which has no questions in it, and from a letter on a Prophet letters page in Mrs Figg’s attic that nobody answered.

The interlude is Remus Lupin, in a northern town, in a room, doing muggle work, three days past the full moon of the 24th. In a newsagent’s he sees a magazine cover with the boy’s face on it and Lily’s eyes, buys it, and reads it standing on the pavement. At home he takes out Dumbledore’s letter, which says not yet, Remus, when it is safe, he is well, and the letter to Harry he has written eleven times and not sent, and writes it a twelfth time, and this time sends it, to Dumbledore, to hold.

The last scene is the end of the month. The Economist has come, and a Landau volume, and Harry reaches over and turns off Breakfast Time, because for the first time in his life there is better input in the room than the box that taught him to talk. That night there is too much day for the night, and he decides, with the pleasure of someone meeting a limit he can work on, that this is the first problem he has had that is not about other people.

Vernon has a new car and says “our Harry” to a man from the golf club and hears himself. Petunia has begun to go into Harry’s room when he is not in it and look at the books. Dudley can read twelve words.

The hottest July on record has run into August. Ethiopia is on the inside pages. Korean Air flight 007 will be shot down on the 1st of September, the day after this block ends.
