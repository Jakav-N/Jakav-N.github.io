# Outline: chapters 1 to 10

**Span:** Saturday 31 October 1981 to the end of August 1983. Harry from fifteen months to three years and one month.

**Where this block ends:** Harry has a bedroom full of books, a trust with a few thousand pounds in it, a postgraduate who drives him to a university library twice a week, a cassette recorder, a mentor he half trusts, a secret that two people know, a daily hour of wandless practice that has just hit a ceiling he cannot explain, and a reading programme that would take a university a decade. He has stopped watching the television that taught him to talk. He has just met the first problem of his life that is not about other people: there is more day than there is night to go through.

**What is dramatised versus what merely happens:** the chapters take perhaps a fifth of what follows. The rest is there so that what is shown has weight behind it, and so that later chapters can refer back to things the reader never saw.

## Month by month

### November 1981. Fifteen to sixteen months.

**State.** An infant whose brain has just started running two hundred times faster than his body. He has perhaps fifty words receptively and five productively from before the night, and for the first week he cannot make his body do anything he intends.

**Events.**

- *Sat 31 Oct.* Godric's Hollow. Bedtime: Lily, the cot, James downstairs. The door. James's shout, cut off. Lily carrying him up the stairs; her words at the cot (the canon words, which he keeps for life: *not Harry, please, not Harry, take me instead*). The tall shape in the doorway. Green. Her fall. The shape turns. Two words. Green. Pain that begins and does not finish beginning. Then the room comes apart slowly: plaster drifting, the shape in the doorway going to pieces and a scream that goes on and on and thins to something that leaves. Dust that hangs. The first silence. His own cry, which he hears start and goes on so long he gets used to it.
- *Later that night.* A huge shape fills the doorway. A voice like a slow landslide. Hands that take a subjective minute to lift him. A coat that smells of dog, smoke and rain. The shape shakes; he knows crying when he sees it. Another man arrives, a face he knows, the one who made the wooden dog bark: frantic, which at this speed is merely brisk. A long argument he cannot follow in which the known face wants him and the giant will not give him up. The known face hands over the motorbike. (This memory is a seed. When Harry is told, in August 1983, that Sirius Black betrayed his parents, he has a data point the official story does not: the man came to the ruin that night and was in pieces.)
- *Sun 1 Nov.* Hagrid has him. Where is not shown. He sleeps most of it, concussed and exhausted. He wakes in the air at night: stars that do not move, a sea of orange lights (Bristol), the engine a deep steady drone, Hagrid talking the whole way in long slow sounds. Sleeps again.
- *Sun 1 Nov, late.* The doorstep. Half-woken by being set down on cold stone. Three voices: the slow sobbing one he knows; a sharp clipped one; a calm one the others wait for. He keeps the sounds, and will replay them for two years before he can decode them: the shapes that will turn out to be *Hagrid*, *Dumbledore*, *Hogwarts*, *Potter*, *Harry*, *Voldemort*, and, in the sobbing voice, a phrase that ends *the boy who lived*. A hand on his forehead; warmth; sleep.
- *Mon 2 Nov.* Petunia's scream, a long rising sound. Her face above him, which is not his mother's face; the eyes are wrong. He watches her read the letter: her face takes a subjective hour to go through fury, fear and grief and settle on the expression it will mostly wear around him for the next twenty months. Vernon's bulk. The kitchen. Dudley, who regards him with enormous interest and then hits him with a spoon, slowly and inevitably. Vernon's voice at its loudest; then, that night, at its quietest, which Harry files at once as the most dangerous sound in the house.
- *2 to 12 Nov.* The body. He has been walking since twelve months; now he cannot. His reaches miss. His babble comes out as drawn-out lowing that frightens Petunia a little ("he makes a noise like a cow"). Petunia's verdict: backward, and no wonder. He works it out over ten days, in the cot, by trial: decide, send the command, wait, watch the hand arrive. By the end of the month he walks again, and better than before, because he can plan each step and watch each one land.
- *Mid Nov.* GP registration (Petunia does the minimum). The birth certificate and NHS card arrive from a Lincoln's Inn solicitor with a covering letter that says nothing else; Petunia files them in the bureau. The health visitor is notified of a new child in the household.
- *All month.* The scar hurts, healing on body time: weeks of it. He learns to put his attention somewhere else, which is the first skill he teaches himself.

**Projects.**

- *Sound.* The house's noises (boiler, fridge, clock, Hoover, television) are sorted in days. Voices are the real work. He knows his own name; nobody says it. He learns *Dudley* within the week because it is said a hundred times a day, and *no*, and the rising shape of a question.
- *The still world.* People are statues that occasionally shift. The things that move at a pace he finds natural: the fly on the kitchen window, the second hand of the kitchen clock (his first clock; he watches it for subjective hours), water from the tap. He likes the tap.
- *Waiting.* Every time the door opens. By the end of the month the inference has been made, at the level a fifteen-month-old makes inferences: the door is Petunia.

**The Dursleys.** Vernon refuses to say the boy's name; "it" for the first fortnight, then "him," then "the boy." Petunia cleans more than she did. Dudley is told he is a good boy for hitting. The cot is Dudley's old travel cot, in the box room, between Vernon's golf clubs and twelve red volumes with gold marks on their spines.

**Wizarding side.** Dumbledore installs Mrs Figg on Wisteria Walk in the middle of the month and gives her her instructions. Sirius Black is in Azkaban without trial; Pettigrew's finger has been buried; Crouch is prosecuting hard; the Longbottoms are attacked around now. Snape begins his first full year as a teacher. Dumbledore's letter to Petunia has said what it says. He does not visit, and has no plan to.

**Muggle world as it reaches him.** The Nine O'Clock News (Vernon), Crossroads and Coronation Street (Petunia), Play School (Dudley). None of it is words yet. All of it is sound.

**Into the chapters.** All of this is chapter 1.

### December 1981. Sixteen to seventeen months.

**Events.**

- *First week.* **The bottle.** Petunia is late with his bottle; Dudley has a cold and all her attention. Harry is hungry in the way a baby is hungry, which fills the whole mind, and he looks at the bottle on the table for a real half-minute, which is a subjective hour and a half, with a picture of it in his hands so complete he can feel the weight. The bottle slides along the tablecloth and drops into the cot. Petunia saw the slide. She screams (the second scream). That night, Vernon over the cot, the quiet voice, for a long time. Nothing is done, because there is nothing to be done; the house's temperature drops for a week. Harry does not understand what he did. He has the sequence: want, picture, bottle, fear.
- *Second week.* Heavy snow (real: 8 to 13 December, then record cold). The window. Each flake takes a subjective minute to fall past the pane. He watches for hours. It is the first beautiful thing he has had time for.
- *Mid Dec.* **First visit to Mrs Figg** (Petunia's Christmas shopping; two pushchairs are too many). Cabbage. Four cats who look at him with an attention he recognises and does not get from people. Mrs Figg, who has been told not to be kind and compromises by being boring, reads him the cat photograph albums, every caption, for an hour. It is the best day since the night. He goes home having been talked to more than in the previous six weeks together.
- *23 to 27 Dec.* **Christmas. Marge** arrives with Ripper (young, already unpleasant). Marge's question: "What's that one, then?" Vernon: "Petunia's sister's. Car crash." Harry keeps *car crash* as a sound attached to Petunia going stiff. Dudley's presents fill the living room. Harry is given a rattle from Dudley's discards and a jumper Marge bought for Dudley that turned out too small. The tree lights. He notices the kitchen's fluorescent tube breathes (a hundred times a second to the world, once every two seconds to him); the tree's bulbs do not.
- *Late Dec.* The first *want* that does not work: he wants the light on in the dark box room, with the whole of himself, and nothing happens. He has no framework in which this is informative, but he keeps it.

**Projects.** *Sound:* about thirty words receptively by New Year; he has the shape of a question and a command and the fact that the sounds are a system rather than a set of signals. *Body:* fine control returning; he can hold a cup. *Still world:* snow, the fluorescent tube, the fire.

**The Dursleys.** Petunia reads the bottle as the beginning of the end. Vernon says "the orphanage" out loud twice; Petunia says "we can't" and does not say why. Marge recommends a firm hand.

**Wizarding side.** Figg's first owl to Dumbledore: "He's here. Thin. Quiet. They don't like him." Dumbledore: expected. The Lestranges and Crouch's son are tried around the turn of the year.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2: the bottle (scene), Figg (scene), snow (a paragraph), Marge and *car crash* (half a page).

### January 1982. Seventeen to eighteen months.

**Events.**

- *First fortnight.* Record cold (Braemar hits minus twenty-seven on the 10th; snow across England). His room is the coldest in the house. He learns that the boiler's sequence of sounds predicts warmth by a subjective half-day.
- *Mid Jan.* **Eighteen-month check.** Both boys. Harry walks well now, says *Duh-ee*, *no*, *more*. The health visitor notes nothing. Petunia's hand on his shoulder tightens when she is asked how he is settling in, and he notices that the health visitor looks at him properly, which almost nobody does.
- *Late Jan.* He climbs out of the travel cot. Petunia finds him on the landing at five in the morning looking at the stairs. A bigger cot with higher sides; he will be out of that by March.
- *All month.* Vernon gets a company Granada. The word *Ford* and the word *Granada* and the tone of gloating.

**Projects.**

- *Sound.* **The television talks in the same system as the people.** Petunia leaves BBC1 on in the mornings because Dudley likes the music: Play School, then schools programmes. Words and Pictures sounds out words with a magic pencil; Harry is eighteen months old and the words are shapes, and he files the shapes. The news at one. He learns dozens of words a day now, because the television repeats, speaks clearly, and talks about what it shows.
- *The night.* Every night, before sleep, he goes through it; it comes whether he wants it or not. He has noticed that the doorstep voices stay clear only because he goes through them.
- *The want.* Twice: once for the light (nothing), once for Dudley to stop screaming (Dudley stops, eventually, as Dudley always does; Harry registers, without words, that this proves nothing).

**The Dursleys.** Vernon's "we should put him somewhere." Petunia: "There's nowhere." Petunia's phone calls to Yvonne begin to be a regular source for Harry: gossip, the neighbours, the Polkisses, the woman at number seven, Mrs Figg ("a bit odd, all those cats").

**Wizarding side.** Quiet. Figg's letters say "fine."

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2, summary.

### February 1982. Eighteen to nineteen months.

**Events.**

- *Mid Feb.* **The television.** Dudley is napping; Petunia is hoovering upstairs; the set is off; Harry is in the playpen facing the dark screen. He wants it on with the whole of himself and pictures the picture coming back. It comes on, at volume, on the lunchtime news. Petunia comes down the stairs at a speed he has never seen her move. She looks at the remote on the mantelpiece, at Dudley asleep, at him. She says nothing. She turns it off. That night the quiet voice, longer. The playpen is moved from the living room to the kitchen, where there is nothing to want.
- *Late Feb.* Laker Airways collapses; Vernon is pleased about it at length. The Six O'Clock News becomes a fixture; Vernon is home for it.

**Projects.**

- *Sound.* Receptive explosion. By the end of the month he understands most of what Petunia says to Dudley, nearly all of children's television, and the gist of the news. About four hundred words receptively, five aloud.
- *Speech.* Production experiments in the cot at night: his mouth is a toddler's, the sounds come out toddler, but he can hear exactly what is wrong and correct it over hundreds of attempts a night. Petunia hears him "talking to himself" through the wall and tells Vernon it isn't normal.
- *The Dursleys, modelled.* Petunia's face has a Dudley setting and a Harry setting. Vernon's voice has three volumes and the quiet one is the dangerous one. Dudley hits when bored, which is most of the time.
- *The want.* Two incidents now. Both: want, picture, the thing done, fear. Both times he had been looking at the thing. This is not yet thought. It is the beginning of a table.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2: the television (scene).

### March 1982. Nineteen to twenty months.

**Events.**

- *Mid Mar.* **The sentence.** Dudley takes a red block. Harry, who has been practising for a month, says, clearly, in a toddler's voice, "Dudley has the red one." Petunia, at the sink, turns round. A plate goes. He sees her face and understands, over the next two subjective days (ten real minutes), that she has filed it with the bottle and the television. He decides to talk the way Dudley talks. He is nineteen months old; it is the first strategic decision of his life, and the narrator may say so.
- *Late Mar.* Out of the second cot. Petunia gives up on cots: a mattress on the box-room floor, a stair gate across the door. From the mattress he can reach the bottom shelf. **He pulls down volume 1 (A–B).** Heavy, full of small marks and some pictures. He cannot read it. He looks at every page anyway: the map of Africa, the aardvark, the page headed ALPHABET showing a dozen scripts in parallel, which will matter later. He begins to see that the small marks are a small set of shapes repeated.
- *19 Mar onward.* South Georgia on the news; Vernon starts watching every bulletin.

**Projects.** *Speech:* alone with Dudley, he talks. Dudley, who has forty words, acquires more; Petunia thinks Dudley is advanced. *Body:* turning pages without tearing them takes a fortnight; he tears eleven pages of volume 1 learning, and nobody notices because nobody has opened these books since 1975.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2: the sentence and the plate (scene).

### April 1982. Twenty to twenty-one months.

**Events.**

- *2 Apr onward.* **The Falklands.** Invasion; the task force sails on the 5th; South Georgia retaken on the 25th. Vernon watches everything. The television is on for news constantly. Harry gets hours of clear adult speech with maps, place names, ship names, numbers, and the concepts *country*, *government*, *enemy*, *killed*. He learns to separate the news from Vernon's commentary: the news says what happened; Vernon says what he feels about what happened; the two have different grammars.
- *Easter (11 Apr).* The Dursleys to Marge's. Harry to Mrs Figg's; she gives him a hot cross bun, the first thing anyone has given him on purpose. He will remember the taste for the rest of his life, which in his case is literally true.

**Projects.** *Sound:* about fifteen hundred words receptively. He can follow the news. *The encyclopaedia:* volume 2 (C–D). He now knows all twenty-six shapes, has paired the capitals with the small letters by position on the ALPHABET page, and knows the order from the spines. He does not know they are sounds. *Speech:* Dudley-level in public (two words), full sentences in private.

**The Dursleys.** Vernon patriotic. Petunia jumpy. Dudley nearly two and learning *want*.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2, summary with one sharp detail: a casualty list read aloud.

### May 1982. Twenty-one to twenty-two months.

**Events.**

- *2, 4, 21, 28 May.* Belgrano, Sheffield, San Carlos, Goose Green. Vernon's moods track the bulletins. From the gap between "a British ship has been hit" and the names three days later, Harry learns that information arrives in pieces and that waiting for more of it is something adults do too. The Mail on Sunday launches (2 May); Vernon takes it.
- *Late May.* **The train.** Dudley hits him with a wooden engine. It hurts for a subjective quarter of an hour before he can get his attention off it, and in that quarter hour he wants Dudley not to be able to do that again, and pictures the engine somewhere Dudley cannot reach, and the engine is on top of the wardrobe. Dudley howls. Petunia comes. Nobody could have put it there. She looks at Harry. Harry, who has learned to arrange his face by watching Dudley's, cries, which is also true. That night there is no quiet voice, because Petunia has not told Vernon; Harry hears her not telling him, a gap at dinner where it would go. He learns she is also afraid of what Vernon will do.
- *Mid May.* Mrs Figg (Petunia's dentist). Mr Tibbles sits on him for an hour and the purr is a slow deep pulse. Mrs Figg produces, from a shelf of Agatha Christies, a child's atlas. Maps. *Falklands* and *Argentina*, which he knows as sounds, as shapes and places.

**Projects.** *Sound* complete in outline: he understands ordinary English; what is left is vocabulary, which comes with input. *The want:* three observations. Want, picture, done, fear; he was looking each time; feeling seems to matter. The narrator may observe that three observations is not very many and that Harry, who has nothing else, has already noticed this.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2: the train (scene); Figg's atlas (a paragraph).

### June 1982. Twenty-two to twenty-three months.

**Events.**

- *14 Jun.* Surrender. Vernon opens a bottle, gives Dudley a sip; Harry watches bubbles rise at a pace he can count.
- *21 Jun.* Prince William born. Petunia buys the commemorative magazine: photographs with captions.
- *23 Jun.* **Dudley's second birthday.** Party: Yvonne and her boy, the Polkisses, the woman from number seven. Eleven presents; Harry counts. Harry is sent to Mrs Figg's for the afternoon so Dudley's day isn't spoiled. He is not sorry. The cat albums again; he has begun matching the cat's name, which she says aloud, to the shapes under the photograph. Tibbles has the same shapes every time. **It is the first time the shapes mean anything.**
- *All month.* He concludes, at nearly two, with a two-year-old's logic and an adult's time to think it over: he is not Dudley; Petunia is not his mother; his mother was the voice in the night; the people on the doorstep wanted him here; the people here do not want him. He will have to arrange things himself. He does not yet know what things.

**Projects.** *The encyclopaedia:* volumes 1 to 4 by pictures. *Dursleys:* the week's structure is known perfectly (bins Tuesday, Yvonne Wednesday, Tesco Thursday, Vernon's golf Sunday), and he has measured that Petunia is kinder to everyone, himself included, by a detectable amount while Vernon is at golf.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2: the captions (short scene).

### July 1982. Twenty-three to twenty-four months.

**Events.**

- *All month.* Warm. The paddling pool. The fence; the neighbours' voices; a radio next door (Radio 2, then the news). A bee, which moves at a pace he adores; he watches one for a subjective hour and learns everything about how a bee lands on a flower.
- *20 Jul.* Hyde Park and Regent's Park bombs. Petunia frightened of London for a month.
- *Mid Jul.* He is dry by day. He understood the whole business in February; the body caught up now. Petunia, who has fought Dudley over the potty for months, is briefly pleased, then disturbed.
- *Late Jul.* **The memory discovery.** Going through the night, as always, he notices that the doorstep voices are clear and yesterday's breakfast is not, and asks why. The answer: he has gone over the one and not the other. He tests it: that night he goes through the whole day, in order, twice. The next day he has it. From now on, each night, he goes through the day; it takes a subjective hour or two and a real half-minute, and nothing is lost again. (The narrator can note that an ordinary person who tried this would run out of night. Harry runs out of day.)
- *31 Jul.* **His second birthday.** Nobody says anything. He does not know the date; he knows that Dudley's birthday gets a cake and his does not. Petunia looks at him oddly at breakfast, and in April 1983, reading the birth certificate, he will understand that she knew.

**Projects.** *Speech:* adult syntax, toddler pronunciation; pronunciation drilled at night. *The encyclopaedia:* volumes 5 and 6. *Memory:* the nightly replay becomes the spine of his life.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 2 ends on the memory discovery and the birthday.

### August 1982. Two years to two years one month.

**Events.**

- *Early Aug.* **Two-year check.** Harry produces two-word phrases on request. The health visitor is pleased; Petunia is pleased. Harry has decided to perform at Dudley's level for this, and it is the last time he will deliberately perform below himself, though he will not know that for ten months.
- *28 to 30 Aug.* **Marge and Ripper**, bank holiday. Ripper corners Harry in the garden. Harry watches the dog's weight shift forward over a subjective minute and steps aside at exactly the right moment; the dog goes past; three times; the dog sits down, confused. Marge: "Dogs know a wrong 'un." Vernon laughs. Harry files that he can do this with anything slow enough, which is everything.
- *All month.* Lebanon on the news (Beirut); Vernon uninterested; Harry not. Petunia's bureau drawer, glimpsed while she is on the phone: a folder with his name on it. He cannot read it. He memorises the shapes of every word on the top sheet anyway.

**Projects.** *The encyclopaedia:* volumes 7 and 8 by pictures, and now by captions: LION under a lion, and he knows *lion* as a sound. One word at a time, by pictures, and the captions are in capitals that do not match the body text. Twenty words by month's end. *Dursleys:* the bureau; Vernon's work (Grunnings, drills, the Japanese, his boss, the Birmingham office), learned from dinner-table complaint, which is the first business education he gets.

**Wizarding side.** Figg: "fine." Dumbledore: the summer; Flamel; the Wizengamot; a thought for the boy in Surrey that goes no further than the thought.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 3 opens with Ripper.

### September 1982. Two years one to two months.

**Events.**

- *Mid Sep.* **The haircut.** Petunia, who cannot stand his hair, takes the kitchen scissors to it. It is the first time he has been handled roughly for no reason he can see, and he hates it, and that night, going through the day, he dwells on his hair as it was, in detail, with a two-year-old's full feeling. In the morning it is back. Petunia screams (the third scream). Vernon is told. The quiet voice, and this time a consequence: **the cupboard under the stairs** for the night, "to think about what he's done." Dark. Spiders. A light cord he finds by feel after a subjective day, and is told off for.
- *From this he learns:* it works on himself; it can work while he is going through the day, half asleep; it goes with picturing-with-feeling rather than wanting-while-looking. Four observations. He revises the table.
- *Sep onward.* **Dudley starts playgroup**, two mornings a week. Harry is not sent; Petunia does not want him "showing off" (she has noticed). Two mornings a week the house is Petunia and Harry. She puts him in the box room with the gate. He has the encyclopaedia and the window, and these mornings, a subjective month each, undisturbed, are the best time he has had. He uses them for the books and for thinking.
- *14 Sep.* Princess Grace dies; Petunia upset for days; Harry learns what a famous person is.

**Projects.** *The encyclopaedia:* volumes 9 to 12. *Speech:* he has stopped practising aloud at night (Petunia hears) and practises silently, which works nearly as well because he can hear what he would have said.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 3: the haircut and the first night in the cupboard (scene).

### October 1982. Two years two to three months.

**Events.**

- *Early Oct.* **Dudley's fall.** Dudley on a kitchen chair reaching for the biscuit tin; the chair tips; Harry is in the doorway; the fall takes a subjective two minutes; he sees the floor coming for Dudley's head and wants it not to and pictures Dudley landing on the chair's cushion, and Dudley lands on the cushion, which was on the chair, which has gone the other way. Petunia, at the sink, saw all of it. She picks Dudley up. She looks at Harry for a subjective hour. "Go in the other room." For nine days she is less cold; he measures it: she says his name twice; she gives him Dudley's portion once. Then it resets. He learns that it works on people and on motion; that she is afraid of it even when it helps; that gratitude in her does not survive fear.
- *12 Oct.* Falklands victory parade on television; Vernon moved to tears, which Harry has not seen before and studies.
- *31 Oct.* The anniversary. Nobody knows. He does, roughly: the dark, the cold, the first fireworks going off early for Guy Fawkes, which to him are long low booms. He goes through the night that night more than usual.
- *Late Oct.* The clocks go back; the box room is dark by five; he discovers that at one angle the landing light reaches the page.

**Projects.** *The encyclopaedia:* finished by pictures; begun again at volume 1 with about sixty caption-words. *The want:* five observations. He begins, in the dark mornings, to try to do it on purpose, small things, and fails every time, and notes that every success so far has had feeling in it that he cannot summon on demand.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 3: Dudley's fall (scene).

### November 1982. Two years three to four months.

**Events.**

- *2 Nov, 4:45 pm.* **Channel 4 launches with Countdown.** Vernon has opinions about Channel 4. Petunia watches Countdown because it is on while she makes tea, and because, Yvonne says, she fancies Richard Whiteley. Harry gets, every weekday, nine letters placed in a row, a clock, people saying words, and the words spelled out on a board letter by letter; then a numbers round, arithmetic spoken and written. Over the month he learns (a) that letters are sounds, because the contestants say *S* and a shape appears and when they say the word the shapes spell it; (b) the names of the letters; (c) that numerals are spoken quantities; (d) arithmetic, from the numbers round's working. By the end of November he can do the numbers round in a subjective minute. He cannot do the letters round, because he does not know enough words as shapes. But he has the alphabet as sounds now, and the idea that words are built from them.
- *5 Nov.* Guy Fawkes. The Dursleys at the Polkisses' bonfire; Harry at Mrs Figg's. She lets him watch the fireworks from her bedroom window. A rocket's rise is the most beautiful motion he has seen. She says, watching his face, "You like those, don't you, dear," and catches herself, and says, "Well, don't get used to it."
- *Mid Nov.* **The telephone.** Petunia to Yvonne, Harry in the box room with the door open: "He watches, Yvonne. He just watches. Dudley's a normal little boy, he runs about, he shouts. That one sits there with his grandad's encyclopaedias open on the floor like he's reading them. He's two." A pause. "No. No, I don't want anyone looking at him." He learns that his looking has been noticed and that she will not let anyone look at him. He adjusts what plans he has.
- *All month.* Second molars. Weeks of pain at 200x. Attention control improves.

**Projects.** *Letters as sounds* (Countdown). *Numbers* (Countdown; Dudley's counting books; Petunia counting Dudley's sweets). *The want:* no new incidents; attempts in the dark continue to fail.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 3: Countdown (a page); the telephone (scene).

### December 1982. Two years four to five months.

**Events.**

- *Early Dec.* **The fever.** Flu, three days, which at 200x is a very long illness; delirium; Petunia's hand on his forehead at three in the morning, competent and without warmth, and he is grateful anyway and keeps it. He learns that the body's misery cannot be thought away, only sidestepped, and that there is a limit to sidestepping.
- *Mid Dec.* **The new television.** Twenty-six inches, teletext, remote control, because the Polkisses have one. Vernon shows the teletext off to Marge when she arrives: news pages, the weather map, football results. Pages of text in big clear letters, capitals and lower case, headlines. Harry, who now has letters as sounds, sees that he could read this if he knew the rules. Vernon leaves page 101 up while he eats breakfast. From the football results (team names he knows from Vernon's shouting) Harry starts to decode. By Christmas he can sound out simple regular words. Irregulars defeat him.
- *23 to 28 Dec.* **Christmas.** Marge; Ripper, older and meaner; the sidestep again; Marge says the dog has gone soft. Dudley's presents: a tricycle, a Big Trak, a cassette player, plastic everything. Petunia says the box room would make a lovely playroom for Dudley.
- *Christmas Day.* Mrs Figg sends, via Petunia, who allows it because Marge is watching, a Ladybird book. His first book of his own. He can get a quarter of the words: *the*, *and*, *shoe* by the picture, *elves* by the picture; *enough*, *thought*, *night* defeat him. It is the first time print has talked to him without a voice, and it is a story, and he does not think much of the story.

**Projects.** *Reading:* regular words by sounding; whole-word shapes about a hundred and fifty; the irregulars a wall. *Numbers:* he can add and multiply anything he hears; he has worked out division from the Countdown target. *Dursleys:* the playroom plan heard through the wall; he knows what is coming.

### February 1983. Two years six to seven months.

**Events.**

- *All month.* **Cupboard experiments.** Bulb: one in twenty. Latch: one in fifty. A new target, the spider, fails entirely for a fortnight and then works once; the spider does not care for it. First explicit rules: picture it exactly; feel it; it works more after going through the night; it never does anything he has not pictured; it is not strength, it is clarity. He thinks about what else could be pictured and concludes that in this house the answer is nothing anyone will see.
- *1 Feb.* TV-am; Vernon switches (it's ITV; it's cheerier).
- *Mid Feb.* **The bolt.** Petunia finds the latch open one morning (the one time he forgot to close it, after a success). She buys a bolt. Vernon fits it on the outside. The bolt is a fact he has to think about for a long time, and does, and it goes into the weight on one side of a decision he has not yet framed.
- *All month.* The Mail. Twenty minutes a day when Petunia leaves it on the counter to wrap peelings. Headlines, captions, the odd paragraph. Sixty per cent. He has *-tion*, four kinds of *-ough*, silent *e*, and has worked out from the half-readable encyclopaedia article on English that the spelling is layered history (Anglo-Saxon, French, Latin, Greek) rather than a system, which means the exceptions have to be memorised, which is fine, because memorising is cheap for him and deriving is not.

**Projects.** *Reading:* the wall of irregulars; the realisation that the television gives sound without spelling and the paper gives spelling without sound, and that what he needs is both at once, and that he does not know where that exists. *Plans:* mental age about six; he can hold a plan. The plan is: books. Books need reading. Reading needs pairs. Everything else waits on that.

### April 1983. Two years eight to nine months.

**Week 1 (28 Mar to 3 Apr). Getting 888 on.**

- He learns the remote by watching Vernon: TEXT, 8, 8, 8, hold. He cannot reach the remote and cannot be seen using it. He shows Dudley that TEXT 8 8 8 makes "writing come." Dudley does it constantly. Vernon rages at the writing; Petunia discovers that the writing lets her follow her serial through Dudley's noise and leaves it on in the afternoons; Vernon turns it off in the evenings; Dudley turns it back on. A domestic war over the 888 button in which Harry is the arms dealer.
- Which programmes are subtitled: the Radio Times marks them, and he can read the listings. Schools programmes on BBC2 in the mornings (Words and Pictures Tuesdays; Look and Read), which Petunia leaves on because they are "educational for Dudley" and which put words on screen by design. Some children's BBC at four. The Sunday serial. Not the news; he gets the news from the paper next morning.
- *3 Apr, Easter.* Dudley's eggs; one from Mrs Figg for Harry; the Dursleys at Marge's on the Monday, Harry at Figg's.

**Week 2 (4 to 10 Apr). Pairs.**

- Hours of paired sound and spelling. Method: for every spoken word, the written word; build the table; infer the rules; list the exceptions. English has a great many exceptions. He has a great deal of time. By the 10th he can read anything he can decode, slowly, at the pace of sounding it out.

**Week 3 (11 to 17 Apr). Fluency.**

- The eye is the bottleneck. A line in three fixations, then two. He experiments: distance, angle, reading the paper upside down (no faster), the pattern of saccades (he settles on something like a Z). By the 17th he is fluent at about five hundred words a minute; by the end of the month, eight hundred. He discovers what reading is like for him: four or five words a subjective minute, a slow lecture he can argue with.
- *At Mrs Figg's, Easter Monday.* He reads her Radio Times, her cat manuals, three Agatha Christies in an afternoon. She sees him with one and does not register that his eyes are moving along the lines, because what she sees is a toddler holding a book; then she does register. "Harry?" "Yes, Mrs Figg?" "...Nothing, dear." She tells herself she imagined it. She does not write to Dumbledore.

**Weeks 3 and 4 (11 Apr to 2 May). The house.**

- In roughly this order: the Mail every day, every word, including the adverts, the letters page and the horoscope (he begins checking Russell Grant and the Mail's astrologer against outcomes, and by June has a sample). The twelve volumes of the encyclopaedia, in the playroom with Dudley and, when Petunia is out, carried one at a time to the cupboard and back, which is the only rule he breaks, and he puts them back. The Collins dictionary. The Radio Times. The Argos catalogue (the world's objects and their prices; his first economics). The Yellow Pages (the world's trades; LIBRARIES; the address). The Surrey A–Z (where he is). Petunia's cookbooks (chemistry and procedure). The Reader's Digest home repair manual (physics and tools). *Kane and Abel*, his first novel, read as a document about what adults think other adults want. Two Catherine Cooksons. Every one of Dudley's books. The King James Bible from the bureau, in two sittings, about which he has thoughts. The car's handbook. The television's manual, which explains 888, too late. The Grunnings catalogue (torque, chuck sizes, prices; he understands Vernon's job now).
- *The bureau folder.* His birth certificate: HARRY JAMES POTTER, 31 July 1980, Godric's Hollow; JAMES POTTER; LILY POTTER formerly EVANS. An NHS card. A covering letter from a Lincoln's Inn solicitor, November 1981, saying nothing else. Dudley's certificate: 23 June 1980, five weeks older; he had assumed more. **The shoebox**: Evans family photographs; Petunia and a red-haired girl as children, Cokeworth, a seaside; the red-haired girl in none of the later ones. His mother's face, for the first time since the night, at the age of nine. He goes through it for a long time.
- *What he has by 2 May.* The shape of the world (the encyclopaedia is 1962, the Mail is 1983; four and a half billion people, not three). Numbers. That there was a war his grandparents' age and another his parents'. Libraries, universities, newspapers, governments, money. That Uri Geller bends spoons and the Mail is undecided. That people die of things, in numbers. That his parents died of something and the only account he has is "car crash," which came out of Vernon with the sound Vernon makes when he lies about golf. Mental age about fifteen. He is, by any reasonable description, a different person from the one who started the month, and the chapter should feel like the lights coming on in a house one room at a time.

**The Dursleys.** Vernon notices nothing. Petunia notices everything and says nothing, in the way she says nothing; she has started to leave the room when he is in it with a book. Dudley thinks the writing on the television is his.

**Wizarding side.** Figg's half-seen thing. Nothing else.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 4, all of it.

### May 1983. Two years nine to ten months.

**The campaign (2 to 16 May).** Six attempts, two weeks.

1. *Tue 3 May.* "Could we go to the library? It's on the High Street. It's free." "What do you want with a library?" "Books." "You've had books." No.
2. *Thu 5 May, Tesco.* A poster for Saturday Story Hour, with puppets. He points it out to Dudley in Petunia's hearing. Dudley wants puppets. "We'll see," which is no.
3. *Sat 7 May.* Dudley asks again, reminded. Vernon's golf; no.
4. *Wed 11 May, Yvonne.* Yvonne's boy goes to Story Hour. Harry asks the boy about the puppets at the right moment. Yvonne: Petunia should bring Dudley, it's lovely, Mrs Ashworth does the voices. Petunia: she might.
5. *Sat 14 May.* Petunia takes Dudley to Story Hour and leaves Harry at Mrs Figg's. A failure of a particular kind: he has got the family to the library and not himself. He recalibrates: he has to be useful to the trip.
6. *Thu 19 May.* To Petunia, in Dudley's hearing: Dudley might like Harry to come to carry Dudley's coat. Dudley wants a coat-carrier. Petunia finds Harry easier to bring than Figg is to arrange.

**The visits.** Saturday 21, Tuesday 24 (Petunia has found the Tuesday toddler session, because Dudley liked the puppets and Yvonne goes Tuesdays), Saturday 28, Tuesday 31 May. About ninety minutes each.

- *Visit 1.* The building; the smell; the Dewey chart on the wall, which he has in a minute; "go and look at the picture books"; he walks to the 500s and takes down the thickest general science book (Asimov's guide, the 1972 edition) and reads a hundred and fifty pages standing. Mrs Ashworth passes, sees a very small boy with a very large book, and assumes he is copying someone.
- *Visit 2.* The atlas; H. G. Wells's short history of the world; the realisation that the nonfiction section is eight thousand volumes, the county system twelve branches, that there is a British Library, and that he will never read all of it at this rate, which is a relief and a provocation. **Mr Hobday**, in the armchair by the periodicals with the Economist, says, as a joke, "Any good?" Harry, who has been thinking about how to begin, says: "It's out of date on the structure of the atom, but the chemistry's fine." Mr Hobday laughs, and then does not. Harry sees that he has one, and stops there, because Petunia is coming.
- *Visit 3.* Hobday has clearly been thinking. He asks a real question about entropy. Harry answers it. "How old are you?" "Two. Nearly three." "Right." Harry reads a medical dictionary and an old Samuelson. Mrs Ashworth watches from the desk, now unsure.
- *Visit 4.* *The Ascent of Man*; an introductory psychology text, with particular attention to the chapters on intelligence testing and child development. He now knows what a prodigy is, what the tests measure, roughly what he would score, what the literature says happens to prodigies (not good), and what happens to children taken into care. He has the information to decide.

**The deliberation (Wed 1 to Fri 3 June).** Three nights in the cupboard; perhaps three real hours of hard thinking, which is twenty-five subjective days. He lays out the options and puts rough numbers on them:

- *Stay hidden; the library twice a week.* Three hours of reading a week against the twelve a day he wants. Certain and nearly worthless.
- *Run away.* He is two feet ten. He would be returned within a day, to a worse position, and the Dursleys would stop the library.
- *Tell the Dursleys everything and ask.* He has tried in part; he estimates the full version at one in ten of working and nine in ten of the cupboard and no library; and the "showing off" telephone call tells him Petunia's objection is fear, not stinginess, and fear does not negotiate.
- *Reveal to one adult who can escalate: a teacher, a doctor, a librarian.* Likely to get him attention, testing, resources, and press, in that order or another. Risks: the Dursleys' retaliation (bounded, now, by outside attention); being taken into care (he puts it at perhaps one in five; the psychology book says prodigies with poor homes are often left in them with "support"; the Dursleys' house looks fine); attention from whatever the Dursleys are afraid of, which killed his parents (he does not know what it is; he knows Petunia's fear of it is specifically about the thing he can do, so the rule is that the thing he can do stays hidden); the thing he can do being discovered by testers (he will refuse tests that could show it; he will not lie about it; he will decline).
- *Reveal publicly on his own.* Not possible; he cannot leave the house alone.

He chooses the fourth, at the library, with two witnesses, at a time Petunia is occupied. He decides to ask Petunia once more first, properly, because it is fair and because her answer is information.

- *Fri 3 June, breakfast, Vernon gone.* "Aunt Petunia. I can read. I've read everything in the house, and the encyclopaedia, and the dictionary. I'm not making it up. I need books, a lot of them, and I need to go to the library more than twice a week, or for you to get books out for me. I'll be no trouble. I'm asking you first." She goes white. "You'll say nothing of this. To anyone. You hear me? Nothing. Or it's the cupboard and no library at all." "I hear you." (True. He does not say he agrees.) Her answer has settled the other question too: whether the Dursleys will ever give him what he needs.

**Sat 4 June. The reveal.** Story Hour; Petunia in the children's corner with Dudley; Mr Hobday in his chair. Harry takes Bronowski to the armchair, reads a paragraph aloud, and explains it. Hobday waves Mrs Ashworth over. Harry, honestly and in order: "I can read. I've been able to for about seven weeks. My aunt knows and has told me not to tell anyone. I'm telling you because I need books and I don't think there's another way to get them. I'd be grateful if you'd tell someone who can do something: a teacher, or the newspaper, or whoever you think. I'm not supposed to be talking to you. She's going to be very angry. Please don't let that stop you." "How old are you, love?" "Two years and ten months." Story Hour ends. Petunia comes round the shelf with Dudley and sees the three of them. "He copies things. He doesn't understand them." Hobday: "He's just explained Boltzmann to me, madam." Petunia takes both boys out, fast. Mrs Ashworth is left holding Bronowski and looks at Hobday.

**The Dursleys.** Vernon notices Petunia is "jumpy." Petunia has worked out, from the request on 3 June, exactly how far things have gone, and has decided on silence as policy, which lasts one day.

**Wizarding side.** Exams at Hogwarts. Figg: "fine; he reads, I think, a bit." Dumbledore reads this and thinks: good; Lily's son.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 5, all of it, ending on Mrs Ashworth looking at Hobday.

### June 1983. Two years ten to eleven months. Day by day.

**Sat 4.** The drive home: Petunia silent and white; Dudley asking about the puppets. At home she puts Harry in the cupboard and bolts it and does not tell Vernon until after dinner, which Harry hears her working up to through the door. Vernon's reaction, at full volume: *freak*, *show us up*, *what did I say*. Then the cupboard door opens and both of them are there, and **Harry speaks to them as himself for the first time.** What he says, in substance: I can read; I've been able to for seven weeks; I asked you first and you said no; I've told two people at the library who will tell others; I did it because I need books and there was no other way to get them; I'm not going to do anything to you; I'd like to be let out of here; I know you're afraid of what I am. Vernon's bluster. Petunia: "He's like her." Harry: "I don't know what she was like. I'd like to." Petunia leaves the room. Vernon: the cupboard, and no library, and no talking to anyone. Harry: "The library's already done." He is bolted in. He expected this and has a plan for it, which is to wait.

**Sun 5.** Nothing visible. Vernon rings nobody because there is nobody to ring. Petunia keeps Dudley away from the cupboard. Harry, in the dark, goes through the two conversations he has had with adults in his life and refines what he will say to the next ones.

**Mon 6.** Mrs Ashworth tells the area librarian, who thinks she is exaggerating, and her friend in the county education department, who does not. Mr Hobday telephones the Surrey Advertiser and asks for "someone who does the human interest" and gets **Tom Brayley**, twenty-four, who thinks it is a hoax and writes it down anyway.

**Tue 7.** Brayley telephones 4 Privet Drive. Vernon hangs up. Brayley telephones the library.

**Wed 8.** Brayley at the door, mid-morning. Petunia does not answer. Harry, let out of the cupboard for breakfast and not yet put back, hears the knock, and the second knock, and goes into the hall and speaks **through the letterbox**: "I'm Harry. I'm the one you're here about. My aunt won't open the door. You should ask Mrs Ashworth at the library and Mr Hobday; they've seen me read. I'm not allowed to talk to you." "Who says?" "My aunt and uncle." "How old are you?" "Two years and ten months. Please don't knock again; it frightens her." Brayley stands on the step for a while. Petunia finds Harry in the hall and does not know what he has done and he tells her, because she asks.

**Thu 9.** General election. Thatcher's landslide. Nothing else gets on any front page for two days.

**Fri 10.** **The Surrey Advertiser** runs it, front page below the fold: a Little Whinging toddler reads university textbooks, say library staff; a retired engineer's quotes; the librarian's; the boy's own words through a letterbox; the family "declined to comment." Vernon reads it at breakfast because it is left on the step with the Mail. The quiet voice, which Harry notes has lost some of its force now that there is a newspaper in the room.

**Sat 11.** A letter from **Dr Anne Whitlock**, Senior Educational Psychologist, Surrey County Council, requesting an appointment to assess Harry "in connection with his educational provision." Vernon: no. Harry, at the kitchen table, lays it out for Vernon as one adult to another: the authority has powers; the paper has the story; refusing looks like hiding something; cooperating looks like good parents; prodigies attract scholarships, television, book deals, and money. Vernon's calculation visibly shifts. Petunia: "They'll find out." Harry: "Find out what?" He knows what she means; he asks because he wants her to say it. She does not. Mrs Figg, who takes the Advertiser, reads it and writes to Dumbledore: "The boy's in the local paper, something about reading. I thought you ought to know."

**Sun 12.** The Mail's Surrey stringer picks up the Advertiser piece and telephones. Vernon, who reads the Mail, is flattered and talks. Harry listens from the hall and afterwards sets terms with Vernon for the first time: his words quoted exactly; nothing said about him that isn't true; and he will not be made to say anything he doesn't believe. Vernon agrees because the stringer has mentioned a figure.

**Mon 13.** **Dr Whitlock's assessment**, at the house, 10 am. The Mail's reporter and photographer arrive at 10:30 because Vernon has double-booked. Whitlock: standardised tests, which he ceilings in minutes; reading-age passages, the hardest of which he reads at a glance; then she tries him on things she has in the car for her own reading (a journal, a textbook) and he discusses them. She asks how. He tells her: the subtitles, the encyclopaedia, Countdown, the Mail. She asks about memory; he demonstrates on the morning's conversation, verbatim. She asks how he thinks so quickly and he says, "I'd rather not say," and she looks at him and lets it go, and he notices that she let it go and thinks better of her for it. She notices he holds the book close; an eye test is arranged. He asks for library access and books; she says she will see what can be done, and that other people will want to meet him. "I know. I'd like them to bring books." The Mail gets its interview in the garden; Harry reads throughout, which the photographer loves.

**Tue 14.** **The Daily Mail**, page three: THE BOY WHO READS. Vernon's paper, with Vernon in it, trying to look paternal. BBC South Today by six; the Nine O'Clock News "and finally." Dumbledore receives Figg's owl at breakfast, among many, and reads "something about reading" and is pleased in a general way and goes to a Wizengamot session. He does not understand what he has been told, and will know that he did not.

**Wed 15.** Every national. Television vans on Privet Drive. Neighbours at windows. Petunia's social position inverts in a morning: she is the aunt of the genius, and hates it, and cannot entirely hate it. **Harry's first television interview**, in the living room, BBC: he has a book open in his lap and reads it through the questions, answering without looking up until the interviewer asks him to, and then looks up and gives her his whole attention and answers in a child's voice with adult syntax, and goes back to the book. "What are you reading?" He says. "Is it any good?" "It's wrong about one thing in chapter two but the author fixes it in chapter five." The nation sees a two-year-old reading a university textbook on camera.

**Thu 16.** Wires: Reuters, AP. American morning shows telephone. The number goes ex-directory. Offers: three publishers, two universities, several television programmes, a toy company. Vernon fields them with growing confidence. Books begin to arrive: a box from Mrs Ashworth, a box from Dr Whitlock, a crate from Blackwell's in Oxford with a card, parcels from strangers. The cupboard cannot hold them. **The bedroom.** Harry puts it to Vernon and Petunia before anyone outside asks where he sleeps: people are going to ask; it would be better for everyone if the answer were a bedroom. Dudley's playroom is emptied into the garage; Dudley's tantrum lasts the day. Harry has the box room, a bed, the encyclopaedia, and stacks.

**Fri 17.** Worldwide. A Japanese crew, a German one. Breakfast Time films him at the kitchen table reading over his cereal; Frank Bough is charmed; Petunia makes tea for a man off the television with hands that shake. Harry has been reading twelve hours a day since Tuesday; the new books are the first he has chosen from more than a shelf.

**The reading, 14 to 20 June, and what it does.** A general history of the twentieth century. The World Health Organisation's statistics on child mortality, which Whitlock brought because he asked for "something true about the world." A book on global poverty (the Brandt Report, 1980). Medical texts. The Mail's own coverage of Ethiopia's drought, of Beirut, of the Cold War (Pershing, Andropov, the "evil empire"), of AIDS, of three million unemployed. An ethics anthology from the library with Mill, Singer's drowning child, and a dozen replies. He is fifteen or sixteen mentally, with a child's freshness of feeling and more time to feel it than anyone has had. **The decision** comes on the night of Monday 20 June, in the new room, going through the day: what matters to him is that things go well for people; almost nothing is arranged to make that happen; he has a resource nobody else has; the rules he will keep (no lying, including by performance; no stealing; no harming; laws when they're good) are cheap insurance against the ways he has just read that people with power go wrong. No vow. A conclusion. He also decides, that night, that he has been talking at Dudley's level in front of adults for fifteen months and that this is over.

**Sat 18.** **The Daily Prophet.** Rita Skeeter, thirty-two, who reads muggle papers for the Prophet's "Muggle Watch" column, sees the name on Thursday and the photograph on Friday and has the front page on Saturday: THE BOY WHO LIVED, AMONG MUGGLES. Potter, 2, called "genius" by Muggle scholars; raised by Muggle relatives in Surrey; Ministry "monitoring." The address is in the piece, because it is in the Mail. *Interlude: the Great Hall, Saturday breakfast.* The owls. The paper lands by Dumbledore's plate. He reads it and is very still. McGonagall reads it over his shoulder. Flitwick asks for it to be passed. Snape reads it and looks at the photograph and leaves the table, and Dumbledore watches him go. McGonagall: "Albus, he learned to read from the television." "So it appears." "At two." "Two years and eight months, Minerva, the article is specific." "Albus." "Yes. I am going." Hagrid, at the far end, is crying and asking whether this means he can go and see him.

**Sun 19.** Dumbledore to Wisteria Walk, in a suit, by Floo to Figg's fireplace, to see the street: a television van, a photographer eating a sandwich on the bonnet of a car, the house. Figg's account, which is more than her letters were: the cupboard, the thinness, the watching, the thing she saw at Easter and told herself she hadn't. Dumbledore to Bagnold in the afternoon, to say that he has it in hand and the Ministry need not send anyone. Bagnold's office is relieved; Crouch's is not. Fudge asks a question about the Statute and is told it is being attended to. *At Privet Drive:* Vernon signs the Mail's exclusive for £8,000 over Harry's objection, and Harry renegotiates the family's end of it over the kitchen table that evening: sixty per cent of everything to a trust for books, forty to the household, nothing said in his name that isn't true, and the bolt comes off the cupboard door tomorrow. Vernon, who has had a very good week, signs the back of an envelope.

**Mon 20.** The eye test (mild myopia; round NHS frames ordered). The Mail's serialisation begins. Dudley, at a loose end without his playroom, comes to Harry's door to be read to, and Petunia allows it because it is "educational"; it becomes twenty minutes a day and Dudley's vocabulary doubles in a month. The Hogwarts Express leaves. The bolt comes off.

**Tue 21. Midsummer. Dumbledore.** See chapter 8.

**Wed 22 to Thu 30.** The reading month begins (see July). ### July 1983. Two years eleven months to three years.

**The reading programme.** Twelve hours a day, by subject, in rotation, so that no subject is read twice in a row and each gets a subjective week to settle before he returns to it. Sources: the Advertiser's "Books for Harry" appeal (hundreds of donations, mostly useless, a few gold); Blackwell's crate; Mrs Ashworth's special loan of fifty at a time from the county system; the Mail's paid-for reading list; Dr Lowry's trunk from Cambridge (12 July); the Dumbledore books. By the end of July: mathematics to about second-year undergraduate (he finds it the one subject in which thinking fast is nearly everything); physics through Feynman's lectures; chemistry; the beginnings of molecular biology; economics (Samuelson, then Friedman, then Keynes himself; he takes the Economist weekly from the 15th); the twentieth century properly, including a hard week on the Holocaust; philosophy (Mill, Singer, Rawls, Hare; he works out roughly where he stands and why); statistics, where he is happiest; medicine (Harrison's; the WHO reports; smallpox, eradicated three years earlier, which is the one thing in the medical books that reads like a victory, and he reads it twice).

**The magical books, and what he sees in them.** The Standard Book of Spells (Grade 1); A History of Magic; Magical Theory; A Beginners' Guide to Transfiguration; One Thousand Magical Herbs and Fungi; Magical Drafts and Potions; Fantastic Beasts and Where to Find Them; The Dark Forces: A Guide to Self-Protection; Hogwarts: A History. He reads them in two days and then reads them again slowly. What he notices: Waffling's *Magical Theory* is not a theory; the field has recipes and no model; spells are treated as a given inventory with inventors' names attached to a few; incantations are Latin-ish and the Latin sometimes fits the effect and sometimes doesn't; wand movement, incantation and intent are described as three ingredients and nobody has asked which does what; transfiguration is described as pure intent with a wand; *A History of Magic* records that the Statute of Secrecy is 1692 and that wizards have been hiding from a muggle world that has since gone to the moon; the beasts book is a taxonomy without a phylogeny; nobody keeps data on anything. He begins a table of every spell mentioned anywhere in the eight books: incantation, movement, effect, inventor if named, page. He dictates it (see below) and it runs to forty pages by the end of the month. His questions for Dumbledore run to sixty.

**Wandless practice.** Half an hour morning and evening, door shut. The feather: one in twenty by the 5th, one in five by the 10th, one in three by the 20th. A pencil. A page of a closed book lifting at the corner. What works: a picture complete in every particular, held, with feeling behind it; he has learned to supply the feeling from the night, and then, in July, from the WHO figures, which is a thing he notices himself doing and thinks about. What does not: anything he cannot picture exactly; anything hurried. **The ceiling (20 July):** he tries to run the picture faster, because he can, and nothing happens at all; slowed to about the pace a hand would take to make a wand movement, it works. He has no idea why. He records it. It is his first real datum about magic that is not in a book.

### August 1983. Three years to three years one month.

**The routine.** Up at half past six. Breakfast with Breakfast Time, still, because it is information. Reading seven to twelve. The body hour (he has worked out that his muscles and bones do not care how fast he thinks and need what any child's need: the garden, the climbing frame the trust bought, running; he is thin and is eating more now that food is something he gets rather than something he is given). **Rachel Dunne**, hired **8 August**: twenty-four, physics postgraduate at the University of Surrey, answered Pell's advertisement. Tuesdays and Fridays to Guildford (the reader's ticket Lowry's letter arranged); inter-library loans fetched from the branch; typing. First conversation: she asks what he wants to read; he hands her a list of four hundred titles, dictated, in priority order by subject; she says the library won't have half; he says the half will do. **The reading.** Physics: Feynman done by the 10th; Landau begun. Mathematics deep and fast: Spivak, Rudin, the beginnings of algebra and measure theory; he reads Fisher and Savage and derives what he can of Bayesian decision theory for himself from the encyclopaedia's article on Bayes and the Economist's book reviews. Chemistry. Molecular biology (Watson; Alberts, just published). Economics: the Economist weekly; the first serious microeconomics; accounting, which he reads as a system for not lying to yourself about money. History: the twentieth century finished; the nineteenth begun. Philosophy of science (Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos), read in a week and argued with. Medicine: Harrison's finished; epidemiology begun.

**The magic.** The practice hour folded into the body hour, in the back garden out of sight of the road (Dumbledore's dampening charm on the garden goes up on the 27th; until then he keeps to the room). Feather one in two by mid-month. A pencil rolling. **A page turning** (one in four): the first step toward the reading spell he will not have for two years; he sees the application at once and the problem at once, which is his eyes. Nothing on living things. Nothing anyone can see. The ceiling confirmed: nothing faster than about a second of imagining per effect, however clearly he holds the picture. He tries varying what he holds constant and finds that the pace is the thing.

**The Hogwarts books, second pass.** The spell table complete for the eight books: incantation, wand movement, effect, named inventor, page. I'm sorry." She says that's fine. (The Statute, handled by declining and never by lying; this will be the pattern with every muggle colleague for eight years.)

**Dumbledore's fourth visit, Saturday 27 August.** Second-year books and a few more (Spellman's Syllabary; a Charms theory text). He reads the sixty-page question list on the spot, in ten minutes, while Harry watches him read at a two-hundredth of Harry's speed and finds it nearly unbearable. He answers a tenth of it and says the rest needs books he will bring. The wandless practice: Harry tells him everything, including the ceiling; Dumbledore is alarmed and, visibly, fascinated, and says that the ceiling is known in a general way and not explained; his conditions (nothing on living things; nothing he cannot undo; nothing a muggle could see) are Harry's rules already, and Harry says so. The Statute explained properly: the Office, the Trace, which Harry does not have and why ("it is laid with the letter"). The owl post: Dumbledore says what he has done; Harry's conditions: a monthly summary and every letter from anyone who knew his parents. The Ministry's interest and Dumbledore's deflection of it. The Gringotts vault: an accounting by September. Harry asks about Hagrid (a visit arranged for September); about McGonagall; about "the friend who's not in a position to visit" (Dumbledore: a man named Remus Lupin, who has reasons). Harry asks who his parents' friends were and Dumbledore gives him three names, and one of them is in Azkaban, and he says so, and why. Harry asks for the trial transcript. Dumbledore says there was no trial. Harry is quiet for a real second, which is a subjective three minutes. "I'd like to come back to that."

**Interlude: Remus Lupin.** A northern town; a room; muggle work; three days past the full moon of the 24th. In a newsagent's he sees a magazine cover with the boy's face and Lily's eyes, and buys it, and reads it standing on the pavement. At home he takes out Dumbledore's letter ("not yet, Remus; when it is safe; he is well") and the letter to Harry he has written eleven times and not sent, and writes it a twelfth time, and this time sends it, to Dumbledore, to hold.

**The last scene.** The end of the month. The Economist has come, and a Landau volume, and he reaches over and turns off Breakfast Time, because for the first time in his life there is better input in the room than the box that taught him to talk. That night there is too much day for the night, and he decides, with the pleasure of a person meeting a limit he can work on, that this is the first problem he has had that is not about other people.

**The Dursleys.** Vernon has a new car and says "our Harry" to a man from the golf club and hears himself. Petunia has begun to come into his room when he is not in it and look at the books. Dudley can read twelve words.

**Muggle world.** The hottest July on record has run into August. Korean Air 007 is shot down on 1 September, the day after this block ends. Ethiopia is in the papers' inside pages.

**Into the chapters.** Chapter 10: a weekday in full; Rachel's first day; the page turning and the ceiling; Dumbledore's visit, selectively; the interlude; Breakfast Time off.

### 9. Marginalia

1. **The trunk** (400). Friday evening; eight books and a photograph that moves. He reads them by Sunday. The photograph he does not go through at speed.
2. **What the books are like** (500). The Standard Book of Spells read as an inventory; Waffling read as a disappointment; the three ingredients nobody has separated; the Statute's date against the moon landing; the spell table begun. Shown as his reading, with the arguments he has with the authors.
3. **The feather** (400). Door shut. Fortieth attempt, a twitch; ninetieth, a lift. What works and what does not. Supplying the feeling from the WHO figures, and noticing himself do it.
4. **Mrs Figg** (300). The cake; kindness that has waited two years; "a friend of Professor Dumbledore's"; what a Kneazle is.
5. **The machinery** (500). Social services, honestly answered, and "I'm sorry about this." Pell, who laughs at the right place. The trust deed and the letter of wishes. Lowry's trunk and his quiet afternoon. The cassette recorder, and what dictation is to someone who thinks at his speed: the slowest fast thing he has.
6. **Wogan** (300). Scene. The sofa; the book through the applause; "Any good?"; the laugh he did not intend and works out.
7. **The reading book** (200). Three evenings; thirty-one thousand words; the typescript checked in an hour; what it is for.
8. **The birthday** (400). Lily's margins; Slughorn drawn in a Potions book; Hagrid's note read aloud and "He was crying"; *The Hobbit* from Tesco, thanked for and meant; a pound; a dinosaur, taken back. The first birthday that was a day.

### 10. Allocation

1. **A Tuesday** (700). The day in full, as routine: Breakfast Time over cereal; reading seven to twelve; the body hour and why; Rachel Dunne at the door at one with a car and a list she thinks is impossible; the library in Guildford; home; Dudley's twenty minutes; dictation; bed; the night, which is getting short.
2. **Rachel** (300). The first conversation; "the half will do"; the first adult who talks to him as a colleague, and how much easier that makes everything.
3. **The page** (400). The garden. A pencil rolling; a page turning, one in four; the application seen at once and the problem with it, which is his eyes. The ceiling: faster does nothing; the pace of a hand does. He has no idea why and records it.
4. **The table** (200). Forty pages; the oldest spells have no inventors and nobody remarks on it. "I can't tell you what it's for yet." "That's fine."
5. **Dumbledore, selectively** (700). The ten minutes of watching him read sixty pages. The ceiling confirmed as known and unexplained. The conditions that were already his rules. The Trace and the letter. The owl post and Harry's terms. Three names; Azkaban; no trial; three subjective minutes of silence; "I'd like to come back to that."
6. **Interlude: a newsagent's** (400). Remus; the cover; the pavement; two letters; the twelfth draft, sent to be held.
7. **Breakfast Time** (300). The Economist and a Landau; the set switched off. The night too short for the day, and the first problem he has had that is not about other people.
