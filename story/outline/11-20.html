Harry Potter and the Speed of Thought

Outline: chapters 11 to 20

This block runs from September 1983 to August 1985. Harry goes from three years and one month to five years and one month. It covers the year in which he reads every muggle subject to the level of someone who has spent a life on it, and the year after, in which he adds his body, his hands, his magic and his money to the day, and it ends with a wand.

By the end of it he knows more about most fields of muggle knowledge than the people who work in them, and has begun to publish in the one field where that can be shown. He has turned a page without touching it ten thousand times and has a list of what magic seems to be made of. He has a trust with a little over a million pounds in it, two companies, a program on sale in every high-street shop that sells software, eight patents and two that pay, five researchers paid for by a fund of his own, an office of six above a shop, and sixteen hundred people he writes to, eighty of whom he calls colleagues. He has published eleven papers in five fields, reads fifteen languages, has a hundred inventions on cassette, and has run the first properly designed trial of a method of teaching children to read, which his method won. In his new world, without a wand, he has set two teachers keeping the first statistical records in the school’s history and found that students fail spells whose words mean nothing; argued with the Charms master for two years about fourteen spells with no roots; got the Herbology mistress measuring her soil; put a book of columns in Hagrid’s hands; read fifty years of the Prophet and written his own history of what it showed; drafted a petition for a hearing that no procedure allows; and found two clauses in a goblin treaty that he has been told not to be clever about. He has read two years of the Daily Prophet and a Ministry file with a hole in it, and he has put a number on Sirius Black’s guilt that he does not like and keeps revising. He has a workshop in a retired engineer’s garage, a physicist who has stopped pretending he is a child, a psychologist who has stopped pretending he is a subject, and a headmaster who has stopped pretending he knows what to do with him. He has had Hagrid and McGonagall and a man from the Ministry in his aunt’s living room, and letters from a dozen people who knew his parents, one of which he has read four hundred times, and he has asked Dumbledore two questions about 1943 and 1945 and been told not tonight to both. On the last day of the block he stands in a shop in Diagon Alley with a wand in his hand that has chosen him, and finds out what the difference is.

The months are written first and stand on their own. The chapter map and the chapter notes follow them. The months hold far more than the chapters can.

What the two years find

Before the months, the threads, so that the months read as steps in them and not as lists.

The channel. What the wandless work finds, in order, each by an experiment with a count. September 1983: the pace, three pictures a second, a hand’s rate, which faster does nothing against. January 1984: one picture per tick, never two. February 1984: a held light flickers at the pace, so a sustained spell is a repeated spell and holding is re-picturing on the beat. March 1984: one subject per tick rather than one effect, since a feather that rises and glows is one picture and works, and a feather and a separate light never do; this is what the twenty-six invented spells have in common and why invention is rare. April 1984: strength is a curve and not a switch, measured in grams and yards, and the curve moves outward month by month as he grows, so that he has a number for his own power and a rate for its growth and can estimate when it will reach what the school expects at eleven. April to May 1984: the understanding limit, which is that the picture must contain the effect and the effect must have been seen, so that twenty spells shown once in the garden become six he can do and fourteen he cannot, and teaching should go show, name, wand. May to July 1984: transfiguration is at least three things to the channel, material, shape and size, each its own tick, so a silver needle can be made in two ticks and a long one in three, and a match becomes a needle in one only when the picture is of a change and not a swap. November 1984: a ward is a picture stored in an object and keyed to a condition, which is the same operation as binding a spell to a word, so a spell is a ward on a word and a ward is a spell on a thing. February to March 1985: a ward’s effect can be another ward’s condition, so wards chain into sequences, and a ward can have two outcomes, so there are choices, and sequence and choice are enough to build any procedure, which he notes and declines to build further without a wand and a witness, because a chain of wards does what it was told. April 1985: magic cannot carry into his head information he does not have, since a picture of an unread page shows nothing and a picture of a read one shows print he already knows, so the eyes stay the wall, and the only route past them is another mind, read directly, which he files as the thing he wants most at Hogwarts.

The Black case. It moves on records and inference, never on luck. October 1983: fourteen points. February 1984: the Wizengamot’s proceedings show Crouch’s emergency order of 1980, fourteen people sent to Azkaban under it without a hearing, four of them released in 1982 with no finding either way, and Dumbledore’s vote for the order and then against its renewal; the base rate changes even where the case does not. March 1984: the charm that hides a house, from McGonagall, and the keeper who can be changed and nobody told, put beside meant to, which gives him the one hypothesis that explains every line of the file; he takes the number to one in two and puts the hypothesis to Dumbledore, who says that it is possible and that he does not believe it. April 1984: the Prophet’s obituaries of 1979 to 1981 have a shape, and the shape is an organisation, and Dumbledore says yes three times. May 1984: the nine ways a wizard can leave a street, and two public registers, which say that Pettigrew was licensed to apparate and that nobody who matters was a registered animagus, and that a register of animagi is a register of the honest. September 1984: Andromeda Tonks, found in a three-line marriage notice from 1973, who stopped deciding in 1981 and starts writing. January 1985: the four routes to a hearing, from the rules and the proceedings together, each priced in standing, and the conclusion that he will have to earn standing with something those people want and does not yet know what that is. The number: three in four, two in three, one in two, and by 1985 two in five, each marked as a number he does not trust.

The world inside, from its own records. The copyist’s parcels and what each shows. The Wizengamot’s proceedings: fifty hereditary seats, twelve that vote together ninety times in a hundred, three unfilled since the war, and a vote he can predict. The Ministry’s accounts: a levy on the bank, a tax on shops, fines; two-fifths on law enforcement and nothing on the school or the hospital, and Dumbledore’s one word for how it decides what matters, which is habit. St Mungo’s annual reports: causes of death with numbers, two-thirds of them natural and not broken down, no line for spell failure, and forty children a year hurt by accidental magic, thirty of them under five picturing a thing they wanted. The Hogwarts letter lists since 1900, which are the birth register a decade on: forty a year, then thirty-two, then twenty-six, then twenty, a quarter of the children lost in a generation, on one page with a line through it that the Ministry has not drawn. The treaty with the bank, found among the proceedings because it was ratified: the exchange rate in a schedule revised by consent three times in two hundred years and last in 1971, since when muggle gold has risen tenfold and the schedule has not moved, and the clause that lets the bank refuse anyone who trades in the metal of the coin, which is the door Dumbledore shut and the reason it is a door.

Muggle ideas carried across before he has a wand. Statistics: McGonagall’s weekly record of twenty first-years and six spells, designed in January 1984, kept from September, and the finding by June 1985 that the order of success is the order in which the effects can be seen. A teaching order: show, name, wand. The population page. Wolfsbane for Remus from February 1984, paid from the vault, because the Prophet of 1979 reported the potion and nobody had thought to buy it for him. The library catalogue read and marked, and the books the bookshop does not stock found through the copyist. The galleon question kept and not asked, until the treaty answers it.

Inventions and money, with the reasons they work. The page-turner, so that he can read in public at the speed of his eyes. The book rest that keeps its angle. The reading program, which is subtitles on purpose: a word on the screen, read aloud by the parent, the child pressing a key when they have it, and the next word chosen by what the child has got wrong, from a paper on how a tutor should choose problems that had been proposed four times and shipped never. The keyless chuck, a brass bar bored for a steel ball, simpler than the Japanese patents Owen Price brings from the British Library, licensed to Grunnings at three per cent. The medicine cap. The salt-solution sachet with the measure printed on it. The annual reports of every listed company read in a month, two thousand of them, and forty chosen, which is the money engine beside the royalties. Everything he builds is built by someone else’s hands, and whose hands are for what is in the bible.

Month by month

September 1983. Three years one month to three years two months.

The routine from August holds and hardens. He is up at half past six, reads from seven until twelve, spends an hour in the garden, reads from one until seven, gives Dudley his twenty minutes, dictates from seven until eight, and is in bed by eight with the night still mostly ahead of him. Rachel Dunne comes on Tuesdays and Fridays and drives him to Guildford. The subjects take their turns through the week as they did in August, and the week’s Economist comes on Friday and is read before lunch.

The reading this month is the beginning of the deep part. In physics he is through Landau’s mechanics and into the fields, and finds that the mathematics is now the limit rather than the physics, so Tuesday starts to bleed into Monday. In mathematics he reads Halmos and the first of the algebra, and begins, around the third week, to notice that he is reading proofs the way he reads the Mail, with the end of the paragraph visible before it arrives, and that this is not true of the other subjects. In biology he is in Alberts and has begun to ask Rachel, who is a physicist, questions she cannot answer, and she brings him a biochemist from her department on the second Friday, who stays for two hours and leaves looking as Mr Hobday looked after the tea. In economics he has moved from the textbooks to the arguments between the people who wrote them, and has read Hayek and Keynes against each other in the same week and decided that both were right about the things the other was wrong about, which he suspects is how most arguments between clever people come out. In history he is in the nineteenth century. In medicine he has reached the papers on smoking and lung cancer from the fifties and reads them as the first thing in the medical literature that looks like the kind of argument he would make himself.

On Wednesday the 7th Hagrid comes. Dumbledore brings him as far as Mrs Figg’s by a means Harry is not shown, and Hagrid walks round from Wisteria Walk in a coat the size of a tent and fills the doorway, and Petunia, who has let a great many people through that door since June, stands in the hall with her back to the wall. Dudley is awestruck and silent for the first time in his life. Hagrid sits on the sofa, which complains, and cries, and produces rock cakes from an inside pocket, and a photograph of James Potter’s school Quidditch team with James in the middle holding a cup. He stays three hours. He talks about James and Lily at school, about Hogwarts, about the grounds and the creatures in the forest, about dragons, with the open indiscretion of a man who has never in his life kept a secret he was not specifically ordered to keep. He talks about the night. He got Harry out of the ruins, he says, before the muggles came, and he had him in his coat, and then Sirius Black turned up on that bike of his, white as a sheet, wanting to take Harry, and Hagrid had his orders from Dumbledore and would not give him up, and Black had argued and then given in and handed over the bike and said he would not be needing it any more. Harry files the phrase. It is consistent with both of the stories he is now carrying, and he notices that he wants it to be consistent with only one. Hagrid lets slip that he has no wand, and why, and that he went to school with Harry’s grandfather’s generation, and that there is a bank run by goblins under London with dragons guarding the deep vaults, or so he has heard. Harry asks him to come again and he says he will if the headmaster lets him, and Dumbledore, consulted, says once a month.

On Saturday the 17th McGonagall comes. She comes alone, by the front gate, in a tweed suit that is a better imitation of a muggle suit than Dumbledore’s was, and she does not cry. She sits upright on the edge of Vernon’s chair and looks at Harry the way she presumably looks at a first-year who has done something unusual, and asks him questions about the eight books, and he answers them, and she asks harder ones, and by the end of an hour she has stopped asking questions from the books and started asking what he thinks. He gives her the three channels: words, movement and intent, described as ingredients by every author and separated by none, and he tells her about the feather and the pace of a hand. She is silent for a while. Then she tells him that she argued with Dumbledore on the doorstep, in November 1981, about leaving him here, and that she was right and he was right and she has never settled which of them was more so, and that she would like to come once a month to talk about transfiguration, if the headmaster permits, and that she thinks he will. She tells him, before she goes, that his mother was the best student of Charms Flitwick taught in his time at the school, and that his father was the most talented student of her own subject in her twenty-seven years and the least disciplined, and stopped being the second in his last two years, later than she would have liked and sooner than she expected.

Dumbledore comes on the 24th with the account he promised. The money in the bank is in gold coins called galleons, and there are something over thirty thousand of them in a vault that Harry may draw on for his schooling and his keep, and a second vault that was his grandparents’ and will be his at seventeen. The bank gives five pounds to the galleon. Harry asks what a galleon weighs. Dumbledore does not know exactly, and gives him one, and Harry weighs it that evening on Petunia’s kitchen scales and finds that if it were gold all through it would be worth at the Mail’s gold price something over sixty pounds, not five. He asks Dumbledore about this the next time, and Dumbledore says that goblin coinage is goblin business and that his world has learned over several centuries not to ask goblins about their metal, in a tone that closes the subject. Harry files it. It is the first thing in his new world that has looked like money left on a table, and he has read enough economics to know that money left on a table is usually there for a reason, and that the reason is usually a goblin.

He asks about Sirius Black, as he said he would. Dumbledore brings, on the 24th, what the Ministry has: the Aurors’ reports from the street in London on the 1st of November 1981, the statements of the wizard witnesses, which are three, the record of the muggle witnesses having been made to forget, the order for Black’s committal signed by Bartemius Crouch, and nothing else. There is no record of Black having been asked anything by anyone. Harry reads it in four looks and spends the evening on it. The three witnesses agree that Pettigrew shouted that Black had betrayed the Potters, that there was an explosion, that Pettigrew was gone and twelve muggles were dead and Black was standing in the crater laughing. They do not agree about the wand in Black’s hand, and one of them does not mention a wand at all. Harry asks Dumbledore how he knows Black was the one who knew where the Potters were, and Dumbledore says that James told him, in October, that he meant to use Black, and Harry says, “Meant to,” and Dumbledore says, “Yes,” and does not say anything else. Harry asks whether he may write to Black. Dumbledore says that the prison is not a place letters reach, or should, and that he will not have a child’s letter opened by what guards it, and will not say more about what guards it tonight. Harry puts the probability of Black’s guilt at about two in three, and notices that he would like it lower, and makes it three in four to correct for that, and then feels that he has over-corrected, and leaves it where it is and marks it as a number he does not trust.

The first monthly summary of the owl post comes with Dumbledore on the 24th, in Dumbledore’s hand, two sides. Four hundred and eleven letters since June. Most are from strangers who wish him well. Some are from strangers who wish him to marry their daughters when the time comes, which Dumbledore has listed under a heading of its own. Several are from people who want something, and Dumbledore has listed what. Three are from people who knew his parents, and these come as letters, not summaries, as agreed: one from Bathilda Bagshot, who lived next door to them in Godric’s Hollow and is very old and writes in a hand that wanders, and tells him what the house looked like and what his mother grew in the garden; one from Augusta Longbottom, whose son and daughter-in-law fought beside his parents and are in hospital and will not come out, and whose grandson was born the day before Harry, and who writes with great formality and, under the formality, something else; and one from a woman called Molly Weasley, who did not know his parents well and says so, and writes anyway, because she has a son his age and could not think of a boy his age with nobody to write to him. There is no letter from Remus Lupin. Harry asks. Dumbledore says there is one, and that he is holding it, and that he will say when he is not.

On the last weekend of the month Mrs Figg mentions, over the cats, that she keeps the old Prophets in the attic for the litter trays, and Harry asks if he may read them, and she hesitates, because Dumbledore said no wizarding world and did not say anything about newspapers, and decides that a newspaper is not a world, and brings down two years of them in cardboard boxes: seven hundred issues, something over twenty thousand pages, which is thirty hours at his speed. He reads them over the weekend and the Monday, on her floor and then in his room. He reads the first days after the night, when his own name was on every front page and there was a day named after him; the trials through the winter of 1981 and 1982, the Lestranges and the younger Crouch and a man called Karkaroff who named names, and a man called Malfoy who said he had been under a curse and was believed; the letters pages, which are where he learns what people in his new world think about people like his mother; the appointments and the resignations and the Quidditch; and, in a letters page in December 1981, a single letter asking why Sirius Black had been sent to Azkaban without a trial when the Lestranges had been given one, and no reply to it in any later issue. He has, by Sunday night, a working model of his new world’s politics that is better than most people in it have, and he knows it is two years out of date, and he knows the names.

Dumbledore learns of the Prophets from Mrs Figg and is displeased, and says so. Harry says that he was told no people and was not told no newspapers, that he will not do a thing like that again without asking, and that he is not sorry. Dumbledore looks at him for a while and says that the Prophet will come to Harry daily from now on, through him, and that he would rather Harry read it in the present than in the past, and Harry recognises this as the first thing he has won from Dumbledore without arguing for it.

Rachel, in the second week, brings him a journal from her own field, because he asked what she actually did all day, and he reads it on the drive home and asks her, at the lights in Guildford, a question about the third paper that she cannot answer and that she takes back to her supervisor, who cannot answer it either. She stops, around then, speaking to him in the voice people use for children, which she had already mostly stopped doing, and starts bringing him the things she is stuck on. He finds that he likes being brought things.

Mr Hobday is at the branch library on Tuesdays, and Harry asks Rachel to stop there on the way back from Guildford, and she does, and he and Mr Hobday talk for half an hour about engines, and about what a Vickers drawing office was like, and about the fact that Harry has read everything about how machines work and has never held a spanner. Mr Hobday says that there is a garage behind his house with a lathe in it that has not been used since his wife died, and that he is there most afternoons, and leaves it at that.

Dr Whitlock comes on the second Wednesday of the month, as she will now do every month, and raises the question of school, because the law will require him to be in one in two years and the county has a form. Harry says that he does not want a school and does not want a tutor; he wants a library and someone to drive him to it, which he has. She says that the form will need something written on it, and that she will write it, and he does not ask what.

The trust stands at twenty-eight thousand pounds at the end of the month, which is the Mail’s money and the publisher’s advance and some television fees, less Rachel’s wages and the books. Pell wants it in a building society. Harry has read about the stock market and wants a spread of shares, and says why, and Pell says that trustees are not permitted to gamble with a child’s money, and Harry says that the building society is also a bet, on inflation, and that he has read the Economist on inflation, and they compromise on half and half, and Pell goes home and reads the Economist on inflation.

Dudley’s twenty minutes continue. He can pick out thirty words by the end of the month, and has started bringing the book to Harry’s door at four rather than waiting to be fetched. Petunia has started nursery for him three mornings a week.

Vernon has been given a rise at Grunnings, which he attributes to the quality of his work and which Harry, who has read the business pages, attributes to his photograph having been in the Daily Mail. Vernon says “our Harry” twice more and does not hear himself either time.

The wandless practice moves to the page. By the end of the month he can turn a page three times in four, and has begun on two other things from the books that seemed to him basic: a light, which he can raise as a glow at the end of one finger about one time in twenty, and a warmth, which he can put into a cup of water about one time in ten. He is careful with the warmth, because water is not a feather. He has started a second list beside the table, of the effects in the books that seem to him to be made of nothing simpler, and the effects that seem to him to be made of those. It is a short list and he does not trust it yet.

In the wider world, a Korean airliner is shot down on the 1st and the Mail is full of it for a week. He reads the coverage of the Soviet response with the Prophet’s trials beside it and notices that both papers are better at anger than at explanation.

And there is everything else, because twelve hours of reading a day at his speed leaves a great deal of mind unused, and he has decided that unused mind is the one thing he will not tolerate.

He starts a language a month, beginning on the 5th with Latin, because the spells are in it, and by the 30th he reads it. With Latin he begins the etymology of the sixty-one spells in the first book: forty are Latin or near it with a meaning that matches the effect, twelve are Latin corrupted past easy recognition, and nine are nothing he can place, and the four with named inventors are all among the forty. He writes this up as a ten-page note and gives it to McGonagall, who takes it to the Charms master, Flitwick, who is a scholar of incantations and writes back through her in October with two corrections and a request for more.

He starts the correspondence. Pell has bought the recorder for muggle matters and Rachel types what comes off it, and from the second week he dictates letters for an hour a day: to the World Health Organisation in Geneva asking for the tables behind the grey report; to the secretariat of the Brandt commission; to three mathematicians whose problems he has read; to the editor of the Lancet, with a letter about the salt solution that is not printed until October; to the author of the psychology textbook, about the chapter on children above a certain score, which the author answers in some confusion. Twenty letters a week go out. He finds that letters are the one form of output in which the slowness of his hands and voice does not matter, because the other person is slower still, and he resolves to use them for everything that can be done by someone else somewhere else.

He starts the inventions notebook, which is a cassette. The first entry is a mechanical page-turner for his own use, because he can turn a page by looking at it now and cannot do so in a library in front of Rachel. The second is a stair gate that a child cannot open and an adult can with one hand. The third is a note about drill chucks that he does not develop for a year. He writes to Dr Whitlock a six-page analysis of the study of children above a certain score, what the data shows and what the textbook claimed it showed, and she sends it to Lowry, who sends it to the author. He asks McGonagall whether the school keeps a table of which spells its students fail most often, and she says that no such table exists, and he asks whether one could, and she says she will see. He takes the Prophet’s advertisements and works out a price index for his new world and an estimate of its size from the number of shops and the frequency of situations vacant, and the size is something over a thousand households, perhaps three thousand people, which fits what Dumbledore told him, and the prices are strange in ways he notes and does not yet understand. He reads all of Shakespeare in a week and the English poets in another and begins a habit, kept for life, of one poem in the going-through each night. He does his first paid work: a firm in Guildford that is a client of Pell’s wants to know whether to enter a market, and Pell, uneasy, puts the question to Harry without saying who will answer it, and Harry reads the industry in an evening and answers it in two pages, and the firm pays five hundred pounds and never knows. He writes to Oxfam and Save the Children for their accounts, and reads them when they come, and makes notes. He begins running round the garden for ten minutes a day, before any plan requires it, because he has read what happens to bodies that do not.

Chapter 11 takes this month: Hagrid, McGonagall, the galleon on the kitchen scales, the file with no questions in it, the three letters, and the attic.

October 1983. Three years two to three months.

The reading goes on at twelve hours a day and begins, this month, to run ahead of the supply. With the page turning itself nine times in ten by the end of the month, a page takes him five seconds and the turning takes him almost nothing, and he gets through something over twenty books on a full day. Rachel’s crate from the branch library is not enough. The trust starts buying, in bulk, from a wholesaler in London that Pell finds, and the boxes come on Thursdays, and Petunia stops remarking on them. Rachel asks, in the second week, whether she can drop to half-time on her doctorate and do this three days instead of two, and the trust pays her properly for the first time, and she tells her supervisor, who has read the papers and does not argue.

In physics he has finished the Landau volumes that exist in English and started on the journals, and has begun to see the shape of what is not known, which is a different kind of reading from the shape of what is. In mathematics he has gone through algebra and is in topology, and on the second Tuesday he writes out, in his head, a proof of something in a problem book that the problem book had marked as open, and sits with it for an hour of the house’s time looking for the mistake, and does not find one, and dictates it to Rachel that evening, and asks her to give it to someone in the mathematics department without saying who it is from. She does. He does not hear anything for three weeks. In biology he has started on genetics proper and on the papers about the new methods for reading the code, which the Economist has been excited about, and he reads the methods papers with particular care because they are the first thing in biology that looks to him like engineering. In economics he reads the Bank of England’s quarterly bulletins, which Pell gets him, and begins to understand what the people who run things believe, as distinct from what the textbooks say. In history the nineteenth century is finished and he goes back to the beginning, with the Greeks, because he has realised that he has been reading history from the wrong end.

McGonagall’s first session is on Saturday the 1st. She brings her own notes on the theory of transfiguration, forty years of them, and reads him passages, and he asks her questions, and by the end of the afternoon she is making notes on his. He asks her where the limits are: what cannot be transfigured into what, and why, and whether anyone has mapped it. She tells him about the exceptions he has read of and about several he has not, and tells him, when he presses, that there is a department of the Ministry whose business is exactly this kind of question and that very little of what it finds comes out, and that he is asking the questions that department asks. He files the department. She comes again on the 29th.

Hagrid comes on Wednesday the 12th with a photograph album he has been putting together since June. He has written to everyone he could think of who might have a picture of James and Lily, and most of them have sent one. There are the two of them at school, in robes, on a lawn with a lake behind. There is their wedding, under a white arch, with a tall dark man beside James laughing at the camera, and Harry knows him, because he has known him since the night and has had the name since August, and he looks at Sirius Black’s face for a long time while Hagrid talks. There is a group of four young men in a garden: James and Sirius, a thin tired-looking one who must be Lupin, and a small round one with watery eyes who must be Pettigrew. Harry has Pettigrew’s face now. He has all of their faces. He asks Hagrid about each of them and Hagrid tells him what he knows, which is a good deal about James and Sirius and less about the other two, and says of Pettigrew that he always tagged along, and of Lupin that he was ill a great deal and nobody said why.

Harry dictates a letter to Dumbledore on the 15th. It is fourteen numbered points about the file, and he has had them since the 24th of September and has waited three weeks to be sure they are the same points on a Tuesday as on a Saturday. They include the wand that one witness did not see; the fact that nobody in the file says where Pettigrew’s body went, apart from a finger; the fact that the explosion that killed twelve people is attributed to Black’s curse and nobody describes the curse; the words meant to; and the absence, in a file about a man who betrayed his closest friends to a murderer, of any sentence in which anyone asked him why. Dumbledore’s reply comes by owl on the 20th, to Mrs Figg’s, and Mrs Figg brings it round. It is one side. It says that Dumbledore has read the fourteen points and that several of them are good, and that he does not have answers to them. It says that he did not look as hard as he should have, in November 1981, because he was grieving and because every alternative to Black’s guilt was worse. It says that he is not proud of this, and that he does not say it to excuse it. Harry reads the letter a great many times. The probability moves, not because the letter says anything about Black, but because it says something about the man who put him in prison without asking, and Harry had been treating that man’s judgement as evidence.

The owl summary for October lists three hundred and six letters. Among them is one from a man called Ollivander, which Dumbledore forwards without comment and which says, in its entirety, that the writer measured Harry’s father’s wand arm in 1971 and his mother’s the same year and looks forward to measuring his, and gives no address. There is one from Lucius Malfoy, on heavy paper, which congratulates Harry Potter on his remarkable gifts and offers the House of Malfoy’s assistance in any matter in which it might be of use, and Dumbledore has included it in the summary rather than forwarding it, with a note that says you will have read of this man in Mrs Figg’s attic. Harry has. He understands the offer, and understands that Dumbledore wants him to understand it, and understands also that Dumbledore wanted him to see it rather than be protected from it, which he thinks is a kind of respect.
