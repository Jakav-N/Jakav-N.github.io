# Story bible

Working title: *Harry Potter and the Speed of Thought* (placeholder until the author picks one).

Hundred chapters of about 3,000 words. Roughly 35 before Hogwarts. Close third person on Harry, with short titled interludes from other points of view. Written in the register of *Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality*: reasoning shown as reasoning, dialogue that argues, a dry narrator, understatement, comedy from the gap between how Harry's mind works and how everyone expects a child's to.

This file holds what must stay fixed. The block outlines (`01-10.md` and so on) hold what happens month by month. When something here is superseded, replace it; do not annotate it.

---

## 1. The premise, exactly

Everything is as in canon up to the night of 31 October 1981, except the rules of magic in section 4.

That night, the combination of Lily's sacrifice and the rebounding Killing Curse left Harry's brain running about two hundred times faster than a human brain runs. Nothing else about him changed. It cannot be replicated, cannot be undone, and does not fade. It is the one unrealistic thing in the story; everything downstream of it is worked out as honestly as possible.

What "the brain only" means in practice:

- **Thinking, perceiving, remembering, deciding** run at 200x. Subjectively, the world moves at one two-hundredth of its speed.
- **The body is normal.** Muscles, bones, growth, hormones, digestion, the inner ear, the retina's chemistry, the cochlea's mechanics, the spinal reflexes: all on body time. He is a small child with a small child's body, and later an eleven-year-old with an eleven-year-old's.
- **Reaction time** is better than normal but not superhuman: roughly 150 ms where an adult's is 250, because most of a reaction is transduction and muscle, not thought. He is very hard to surprise and only slightly hard to hit.
- **Eyes and reading.** Saccades are a motor act and the retina needs its integration time, so the eyes can take in perhaps five or six still pictures a second and no more. Each picture is a fixation: a clear patch a few centimetres across at reading distance, blurring outward. He does not read along a line. Each fixation is a photograph that he keeps, and he takes enough of them to cover the page, in a grid rather than in rows, and then reads the assembled page from memory, at whatever pace he likes. Where fixations overlap, the blurred letters at the edges of one are sharp in the next, and context does the rest. The costs are the number of fixations a page needs (perhaps twenty to forty for a page of ordinary print, fewer if he holds the book further off, which costs acuity, and which his mild short sight makes worse until he gets glasses in June 1983); page turns, which a small child's hands do in about a second; light; and getting hold of the book at all. Working estimate: a paperback page in five to eight seconds, a three-hundred-page book in half an hour to forty minutes, roughly fifteen hundred to twenty-five hundred words a minute of real time, climbing toward the upper figure with glasses and practice. This is the least certain number in the bible. The story only needs a page to take him seconds and a book to take him under an hour. What it feels like to him: a few seconds of looking, each fixation a long frozen stare at a patch of print; then the page is in, and reading it is thinking, as fast as thinking. The bottleneck is turning pages and getting books, which is why the page-turning charm in 1983 and the page-reading spell in 1985 matter so much. To anyone watching, his eyes do not move like a reader's. They hop about the page in a pattern, and he turns the page after a few seconds. Mrs Ashworth notices this in May 1983, before she notices anything else.
- **Hearing**: pitch is normal (the cochlea does the frequency work); rhythm is stretched. A syllable lasts most of a subjective minute, a sentence a quarter of a subjective hour. Speech, to him, is a slow tide of sound with a shape he can study. It is the richest input he has for the first two years.
- **Motor control** had to be relearned in the first weeks (his internal clocks ran 200x against a body that did not), and after that he moves well, because he can plan every movement and watch it happen. Skill acquisition is faster than normal per attempt but attempts happen on body time: perhaps three to five times a normal child's rate, no more, and his hands are still a child's hands.
- **Output is the scarcest thing he has.** Speech is about 150 words a minute, roughly half a percent of his inner speech. A child's handwriting is a few words a minute. He never writes anything down for himself; he remembers it. He writes or speaks only for other people, and he compresses. From mid-1983 his default output is dictation: muggle matters onto a cassette recorder and typed in Guildford; anything that touches magic onto the dictation quill Dumbledore brings with the first trunk in June 1983, which writes what is said to it onto parchment that stays in the locked trunk. No muggle ever types or reads a word about magic, and the Hogwarts books go into the trunk whenever Rachel or anyone else is in the room. The ink telekinesis that solves this comes much later (1985 to 1986).
- **Sleep** is on body time. He sleeps the hours a child his age sleeps. Dreams are long and mostly unrecalled. Subjectively a night is a skip, which in the first years is a mercy and later the thing he most resents.
- **He never re-reads for content.** Once a page is in, it is in, and the nightly going-through fixes it. A second pass is in his head. He looks at a physical page again only for the thing itself, the handwriting or the photograph, and the text says so when he does. "He read them again, slowly" was a fault of the first draft of chapter 9.
- **Pain** lasts two hundred times longer. Teething, fever, the healing scar. He learns attention control very early because he has to, and it becomes one of his most useful skills.
- **Emotion**: hormones arrive on body time, thought on brain time. He can think all the way round a feeling before the feeling has finished arriving. He still has the feeling. Toddler affect with adult-speed reflection is the engine of the early chapters' comedy and their sadness.
- **Memory**: infant memory until about his second birthday, with one exception: the night, and the doorstep conversation, which he replayed so constantly that they survived. In July 1982 he discovers deliberate replay (going through the day each night fixes it) and from then on retains everything he hears or reads. Assume no capacity limit, ever.
- **Mathematics in the body hour.** From August 1984 he carries mathematics into the garden in his head: hard formal texts with no padding and difficult exercises, worked while he runs or climbs, because mathematics compresses further than any other subject and is the one he can carry whole. It is the subject in which his speed counts for most.
- **Boredom** is the first enemy. The world is a still photograph most of the time. The only things that move at a pace he finds natural are flies, dripping taps, snowflakes, the second hand, bees, smoke, the rise of a firework. He develops an eye for slow beauty.

### The arithmetic, for reference

| Real time | Subjective time |
|---|---|
| 1 second | 3 minutes 20 seconds |
| 1 minute | 3 hours 20 minutes |
| 1 hour | 8 days 8 hours |
| 1 waking day (14 h) | about 4 months |
| 1 week | about 2 years 3 months (waking) |
| 1 month | about 10 years |
| 1 year | about 120 years (waking) |

How he measures it: his only external anchor is a clock, so the number is a count. He counts at the pace people count aloud and gets a little over two hundred counts while the kitchen clock's sweep second hand travels from one mark to the next, the same every time. The unit is his own speaking-pace second, so "about two hundred" is as exact as the number honestly gets, and he says so when asked. He never says "three minutes of mine" or the like without saying what he is counting.

Do not quote these numbers in the prose more than once or twice in the whole story. The narrator gives the idea early and then trusts the reader.

## Numbers to keep straight

**Reading.** Five or six fixations a second; twenty to forty per page of ordinary print. About ten seconds a page before the glasses (April to June 1983), about five with them (from 28 June 1983). Turning a page by hand takes a small child a second and a half; by magic, from October 1983, next to nothing. So roughly seven hundred pages an hour, eight thousand in a twelve-hour day, twenty-five books of ordinary length a day, nine thousand a year. By the wand in August 1985 he has read something like fifteen thousand books and owns about five thousand. The eyes are the wall, and the page-reading spell of 1985 is what removes it.

**Going-through.** The nightly review is a review, not a re-living: he goes through the day at a summary pace, two or three subjective hours for a full day, a real minute or two. It fixes everything. It never runs out of night.

**Output.** Speech and dictation at 150 words a minute, so an hour of dictation is 9,000 words. The reading book, 31,000 words, took three evenings. Letters: twenty a week in September 1983, about seven a day by 1984, 2,400 in 1984, 1,600 correspondents by mid-1985. Handwriting legible by autumn 1984; typing thirty words a minute by mid-1985.

**Languages.** One a month from September 1983: Latin, Greek, French, Russian, German, Italian and Spanish together, Hebrew and Aramaic together, Japanese (two months), Arabic, Chinese (four months), Dutch, Portuguese, Swedish, Hindi. Fifteen by 1985.

**Books and papers.** Grandad Evans's encyclopaedia: twelve volumes, about 9,600 pages, thirty hours at April 1983 speed. The eight Hogwarts first-year books: about 2,500 pages, an evening. Two years of the Prophet from Mrs Figg's attic: seven hundred issues, twenty thousand pages, thirty hours, a long weekend. A decade of the Hogwarts Prophet archive: 140,000 pages, two hundred hours, an hour a day for a month. The Economist: eight minutes.

**Money (the trust).** September 1983 £28,000. December 1983 £45,000. March 1984 £95,000 (first royalties £31,000). June 1984 £135,000. September 1984 £180,000. December 1984 £260,000 with the program's first payment of £260,000 due in January. January 1985 £540,000. March 1985 £720,000. June 1985 a little over £1,000,000. The giving account has given £196,000 by July 1985. Income streams in order of appearance: the Mail's £8,000 (June 1983, 60/40); the reading book (£15,000 advance, then royalties); television fees; paid analysis for Pell's clients (£500 to £1,000 a job, two a month from 1984); the numbers book; the Observer, monthly; Radio 4, monthly; the medicine-cap licence (granted October 1984, licensed December); the reading program (60,000 copies by Christmas 1984 at £9.95, the company's share about £260,000); the chuck licence (3 per cent of each drill, small at first); the second and third programs; unsigned pieces for the Economist.

**Wizarding Britain.** About three thousand witches and wizards, which is Dumbledore's figure and fits Harry's estimate from the Prophet's advertisements of something over a thousand households. Hogwarts about 280 pupils, forty a year; a first-year class is two houses together, about twenty. The Wizengamot about fifty seats. The Daily Prophet reaches nearly every household. Diagon Alley is a few dozen shops. The Potter trust vault holds about 30,000 galleons; at the bank's rate of five pounds to the galleon that is £150,000, and at the weight of the coin and the Mail's gold price it would be twelve times that, which is the question Harry has been told not to be clever about.

**The galleon.** About the weight of a sovereign, a little under seven grams; if gold throughout, about sixty pounds at 1983 prices; the bank gives five.

**People who know about the speed.** Harry; Dumbledore (21 June 1983); Mr Hobday (March 1985, asked directly from his sickbed). Dr Whitlock asked once and let it go. Rachel has not asked.

## 3. Harry

**Character.** Curious, patient because he has had to be, warm toward individuals and clear-eyed about them. Not a cynic. Not a wit for its own sake; funny mostly by being literal. He wants things badly and plans for them calmly. He is not angry at the Dursleys; he has had too long to think about them for anger to survive, and he understands them better than they understand themselves. He misses his mother in a way he has made room for rather than got over.

**The decision.** From about his third birthday (the week of 13 to 20 June 1983, reading twelve hours a day while the world's press waits in the hall) he concludes that what matters to him is that things go well for people, that very little in the world is arranged to make that happen, and that he has a resource no one else has. He does not call it anything. He does not announce it. It shows in what he chooses.

**Rules he adopts, and why.** He has read enough history by June 1983 to know how people with unusual power go wrong, and he picks cheap insurance:

- He will not lie. He will decline, say "I can't discuss that," or stay silent. He decides in June 1983 that deliberately performing below his ability in order to mislead is a lie in all but form, and stops doing it (he had been talking at Dudley's level in front of adults since March 1982; that ends with the reveal).
- He will not steal, and where he must take he makes restitution that exceeds the taking.
- He will not kill, and will not harm people. Harm to interests only with informed consent or as a side effect he has tried to minimise and accounts for honestly.
- Laws bind him when they are good. When they are not, he still counts the cost of breaking them truthfully, including the cost to trust in laws generally.
- The speed stays private. Dumbledore learns it on 21 June 1983. A handful of others later, each for a reason. His capabilities in general are not hidden; hiding them would cost more than it saves.

**How he finds things out.** By reading, at scale, and inferring: records, archives, division lists, accounts, catalogues, back numbers. People do not hand him the key fact. When a person does tell him something that matters, it is in character for that person to be indiscreet (Hagrid), to confess a piece at a time over months (Petunia), or to answer a direct question within limits they keep (Dumbledore), and the fact is usually one he could have found, or then does find, in a document. Nobody conveniently volunteers. In the first draft of chapter 10 Dumbledore said "there was no trial"; now he promises "what the Ministry has," and Harry learns there was no trial from an eleven-page file with no questions in it and a letter on a Prophet letters page that nobody answered.

**Method.** He thinks like a Bayesian who knows Bayesian methods are rarely tractable: he keeps rough probabilities, updates on evidence, notices when a sample is three observations, and distrusts his own confidence in proportion to how much he wants a thing. He understands statistics deeply from 1983 on. None of this is lectured. It is visible in how he decides, in what he counts, and in the occasional dry aside from the narrator.

**Speech.** From the reveal onward he speaks as he thinks, in a three-year-old's voice. Adults find it uncanny for about a minute and then forget to. He compresses. He asks questions in the order that gets the most information soonest. He does not fill silence.

**What he never does.** Write for himself. Pretend. Waste a real minute if a subjective day can be spent instead. Hold a grudge long enough for it to matter.

## 4. The magic system (author's rules; these override canon where they differ)

**Primitives.** There is a fixed set of fundamental magical effects. No one has created a new one in living memory or recorded history; nobody can prove it is impossible, but no one has done it. The same holds for potion primitives, where some development is still possible at the level of recipes and interactions.

**Spells** are bindings of an incantation and a wand movement to a primitive or a combination of primitives. Most common spells are ancient bindings whose makers are unknown. About one in ten commonly used spells was invented by a known person. Inventing a spell means making a new binding; it is very difficult and requires some real connection, felt or symbolic, between the effect and the words and motion you bind it to. Complicated spells are built like programs out of primitives, and like programs they have bugs. Spell creation is dangerous for that reason. Combination is possible, rare, and underused.

**Warding** is attaching spells to things: objects and places easily, people with difficulty, and other referents besides (times, conditions, relations).

**Rituals** exist, akin to spells and potions, and rarely come up.

**Intent and mind matter.** All spells and potions are shaped by the caster's intent and state of mind. Transfiguration (no incantation or set movement, though a wand is used) and the Patronus are the clearest cases.

**Wandless and nonverbal magic** work by visualising the effect well enough that magic reads the visualisation as a spell. Because Harry visualises at 200x, he will be far better at this than anyone, once he knows what he is doing. Limits: beyond a certain speed magic stops reading a visualisation as a spell, so he can cast wandlessly at most about twice as fast as the fastest ordinary casting; and wandless casting is weaker and less precise than wanded. He discovers the speed ceiling in August 1983, before he knows why it is there.

**Accidental magic** is this same mechanism in children: want plus a vivid enough picture. Harry does not lose it with age. His early incidents are listed in the block outline. Before 21 June 1983 he has no word for it and the narrator describes it rather than naming it.

**Gamp's law.** No primitive can create food, create gold, or reverse ageing. Alchemy can get around Gamp's law and is close to impossible.

**The Trace** is laid with the Hogwarts letter. Harry has none until July 1991. His childhood magic is undetected by the Ministry; it is seen only by the Dursleys, later by Dumbledore's wards.

**Dementors** feed on suffering and the soul; permanent harm requires direct contact. The Patronus, cast from a happy memory, is a shield. Cast with the right intent by someone genuinely good beyond almost anyone, and centred on the destruction of evil and the increase of good, it can destroy them.

**Practical spells.** There are many useful spells that never appear in canon. To find them, think about what limits wizards face (time, distance, attention, labour, information) and what a competent people would have built.

**Things Harry notices about this system when he first reads the textbooks (June to August 1983)**, which the later outlines build on: the field has no theory, only recipes; the inventory of spells is treated as a given; nobody has catalogued primitives or asked what the full set is; incantations have etymologies that might carry information; wand motion, incantation and intent are three channels that could be studied separately; wards are a programming model nobody has written down; nobody keeps data.

**Occlumency.** Taught by Dumbledore every Saturday from the 8th of September 1984 and done in seven lessons, by the 27th of October. Harry can build a surface in a week, a room with a reading child in it and a garden in the window, and cannot test it alone, because the only practice is a Legilimens reaching for what is behind it. Each probe finds a new way through: a question asked aloud about his mother's voice, waiting until his attention drifts, a sharp sound, a question about the table. He mends each in an evening and finds out the next Saturday whether the mending holds, which is why seven lessons do what seventy usually do not. On the sixth Dumbledore uses his whole force and is through, and Harry says, "That is what he would do." On the seventh it holds against everything. Dumbledore tests it afterwards without warning, and it holds, and he says he thinks Voldemort would not see what Harry is and is not certain. The river itself, too much and too fast to read, is a kind of defence, and Dumbledore says plainly that it is not safety, because a looker who cannot read a river can still see that it is one.

**What Dumbledore knows and withholds.** Knows the speed from 21 June 1983, infers it came from the night, and wonders whether it is "the power the Dark Lord knows not." Suspects something about the scar; does not know what. Tells Harry nothing of the prophecy. Promises not to lie to him and says plainly that he will not tell him everything; agrees to try to say when he is withholding. Limits Harry's contact with the wizarding world until he judges him mature (August 1985): no wand, no Diagon Alley, visitors only by his arrangement (Hagrid in September 1983, McGonagall the same month).

## 6. Style rules

Two reference texts, and the register sits between them: *Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality* for the shape of the reasoning and the arguing dialogue, and *Mother of Learning* for the plainness of the prose and the closeness to the character's own practical voice. When in doubt, lean toward the second. The author's standing instruction is that it should read like a person wrote it.

What the register is:

- **Names.** The narrator knows who everyone is even when Harry does not, and uses the names: Petunia, Vernon, Dudley, Hagrid, Mrs Figg, the television, the Hoover, the encyclopaedia. A description stands in for a name only when the description is the point of the sentence, and never as a running substitute ("the round child," "the box," "the giant" were faults of the first drafts).
- **Plain diction.** Say the thing. Narration may use contractions where a person would, and Harry's own questions to himself. Abstractions about "the shape of things" and sentences that end on a soft reversal ("as if he had all night, and did not") are the drift to watch for; the last paragraphs of the first draft of chapter 10 had it. A figure of speech is rare and concrete (a hand that sets off like a boat leaving harbour), never decorative. No lyrical cadences, no sentences built for sound.
- **Questions people can answer.** Harry frames every question so that the person asked can understand it from what they know. He does not refer to a thing by a private name, or ask about an event the listener has not been told of, without first saying what happened. ("What did I do to the bottle?" was a fault of the first draft of chapter 8.)
- **The character's own voice.** Thought is rendered in free indirect style, in Harry's idiom, including the questions he asks himself and his casual reactions. Casual beats ("Oh well." "Huh." "That was useful to know.") are fine. Portentous fragments ("Something shifted.") are not. In chapters 1 and 2 he is pre-verbal and the narrator has to supply the words; from chapter 3 he has them, and from chapter 4 he thinks in full sentences and the narration should lean on that heavily.
- **Reasoning laid out as reasoning.** The puzzle, the candidate explanations, what would tell them apart, what he will do next. His hedges are his own ("probably," "he couldn't be sure"). Conclusions are marked plainly. Not announced ("Harry reasoned that"), not summarised afterwards.
- **Summaries say the thing.** When a scene or chapter takes stock, it names what it is taking stock of: he cried; the speed; his mother's protection; the Killing Curse. No "a thing that had never happened before, to anyone" for the speed, no "his body did what a body that age does" for crying. The end of the first draft of chapter 8 was the fault.
- **Summary in plain voice.** Weeks can pass in a sentence ("A month went by with little to show for it"), as long as the sentence is specific.
- **Dialogue carries scenes** whenever there is anyone to talk to. People answer the question asked, or visibly do not. Tags are plain ("said"). Small comic misalignments between what people mean and what they hear. Double quotes.
- **Minimal narrator.** No aphorisms about the human condition, no "it is worth bearing in mind," no stepping outside the story to comment on it. One wry observation per scene at most, and it should be one Harry could have made.
- **Paragraphs vary.** Short ones are reactions or turns, not drama.
- **One clock.** There is one clock, the world's, and durations are given on it. Harry does not have a clock of his own, and the prose never says "by either clock" or treats his experience as a second timepiece. When how long a stretch was for him matters, say what he did or thought in it, or give the comparison once ("a real second, which was long enough to go through it twice"); use that sparingly. In real time he is never slower than a person. He takes in a room in seconds, a page in seconds, a conversation as it is spoken; what takes him time is waiting for the world.
- **Understatement.** The sad thing is said once and left. Emotion is practical ("Being repeatedly stabbed in the chest hurts").
- British vocabulary and spelling for the setting (nappy, cot, pushchair, jumper, lavatory).

Avoid, every time:

- Short clauses chained with "and" for rhythm. This was the main fault of the first drafts of chapters 1 and 2; one "and" joins two things, and a third thing gets its own sentence.
- Stating the obvious, or restating what a scene has shown.
- "It wasn't X. It was Y." and its relatives.
- Fragment sentences for punch; paragraph-ending one-liners more than once a chapter.
- "Something shifted." "Suddenly." "A testament to." "In that moment."
- Reflexive tricolons. Rhetorical questions in narration (Harry may ask himself questions; the narrator may not ask the reader any). Present-participle tails ("he said, his voice breaking").
- Coining a term, or capitalising one of Harry's private concepts into a Name. Ask the author first if a coinage seems needed.
- Overloading an existing word with a special meaning it does not have.
- Emphasis everywhere; italics for stress more than two or three times a chapter. Therapy-speak. Characters explaining their own feelings in abstractions.
- Scenes that end by stating their theme.
- Anything in the neighbourhood of computer security. Harry's later software work is described at the level of "wrote a program for" and "sold it."
- Harry writing anything down for his own benefit. He dictates for others, or remembers.

## 7. Working rules for the outlines and chapters

- Outlines are written in ordinary sentences, with names, the way the chapters are. Compressed notation, headings used as sentences, and semicolon lists train the wrong habits and they show up in the prose.
- Outlines come in blocks of ten chapters. Write the months first, without reference to chapter boundaries, each with a week-by-week block of what Harry is working on across every front he has; then derive the chapter map and beat sheets from the months and put them after the months in the file. The months should hold far more than the chapters can.
- Each month entry records: his age and state; what he is working on (by project); dated events; the Dursleys; Mrs Figg and the wizarding side; what the muggle world is doing that reaches him; what the chapters take from it.
- Chapters are about 3,000 words. Write no more than two chapters ahead of the author's reading position unless told otherwise.
- When the author redirects, update the outlines to match and delete what was superseded. No notes about what is not being done.
- Keep the first appearance of every named original character in the block outline, with the date.
