# Chapter 2: Three Observations

It took him ten days to get his hands back.

The problem was not weakness. His arms did what they had always done; they just did it when they were ready, and what they were ready for had nothing to do with what he wanted. He would look at a cup and send his hand toward it. The hand would wait, as if listening for something, then go. He would watch it cross the tray at the pace of a cloud. By the time it got there he had sent it three more instructions, and it would try to carry out all of them at once. Things fell. He learned to crawl again before he learned to walk again, and he learned both the same way, by finding out that his body was a slow animal that had to be told one thing at a time and then left alone to do it. Decide, send, wait, watch. By the end of November he could cross the kitchen without falling. By Christmas he walked better than he ever had, because he could plan every step and watch every step land, and nobody in the house noticed, because nobody in the house was watching.

* * *

The sounds were the real work, and he had known from the first night that they would be.

The house's own noises he had sorted in a few days. The boiler had a sequence, and the sequence meant warmth was coming, though it came, by his reckoning, about half a day after the first click. The Hoover was simply dreadful. These were easy because they did not mean anything; they were the weather of the house.

Voices were different. Voices meant, and he knew they meant, because his mother's had. He had come to the house with perhaps fifty sounds he recognised and five he could make, and for the first fortnight he could not find his fifty anywhere in the sharper, quicker ones Petunia made at Dudley. But a sound that is said a hundred times a day will come out of any noise eventually. *Dudley* was the first. He had it inside a week: it was what Petunia said whenever Dudley did anything at all, and it came with a face. *No* was the second. It came with a different face, and was mostly said to Harry. After that they came faster, because he had worked out what he was listening for, which was not the sounds themselves but the places where they came back. A sentence that took Petunia a second and a half to say took him a quarter of an hour to hear. In that quarter of an hour he could hold the whole of it, turn it over, lay it beside the last one, and find the piece that was the same. He had not been given cleverness. He had been given time, in a quantity nobody has ever had, and he spent it on the one thing in the house that moved.

The television in the front room, he worked out in December, talked in the same system. It talked more clearly than Petunia did, and about the pictures it showed. The newsreader who talked for half an hour each evening about things that were not in the room became the clearest voice in Harry's life. Vernon listened to him too, and sometimes said the same words back to the television, loudly, in a tone that was easy to learn from.

* * *

The bottle happened in the first week of December.

Dudley had a cold. Petunia had been upstairs with him most of the morning, and Harry had not been fed. He was hungry in the way a child of sixteen months is hungry, which is to say that the hunger was not a feeling he had but a place he was, and from inside it he could see the bottle. It was on the kitchen table, made up and ready, where Petunia had put it down when Dudley started crying. It was perhaps four feet from the cot. He had nothing to do but look at it and nothing else to want, and because he had a great deal of time, he did more than want. He had it. He had it in his hands: the weight of it, the give of the teat, the warmth of the milk through the plastic. He could feel his fingers round it. He held the feeling, and went on holding it, for a stretch that would have been half a minute if anyone had been counting and was, for him, an hour and a half of holding a bottle that was four feet away.

The bottle moved. It slid rather than flew, across the cloth, in a straight line and quite slowly even by the standards of the house. When it reached the edge of the table it tipped and dropped and landed in the cot beside his foot. He picked it up. He was drinking from it when Petunia came in.

She had seen the end of the slide from the doorway. He knew this because her face did the thing it had done on the doorstep, all of it at once. Then she made the climbing sound again, and after that the day went wrong in a way he did not understand. He was not fed again until evening. Vernon came home and was told something at the door in a low fast voice. Then he came and stood over the cot, and did not shout. The quiet voice went on for a long time. Harry lay and listened to it and did not know what he had done. He knew only the order things had happened in, and he kept the order, because keeping things was what he did now. He had wanted the bottle. He had held the bottle, in his mind, exactly. The bottle had come. Petunia had been afraid.

He did not know what to make of that. He was sixteen months old. He put it beside the other things he did not understand, and waited for more.

* * *

Petunia took him somewhere in the middle of December, for the first time. It was two streets away, a house that smelled all through of something green boiled for a long time. It belonged to Mrs Figg, who had wild grey hair and a cardigan with cats on it, and there were cats. There were four, and they looked at him. This was the thing he would remember. Nobody at the Dursleys' looked at him. They looked near him, or through him, or at the place he had been. The cats looked at him with their whole heads, the way he looked at things, and one of them, a big grey one with a bent ear called Mr Tibbles, got down from the table, came over, sat down in front of him, and looked some more.

Mrs Figg talked the whole time. She had been left with him, he would learn much later, on the understanding that she would not be kind to him, and she had arrived at a compromise, which was to be as boring as she could manage. What she had chosen to be boring with was a pile of photograph albums. Every photograph was of a cat, and she read him the words under each one. "Mr Tibbles on the garden wall, nineteen seventy-eight. Mr Tibbles asleep. Snowy with the new collar. Snowy, that's the one by your foot, dear, Snowy with the collar she didn't like." It went on for an hour. He had never been talked to for an hour. He sat with Mr Tibbles against his leg and the pulse of the cat going through him, slow and deep, and when Petunia came to take him away he found that he did not want to go, and that he had no way at all of saying so.

It snowed on the way home. He saw it from the pushchair: each flake coming down past the hood at the pace of a feather, the whole air full of them, the street gone white and silent. He watched one flake all the way from the top of the hood to the ground, which took him about a minute, and then he watched another. Nobody had ever given him time to look at anything properly before. Petunia pushed him home through it and did not know.

* * *

Vernon's sister Marge came for Christmas. She was wider than he was. She brought a bulldog called Ripper, and she looked at Harry across the lunch table in a way that had nothing in common with the cats.

"What's that one, then?"

"Petunia's sister's," Vernon said, and his voice did something Harry had not heard it do before. It went flat and quick, as if stepping over something. "Car crash."

Petunia, beside Harry, went stiff. He felt it through the high chair, and he kept the two sounds, *car* and *crash*, with the stiffness attached to them, in the place where he kept things that had happened and had not been explained. The pile there was growing.

* * *

The television happened in February.

He had been moved, for the daytimes, to a playpen in the front room, where Petunia could see him while she did whatever she did, and from the playpen he could see the television. It was off. Dudley was asleep upstairs. Petunia was upstairs too, with the Hoover, and the house was full of its noise. The television sat there dark and said nothing. He wanted it on.

He had learned, by then, what wanting was for. He wanted it the way he had wanted the bottle, from inside, with the whole of himself, and he did not stop at wanting. He saw it. He saw the note the television made when it came on, the grey light that came before the picture, the picture itself. He held the whole of it, exactly, for what would have been half a minute of anyone else's time. The television came on.

It came on at full volume, on the newsreader, in the middle of a word. Upstairs the Hoover stopped. Petunia came down the stairs faster than he had ever seen her move, which was nearly the speed of a person, and stood in the doorway. She looked at the switch on the front of the set. She looked at the stairs, and at him. She did not make the climbing sound this time. She did not say anything at all. She crossed the room, turned the television off, and stood with her back to it, breathing. Then she picked up the playpen with him in it, carried it into the kitchen, and put it down facing the wall, where there was nothing to want.

That night Vernon's quiet voice went on longer than it ever had, and Harry lay in the cot with two things now instead of one, and put them side by side, the way he had learned to put sounds side by side. He had wanted. He had seen the thing exactly, as if it were already so. It had been so. Petunia had been afraid. He had been looking at the thing both times, and did not know whether that mattered. He knew that two was more than one, and that he would have to wait for three.

* * *

He had been practising since the turn of the year, in the dark, an hour of the house's time each night: his mouth was a small slow thing that had only ever made five sounds, but he could hear exactly what went wrong with the sixth, send the correction, and try again. Through the wall Petunia heard a child talking to himself in a low continuous mumble, and told Vernon that it was not normal. By March he had most of the sounds, and in the middle of March Dudley, who had learned to take things, took a red block, and Harry, who had been waiting for an occasion, said, "Dudley has the red one."

He said it clearly. He had worked on *red* for a week. Petunia was at the sink with her back to them. She turned round, and a plate went out of her hand and broke on the floor at the pace of plates in that house, which was slow enough that he saw every piece decide where it was going. She looked at him. He had learned a great deal about her face by then, and he saw it do the thing it had done for the bottle and for the television, and he understood that she had put the sentence in the same place.

It was perhaps ten minutes before she had finished sweeping up, and in those ten minutes he went over everything he knew about Petunia and came to a conclusion he would later recognise as the first strategic decision of his life. Dudley said two words at a time, badly. Petunia loved Dudley. So Harry would say two words at a time, badly, when Petunia was in the room, and say what he liked when she was not.

He was nineteen months old. The decision was not clever; any child works it out eventually. What was unusual was how long he had to work it out in, how completely, and how little he was ever going to forget it.

* * *

He was out of the second cot by the end of March, and Petunia gave up on cots and put a mattress on the floor of the back room with a gate across the door. From the mattress he could reach the bottom shelf. He pulled down the first volume of the encyclopaedia. It was heavy and full of small black marks and some pictures. He could not read it, and would not be able to for a year. He looked at every page anyway over the following weeks, tearing eleven of them before his hands learned how pages turn. There was a page near the front headed ALPHABET, with twenty-six shapes under the heading, then the same twenty-six again, smaller, then rows of other shapes that were not the same at all and were, he somehow understood, meant to be. He looked at that page more than any other, without knowing why.

Spring was the Falklands war, though he would not learn its name until later. What it gave him was hours of the newsreader, with maps, ships, and a word, *killed*, that came with a list. It also gave him an observation: the newsreader said what had happened, while Vernon said back to the television what he felt about it, and the two had different shapes in the mouth. Harry learned the shapes before he learned the words for them, which were *news* and *opinion*, and never afterwards quite lost the habit of hearing which one he was being given.

* * *

The third thing was the train.

It was the end of May. Dudley, who was nearly two and had learned to hit with objects, hit him with a wooden engine. It struck him above the ear. The pain was a quarter of an hour of pain before he could get his attention off it, and in that quarter of an hour he wanted. He did not want Dudley hurt. He wanted Dudley not to be able to do that again, and because he was looking at the engine, the shape the wanting took was the engine somewhere Dudley could not reach, and he saw it there, exactly, on top of the wardrobe among the dust.

The engine was on top of the wardrobe, and Dudley looked at his empty hand and howled.

Petunia came. She looked at Dudley, at the wardrobe, which was six feet high, and at Harry, who was sitting on the floor with his hand over his ear, and he saw the calculation go across her face at a pace he could read: nobody could have put it there. He cried. He had learned how to arrange his face from watching Dudley's, and he arranged it now. It was not a lie, because his ear hurt. Petunia picked Dudley up and took him out of the room without a word, and did not look back.

That night at dinner Harry listened for the quiet voice, and it did not come. He listened instead to Petunia not telling Vernon. There was a place in the talk where it would have gone, between Dudley's cold and the price of something, and she went past it without stopping. So she was afraid of Vernon's reaction as well, then, and would sooner carry a thing than hand it to him. That was useful to know.

He had three now, and three is not many. He would come, in time, to think of three as nearly nothing, the number of coincidences anyone collects on an ordinary Tuesday. But he was not yet two, and three was what he had. He laid them side by side in the dark. Each time he had wanted from inside a feeling that filled him. Each time he had seen the thing exactly, as though it were already so. Each time it had been so. Each time Petunia had been afraid rather than angry, which was different, and he had noticed it. He did not know what it meant. He knew that he would not try to make it happen again inside this house unless he had to, because of the fear, and he knew that he wanted very much to find out what it was.

* * *

In June Dudley turned two. There was a party, and Harry was sent to Mrs Figg's so that the day would not be spoiled. He was glad to go. Mrs Figg had the albums out before he was through the door, and he sat with Mr Tibbles on his legs and looked at the pages with her, and something happened that he had not expected. Under every photograph of Mr Tibbles was the same small row of marks. He had seen them in December and they had been marks. Now Mrs Figg said *Mr Tibbles* and pointed. She turned the page, said *Mr Tibbles*, and pointed again. The marks under her finger were the same marks; the cat was the same cat; the sound was the same sound. The three of them went together.

It was the first time the marks had meant anything. He looked at the next page before she turned it, found the marks, and knew what she was going to say before she said it. She said it.

* * *

The last thing in that first year was the smallest, and it mattered most.

He had been going through the night every night since it happened. He did not choose to. It came, in the dark, the way the dark did: the stairs, the cot, Voldemort, the green, the dust, Hagrid, the stone, the three voices. He knew it by now the way you know a room in your own house, and he had noticed that it did not fade when other things did; he could not, in July, have said what Mrs Figg's kitchen looked like. But the three voices on the doorstep were as clear as they had been, every sound of them, and one night at the end of July, lying on the mattress with the encyclopaedia above him, he asked himself why.

The answer, when it came, was simple enough that he distrusted it. He had gone through the one and not the others. He had taken the night out every evening and looked at it and put it back, and the looking was what had kept it. So he tried it. That night, instead of the night, he went through the day: the morning, the porridge, the television, Dudley's shouting, the walk to the shops in the pushchair and every shop front they passed, Petunia's telephone call and every word of it, dinner, the stairs. In order. Twice. It took him two or three hours, and the house half a minute. In the morning he had it, all of it, as clear as the doorstep. He tried it again the next night, and the next. It held.

He did not know, then, what he had found, except that it was a second thing he could do, after the walking, that nobody had taught him, and that from now on nothing that happened to him need be lost. An ordinary person who tried to go through each day before sleeping would run out of night long before they ran out of day. Harry, from the end of July 1982, ran out of day every evening with a great deal of night left over, and lay in it listening to the house.

His second birthday was in there somewhere. He did not know it. Nobody said anything, and there was no cake. The only sign of it, which he kept without understanding, was that Petunia looked at him oddly at breakfast on the last day of the month, for longer than she usually let herself, and then looked away.
