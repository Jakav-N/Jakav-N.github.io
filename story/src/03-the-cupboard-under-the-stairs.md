# Chapter 3: The Cupboard Under the Stairs

Marge's dog did not like him. That was not news; nothing that came into the house liked him apart from Mrs Figg's cats. What was new, that August, was that the dog decided to do something about it.

Ripper was a bulldog, and a bulldog's charge is not fast even to an ordinary observer. To Harry, sitting on the back lawn with his back to the fence, it was a long slow business of weight moving forward, and he had most of a minute, by his own reckoning, to decide what to do about it. He had never dodged anything in his life, because Dudley's spoons and blocks always arrived before his body could be got out of the way. A dog that was going to take a second and a half to reach him was a different proposition. He sent his legs the instruction early, which was the whole trick, and sat still while they got round to obeying, and when Ripper arrived at the place where Harry had been, Harry was two feet to the left, watching him go past. Ripper tried twice more. The third time he stopped short, sat down on the grass, and looked at Harry with an expression Harry would later find in the dictionary under *baffled*.

"Dogs know," Marge said from the patio, where she had watched the whole thing with a glass in her hand. "Dogs know a wrong 'un, Vernon. Look at him. Won't go near it."

Vernon laughed. Harry sat on the grass and thought it over. To everyone else in the garden the dog was fast, and he had got out of its way three times without any trouble, because the dog was slow to him, and so was everything else. He had known that for nine months without seeing what it was for. He put it with the things that might one day be useful, which was a short list, and went back to watching a bee.

* * *

Petunia cut his hair in September.

He did not know why. He went over the morning afterwards looking for a reason and could not find one. She had simply looked at him across the kitchen while Dudley ate his toast, said "Right," and fetched the scissors. She held his head still with one hand. The blades went past his ear with a sound like something tearing slowly, which to him it was, and when she had finished there was almost none of it left. She looked at the result and seemed, if anything, more annoyed than before.

It was the first time he had been handled roughly for no reason he could see, and he hated it with a child's whole heart. That night, lying on his mattress going through the day as he always did, he came to the part with the scissors and stopped there. He stayed there. He went over his hair as it had been that morning, every piece of it, with a two-year-old's full and furious attention.

In the morning it was back.

He noticed before Petunia did. He put his hand up and it was all there, exactly as it had been. Then Petunia came in to unhook the gate and saw, and made the sound again, the one she had made on the doorstep and for the bottle.

Vernon was told that evening. Harry listened to the telling through the floor, and then to the quiet voice, and then the door of the box room opened and Vernon was in it, which had never happened before. He did not say much. He picked Harry up under the arms, carried him downstairs, opened the door of the cupboard under the stairs, put him in, and shut it.

"You can think about what you've done," Vernon said through the door, and the catch clicked.

The dark was total. Harry had never been anywhere with no light in it at all, and he discovered that his eyes went on trying to see for a long time before they gave up. There were spiders; he could hear them, which nobody else in the house could have. There was the smell of shoes. There were the stairs directly above his head, so that when Petunia went up to bed the whole ceiling of his world creaked past him, step by step.

He thought about what he had done, since he had been told to. He did not know what he had done. He knew that his hair had been cut, that he had minded very much, that he had gone over it in the night, and that it had come back. So that was four: the bottle, the television, the engine, the hair. He laid them out in the dark and found that the fourth one broke two of the rules he had made from the first three. He had not been looking at anything; his eyes had been shut. And he had not, exactly, been wanting. He had been remembering, with feeling, which was not the same thing. Or was it? He did not know. He knew that four was still not many, and that one of the four did not fit, which was either a problem or the most interesting thing that had happened to him since the night.

He found the light cord by feel after a long time, perhaps a day of his own. There was a bulb. There were the spiders, seen now, and the shoes, and the underside of the stairs, and a bolt on the inside, rusted open. He looked at everything there was to look at, which took about an hour, and then Petunia's voice came through the door and told him to turn that light off this minute, and he did.

* * *

Dudley started at a playgroup that month, two mornings a week. Harry was not sent. He heard why through the kitchen door, in the voice Petunia used for things she had decided: Dudley needed other children, the other one didn't need anything, and anyway she wasn't having him showing off in front of other people's mothers. Harry considered the phrase. He had not, that he could remember, shown off; he had hidden, carefully, since March. It seemed to be possible to show off by sitting still, if you sat still in the right way.

He did not mind about the playgroup. The mornings Dudley was out were the best he had. Petunia put him in the box room with the gate across and got on with the house, and for three hours, which was a month of his own, nobody looked at him or hit him or needed anything, and he had the encyclopaedia and the window. He finished the twelve volumes that autumn, by pictures. He could not read a word of them. But he knew the shape of every caption in every volume, and about sixty of the shapes had attached themselves to sounds, because the pictures told him what they must be: LION under a lion, AFRICA across a map he had seen on the news, ELEPHANT, which was easy. Sixty words in a year was not fast, and he knew it. It was sixty more than nothing, and it was his.

* * *

In October Dudley fell off a chair, and Harry caught him.

That was not how anybody would have described it. Dudley had climbed onto a kitchen chair to reach the biscuit tin on the counter, which he did most days and was told off for most days, and the chair tipped. Harry was in the doorway. He saw the chair start to go. He saw Dudley's arms come up and his face begin, very slowly, to change its mind about the biscuits, and he saw where the back of Dudley's head was going to meet the floor, and he did not want that. He did not stop to wonder whether wanting would do anything. He wanted it with the whole of himself, the way he had wanted the bottle, and because he had time he saw it as well: Dudley landing not on the tiles but on the chair's cushion, the fat square cushion that was tied to the seat and was at that moment travelling the other way.

Dudley landed on the cushion. The chair landed on its side three feet away with nothing tied to it.

Petunia had been at the sink, and had turned at the first scrape of the chair, and had seen the whole thing. She picked Dudley up and held him while he roared, in outrage rather than pain, and over the top of his head she looked at Harry for a long time. It was, by his reckoning, about an hour. He could not read the look. It had fear in it, which he knew, and something else that he did not.

"Go in the other room," she said at last, quite quietly, and he went.

For nine days afterwards she was different. He counted the days, because he counted everything now. She said his name twice, which she did perhaps twice a month. She gave him Dudley's portion at dinner once, by what might have been accident. She did not tell Vernon; he listened for it and it did not come. On the tenth day she was as she had been, as if the nine days had been an illness she had recovered from.

He learned three things. It worked on people, not just bottles. It worked on things that were moving, which he had not tried. And Petunia could be grateful to him and afraid of him at the same time, and when the two were in the room together the fear won. He would have liked to be wrong about the last one. He was not wrong about it for a long time.

* * *

In November a new channel appeared on the television, and the first thing it showed was a game with letters in it.

Vernon had views about Channel 4, which he aired at length. Petunia watched Countdown anyway, every afternoon at a quarter to five, because it was on while she made the tea and because, she told Yvonne, Richard Whiteley had a nice way about him. Harry watched it from the high chair, and it was the most useful half-hour the television had ever given him.

People chose letters, one at a time, and said their names aloud, and the names were sounds Harry knew from Dudley's alphabet blocks. As each one was said, a shape went up on a board. Then the clock ran, and the people said words, and a woman wrote the words up out of the same shapes. He had known for a year that the shapes meant something, since Mr Tibbles, and for months that they came in a fixed order, since the spines of the encyclopaedia. What he had not known was that each shape was a sound, and that a word was its sounds laid out in a row. By the end of the first week he had the names of all twenty-six. By the end of the second he could watch a word go up and hear, more or less, how it would be said before Richard Whiteley said it, provided it was a short word and a kind one. English, he was discovering, had a great many unkind words.

The second half of the game was numbers, and he took to that at once. Six numbers went up, and a target, and when the thirty seconds were over a man wrote on the board how the target could be made from the six. Harry had numbers already, from Dudley's counting books and from Petunia counting out sweets, but he had not known what you could do with them. He learned adding from the first week's working and multiplying from the second, and by the end of November he could do the numbers round in his head before the clock had finished its first tick, which was not difficult, given that he had three thousand seconds of his own for every one of its thirty. The letters round he could not do at all. He did not know enough words by shape. He knew exactly what he needed, which was more of them, and had no idea where to get it.

* * *

He was in the box room with the door open, because Petunia had forgotten to shut it, when she said it to Yvonne.

"He watches," she said. He could hear her through the floor and up the stairs; he could have heard her from the garden. "He just watches, Yvonne. Dudley's a normal little boy, he runs about, he shouts, he's into everything. That one sits there with my dad's encyclopaedias open on the floor like he's reading them. He's two." There was a pause while Yvonne said something. "No. No, I don't want anyone looking at him. I don't want that sort of attention, thank you very much. We'll manage."

Harry sat on the floor with volume 7 open in front of him and thought about it, which took him the rest of the afternoon.

So she had noticed. He had thought he was being careful, and he had been, by the only measure he had, which was Dudley. It had not been enough. There was something about the way he sat with a book that was wrong to her, and he did not know what it was, because he could not see himself sitting. The sentence that mattered was the last one. *I don't want anyone looking at him.* It meant that whatever he did in this house, nobody from outside it was going to be asked to look. So if he ever wanted anything the Dursleys would not give him, he was going to have to find a way to be noticed that went round Petunia rather than through her. He did not know how to do that. He was two and a quarter. He put it with the other things he did not know how to do, which was a longer list than he liked, and went back to volume 7.

* * *

The new television came the week before Christmas.

Mr Polkiss had one, which was the reason. It was enormous. It had a remote control, which Vernon would not let anyone else touch and which Dudley was therefore allowed to work on his lap, and it had teletext, which Vernon demonstrated to anyone who sat still for long enough. You pressed a button and the picture went away and there were pages of writing instead, big clear letters on black, with numbers in the corner, and the pages changed by themselves. Vernon showed Marge the weather map and the football results and the news headlines on page 101, and Harry, in the high chair, looked at page 101 and felt something go very still inside him.

He could read some of this. Not much, and not properly. But the letters were large and clear, and he knew their sounds now, and the football results were a list of names he had heard Vernon shout at the set every Saturday for a year. ARSENAL was a shape with its sound attached before he had finished looking at it. TOTTENHAM took longer, and taught him something about the letter H. By Christmas he could sound his way through any word that was spelled the way it sounded, slowly, one shape at a time, and he had found the wall, which was that a great many words were not.

Mrs Figg gave him a book for Christmas. It came through Petunia, who allowed it because Marge was watching and because it was small. It was the first book that had ever been his, and he read it in the box room on Boxing Day and got perhaps a quarter of the way into the words. *The* he had. *And* he had. *Shoe* he got from the picture. *Enough*, *thought*, and *night* sat on the page like locked doors. He went through the book eleven times, and on the eleventh he knew the shape of every word in it without knowing the sound of half of them, and he understood what he was up against. The rules only took you so far. After that you had to be told, and there was nobody in the house who would tell him.

* * *

In the first week of January the box room became Dudley's playroom, and Harry moved under the stairs.

He had known it was coming. He had heard it decided on Christmas Day and settled over the washing-up on New Year's Eve, so when Petunia carried his mattress downstairs he did not cry, which she seemed to find more unsettling than if he had. The encyclopaedia stayed where it was, because Vernon thought it might be worth something. Harry's mattress went onto the cupboard floor, his clothes onto the shelf above it, the door shut, and he was two and a half years old in the dark with nothing to look at.

It was the worst thing that had happened to him since the night, and he spent the first evening deciding how to think about it. The window was gone. The books were gone. There was no light unless he pulled the cord, and Petunia had made it clear that the cord was for five minutes at bedtime and no more; she could see the line under the door from the hall. He learned within two nights that the fourth stair creaked when she came down to check, and to have the light off by the third. What was left was sound. The cupboard was in the middle of the house, and it turned out that everything in the house could be heard from it: both televisions, every telephone call, and Petunia and Vernon in the kitchen at night with the door shut, which had never been audible from the box room and was perfectly audible here. By the end of January he knew the Dursleys' life better than they did.

And there was the other thing, which he had not tried since October, and which there was nothing now to stop him trying. The light bulb hung from the ceiling a foot above his face. He wanted it brighter. He did not need it brighter; he wanted it, and he had learned that the wanting had to be real, so he made it real, by lying in the dark and thinking about the window he no longer had until the feeling came. Then he pictured the bulb, exactly, filament and glass and the dust on top of it, glowing harder. Nothing happened. He did it again. He counted, because counting was what he had.

The bulb brightened on the four hundred and somethingth attempt, in the second week of January, for about a second. Then it went back to normal, and Harry lay under it and shook with something that was not quite laughter and not quite anything else.

After that he was systematic, because there was nothing in the cupboard to be anything else with. He varied one thing at a time. The picture mattered: a vague bulb did nothing, and a bulb exact to the dust did something perhaps one time in thirty by the end of the month. The feeling mattered, and the quickest way to it was to go through the night first, which he did not enjoy and did anyway. Looking did not matter; it worked with his eyes shut. Wanting mattered less than seeing. He kept the counts in his head, where everything was kept now, and by the end of January he had a rule he could say to himself: *it isn't strength, it's clarity*. He did not know what it was. He knew what it was like.

On the last Friday in January the door catch clicked open while he was lying there picturing it. He had not expected it to. He lay and looked at the line of hall light that had appeared down the edge of the door, and did not get up. After a while he reached out and pulled the door shut again, and thought for a long time about the fact that he could.

The bolt came in February. He had left the catch open once, by mistake, after a success that pleased him too much, and Petunia found it in the morning and said nothing, and that afternoon there was a new bolt on the outside of the door, which Vernon fitted in silence after work. Harry listened to the screws go in. It took Vernon four minutes, which was a long time to listen to someone making sure of you.

* * *

In March he asked for a book.

Dudley, at two and three-quarters, spoke in sentences now, badly, so a sentence from Harry would not by itself be remarkable. He chose a morning when Vernon had gone and Dudley was occupied and Petunia was at the sink, which was where she was easiest, and he said, "Aunt Petunia, can I have a book? One with words in."

She did not turn round for a while. "You've got Dudley's old ones."

"One with words in," Harry said. "Please."

"Books," said Petunia, "are for people who can read."

She did not say anything else, and neither did he. He took the plate she gave him and went and sat with it. It was the answer he had expected, more or less, and the point of asking had not really been the book. The point had been to find out whether asking was a thing that could work in this house, and now he knew. She had not stopped to wonder whether he could read. She had not asked why he wanted one. She had said the first thing that came to hand that meant *no*, and gone back to the sink, and if he asked again when he could read every word in the house he did not think the answer would be different. So the books, when they came, would have to come some other way.

He could get perhaps seventy words in a hundred by then, from page 101 and the headlines of Vernon's newspaper and the dictionary that lived by Vernon's chair, which he read a page at a time when the room was empty. The thirty he could not get were the ones that broke the rules, and he had come to understand, from the half-readable article on English in volume 3, that the rules broke because the language had been built out of four other languages at different times and nobody had tidied up. The exceptions could not be worked out. They had to be learned, one by one, from someone who knew them. The television knew them. It said them all day long, clearly, in the mouths of people who had never got one wrong in their lives, and it never once showed him how they were spelled. The newspaper showed him how everything was spelled and never said a word aloud. He needed the two together, and he had been trying for a month to think of anything in the world that did both.

* * *

It was Dudley who found it, on a Monday evening at the end of March.

Vernon had got into the habit of letting Dudley hold the remote control, on the grounds that it kept him quiet and that the buttons could do no harm. Petunia was watching a serial she followed. Vernon had the paper. Harry was in the high chair by the kitchen door, which was where he was put in the evenings now so that he could be seen. Dudley pressed buttons, a great many of them, in no order, with the whole flat of his hand, and the picture went to teletext and back, and the sound went off and on, and Vernon said "Dudley" in the tone that meant nothing. Then Dudley pressed something, and the picture stayed, and a line of writing appeared across the bottom of it.

The people in the serial were talking. The writing was what they were saying.

Harry saw it in the first sentence. A woman on the screen said eight words, slowly, over what was for him a quarter of an hour, and under her the eight words sat in white letters on a black strip, and he read them one at a time as she said them, sound against shape, shape against sound. Two of the eight were words he had never been able to work out and now could. Then she said another sentence, and so did the man she was talking to, and the writing kept up.

Vernon noticed after the fourth sentence and wanted it off. He did not know how it had got on, and Dudley, asked, hit the remote control against the arm of the sofa. It took Vernon the better part of five minutes of irritable button-pressing to find the right one, and in those five minutes Harry read perhaps sixty sentences, learned eleven words he had not known, and saw the whole of it.

Thousands an hour. That was what the thing would give him, if he could get it turned on: thousands of pairs of sound and spelling an hour, every exception in the language said and shown at once, by a machine that never tired and was in the front room every evening. The machine belonged to Vernon, and the button, for the moment, belonged to Dudley.

He looked at Dudley, who was being told off, and then at the three figures that had sat in the corner of the screen the whole time the writing was there, which were 8, 8 and 8, and he began to work out how it was going to be done.
