# Chapter 6: Through the Letterbox

Petunia did not say a word on the drive home. Dudley talked about the crocodile for the whole half-mile and she did not answer him either, and Harry sat in the back with the coat on his knees and watched the side of her face, which had gone from white to a mottled red that he had not seen before and did not have a name for yet.

At the house she took him straight to the cupboard and bolted it. He heard her go into the kitchen and run the tap, and not turn it off for a long time.

She did not tell Vernon when he came in. Harry listened to her not telling him through the whole of dinner. There was a place for it after Dudley's day and a place for it after the golf. She went past both. He heard the pauses where she went past them, and understood that she was working herself up to it the way he had once watched her work herself up to telling Vernon about the engine, which she had never managed. This time she managed it. The plates went into the sink and the kitchen door shut and her voice began, low and fast, and he could not make out the words through two doors, but he did not need to, because Vernon's answer was at a volume that did not need doors.

*Freak* was in it. He had heard the word before, in the quiet voice, and this was not the quiet voice. *Showing us up* was in it, and *in front of the whole street*, *what did I say*, *what did I tell you*, and a good deal more of the same kind. Under it was Petunia's voice, which had stopped being low and fast and gone thin. It went on for perhaps a quarter of an hour of the house's time. Then the kitchen door opened. Two sets of footsteps came down the hall, the bolt went back, the cupboard door opened, and the two of them were standing in the light from the hall looking down at him.

He had been waiting for this, and had decided what he would say. He sat up on the mattress and said it.

"I can read," he said. "I've been able to since April. I asked Aunt Petunia for books first, on Friday, before I did anything, and she said no. So I told two people at the library today. They've seen me read and they'll tell other people. I did it because I need books and there wasn't another way to get them. I'm not going to do anything to you. I'd like to be let out of here. And I know you're frightened of what I am. I don't know what I am, but I know you're frightened of it, and I'm sorry about that, and it doesn't change what I need."

It was the longest thing he had ever said aloud to anyone, and he said it in the voice he had, which was a small child's voice, and he watched it land.

Vernon's face did several things. It had been red when the door opened and it went redder, and then, as the sentences went on, something else came into it under the red, and Harry recognised it, because he had seen it on Petunia a dozen times. Vernon had not been frightened of him before. Vernon was frightened of him now. It did not stop him. He said that Harry would do as he was told in this house, that he would not go anywhere near that library again, that he would not speak to anyone, that if he thought a few lies to a couple of busybodies were going to get him anything he had another think coming, and he said these things loudly and at length, and Harry let him, because none of it was a question and none of it changed anything.

Petunia had not said anything. When Vernon ran down she said, to the hall rather than to Harry, in a voice with nothing in it:

"He's like her."

Harry looked at her. He had wanted to ask about his mother for a year and a half, in the way one wants a thing one knows one cannot have, and he had never once asked, because every road to the question went through this woman, and this woman went stiff at the word *sister*. He asked now, since the door was open.

"I don't know what she was like," he said. "I'd like to."

Petunia turned and walked away down the hall, and the kitchen door shut behind her.

Vernon looked after her, then down at Harry. Whatever he had been going to say next had gone. "Cupboard," he said. "And no library. And you don't talk to anyone. Anyone. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," Harry said. "The library's already done, though. They know."

Vernon shut the door. The bolt went across. Harry lay back down on the mattress in the dark and let out a breath he had been holding since the door opened, and then began, because there was nothing else to do and because it was the plan, to wait.

* * *

Nothing happened on the Sunday, which was what he had expected. Vernon telephoned nobody, because there was nobody for Vernon to telephone. Petunia kept Dudley away from the cupboard door and let Harry out for meals and the lavatory and put him back. Harry spent the day going over the two real conversations he had had with adults in his life, on Saturday morning and Saturday night, taking them apart, and deciding what he would say to the next adult, who he thought would be a stranger.

He learned afterwards what happened on the Monday, and it was more or less what he had hoped. Mrs Ashworth, who had spent Sunday thinking about a child's fingerprints on a copy of Bronowski, telephoned the area librarian, who thought she was exaggerating, and then a friend of hers in the education department at the county council, who did not. Mr Hobday telephoned the Surrey Advertiser and asked for whoever did the human interest, and was put through to a young man called Tom Brayley, who was twenty-four and had been at the paper for a year and thought it was a hoax, and wrote it down anyway, because a retired engineer from Vickers does not sound like a hoaxer, and because it was June and there was nothing else on.

On the Tuesday evening the telephone rang in the hall during dinner. Vernon answered it. Harry, in his high chair, heard him say "Yes?" and then "Who?" and then "No, he is not, and you can tell whoever gave you this number—" and then the receiver going down hard enough to make the bell inside it chime. Vernon came back to the table and said "Bloody reporters" to Petunia. Petunia put down her fork. Neither of them looked at Harry, who ate his dinner and thought: *Tuesday*. He had guessed Wednesday.

On the Wednesday morning, with Vernon gone to work and Harry let out for his breakfast and not yet put back, somebody knocked at the front door.

Petunia was in the kitchen. She did not move. The knock came again, politely. She still did not move. Harry, standing in the kitchen doorway with his empty bowl, looked at her back and saw that she was going to stand at the sink until whoever it was went away, and that she was shaking.

He put the bowl on the table and went into the hall.

The letterbox was a brass flap at about the height of his face. He pushed it open with his fingers. Through the slot he could see a strip of the step, a pair of brown shoes, and a notebook.

"Hello," he said through the slot. "I'm Harry. I think I'm the one you're here about."

The shoes moved. A face came down into the strip, sideways, a young man's, with a moustache that was not succeeding. The face looked at the letterbox for a moment and then at the slot, where Harry's eyes were.

"My aunt won't open the door," Harry said. "She's frightened. If you want to know whether it's true you should ask Mrs Ashworth at the library, and Mr Hobday, who's there on Tuesdays and Saturdays. They've both seen me read. I'm not allowed to talk to you."

"Who says?" said the young man, after a pause.

"My aunt and uncle."

"How old are you?"

"Two years and ten months."

The young man said nothing for a while. Harry could see him deciding whether to write it down, and then writing it down.

"Please don't knock again," Harry said. "It frightens her. The library's better." And he let the flap drop, because he could hear Petunia coming, and stood back from the door with his hands at his sides.

She came into the hall and stopped. She looked at the door, at the shadow of a man on the glass, and at Harry.

"What have you done?"

He told her. He told her exactly, because she had asked, and because he had decided on Saturday that he was not going to lie to anyone in this house, or outside it, about anything, and he was finding that the decision made a great many things simpler. She listened to all of it. Then she took him by the arm and put him in the cupboard, and did not bolt it, which he noticed.

* * *

On the Thursday the country voted, and nothing else got into any newspaper for two days.

He read about it on the Friday morning, over Vernon's shoulder, because the Mail was on the table and he was in the high chair and Vernon was too absorbed to notice where Harry's eyes were. The Conservatives had won by a very great deal. Vernon was pleased in the way he had been pleased about the Falklands, and said so to Petunia, who said that was nice, in a voice that was thinking about something else.

The other paper came on the Friday as well. It came through the door with the Mail because the paperboy delivered both. Vernon unfolded it to see what it was, and went still.

Harry could not see the front page from the high chair. He could see Vernon's face, which was enough, and he could see the back page, which had an advertisement for a garden centre on it, and he waited. It took Vernon a real minute to read whatever he was reading, which was several hours of Harry's own, and then Vernon put the paper down on the table face up and Harry read it in four looks.

It was below the fold on the front. LITTLE WHINGING TODDLER 'READS UNIVERSITY TEXTBOOKS', SAY LIBRARY STAFF. There was a photograph of the library. There was Mrs Ashworth, quoted, saying that she had worked with children for twenty years and had never seen anything like it. There was Mr Hobday, described as a retired engineer, saying that the boy had explained the second law of thermodynamics to him and had done it better than his lecturers. There was a paragraph that said the family had declined to comment, and under it a paragraph that said the boy himself, speaking to the Advertiser through the letterbox of the family home, had given his age as two years and ten months and asked that the family not be disturbed. It did not say anything about a cupboard. He had not told the young man about the cupboard.

Vernon said something, in the quiet voice, about who had been talking to the papers through his front door. Harry listened to it. It was the same voice, and he had been afraid of it for nineteen months, and he found, sitting in the high chair with the Advertiser face up on the table between them, that it had less in it than it used to. It was not that Vernon had changed. It was that there was now a newspaper in the room.

* * *

The letter came on the Saturday morning. It was addressed to Mr and Mrs V. Dursley, and it was from Dr Anne Whitlock, Senior Educational Psychologist, Surrey County Council, and it requested an appointment at their earliest convenience to assess Harry Potter in connection with his educational provision.

"No," said Vernon, and put it down.

Harry was at the table. He had decided on Friday night that he would have this conversation if it came, and that he would have it as one adult has it with another, in the terms the other adult understands, and he had gone over Vernon's terms for most of a subjective week. He waited until Vernon had finished saying no in several ways and then he said:

"May I say something?"

Vernon looked at him. It was not permission. It was not refusal either.

"She's from the council," Harry said. "The council has powers. If you say no, she can come anyway, with someone else, and that will look worse than saying yes. The newspaper's got the story already. If a family in the paper refuses to let the council see the child, that's what the next story is about. If they let the council in and the council says the child is fine, that's the end of it. And the people who've telephoned since Friday aren't only reporters. One of them was a man from a publisher." He had heard Vernon take that call. "Children like this get scholarships. They get asked onto television. They get book contracts. There's money in it, and the money goes to the family. It doesn't go to a family that's shut the door on the council."

Vernon did not say anything for a long time. Harry watched the calculation go across his face, because at Harry's speed it was possible to watch a man calculate, and he saw the moment when the money went in on one side and the shame of being asked questions by a woman from the council went in on the other, and the money was heavier.

Petunia had been standing behind Vernon's chair. "They'll find out," she said.

Harry looked at her. "Find out what?"

He knew what she meant. He asked because he wanted, once, to hear her say it; because if she said it he would know what she called it, and that would be the first thing he had ever learned about the thing from anyone but himself. She looked back at him and did not say it. After a moment she went out of the room.

Vernon wrote to the council that afternoon. Harry watched him do it from the doorway, and noticed that he wrote slowly and pressed hard, and that he signed it *V. Dursley* with a flourish that took the pen most of a second.

* * *

Dr Whitlock came on the Monday at ten.

She was a tall woman in her forties with grey eyes and a bag that she set down on the dining table and opened like a doctor's, and she looked at Harry, when Petunia brought him in, the way the health visitor had once looked at him and nobody else had since: at him, rather than at the place where he was.

"Hello, Harry," she said. "I'm Anne. I've come to find out a bit about how you think. Is that all right?"

"Yes," said Harry. "Some of it."

She took that in without comment, and began.

The tests were the ones from the textbook, more or less. There were pictures to name and shapes to match and a row of blocks to arrange like a pattern on a card, and he did them as fast as his hands would go, which was the only limit. Then there was a book of words to read aloud, in order, getting harder. He read down the first column and the second and the third without stopping. She turned the page, and he read that one, and she closed the book and sat for a moment with her hand on it. Then there were passages: short ones to begin with, about a dog and a ball; longer ones, about a storm and a lighthouse; and finally a paragraph from what he thought was a school history book, about the causes of the First World War. She asked him questions about each, and he answered them.

"That's the top of the scale," she said, when she had turned the last page. "Nothing I've brought goes higher than that. It's meant for sixteen-year-olds."

"I'm sorry," Harry said. "I don't know what to do about that."

She laughed, which he had not expected. Then she stopped, and looked at him with the expression Mr Hobday had had after the tea. Then she got up and went out to her car and came back with two things that had not been in the bag: a journal with a grey cover and a textbook with a cracked spine.

"These are mine," she said. "I read them on the train. Would you look at this and tell me what it says?"

It was an article about how children learned to read. He took it in in twelve looks, because the type was small, and told her what it said. He told her that the author had tested one group of children and not another, and could not therefore say what he had said in his conclusion, and that the numbers in his third table did not support his second paragraph even on their own terms. She took the journal back and found the third table and looked at it for a long time.

"How?" she said. "I mean that as a real question. How did you learn?"

He told her. He told her about the alphabet page and the Countdown letters and page 101 and the Ladybird book and the night Dudley found the subtitles, and about the schools programme that had taken *ough* apart for him, and he gave her the dates, because he had them. She wrote it all down. He watched her write it, and saw that she was not writing what a woman writes who is making a note, but what a woman writes who is making a record.

"And you remember all of that," she said. "The dates."

"I remember everything. Since July the year before last."

"Everything?"

"Everything I pay attention to. I pay attention to most things."

"Tell me what I said when I came in."

He told her. He gave her the whole of it, from "Hello, Harry" to the moment she sat down, including the thing she had said to Petunia about the parking, in her words and in the order she had said them, and she listened to it with the pen not moving, and when he had finished she said, carefully:

"And how is it that you think so quickly? When I ask you something, you don't pause. Not even for the hard ones."

He had known the question would come. He had decided in the cupboard, three weeks ago, what he would do when it did, and he did it.

"I'd rather not say," he said.

Dr Whitlock looked at him. He looked back. He could see her weighing it, and see her notice that he had not said *I don't know*, which would have been easy, and see her decide what to do about that; and what she did was let it go. She nodded once, made a short note, and did not ask again. He thought better of her for it. He did not think there were many people who would have let it go, and he had a feeling, which he turned over later, that he had just found out something about her that mattered more than anything she had found out about him.

"You hold the book very close," she said instead. "Can you read the clock on the wall from here?"

He looked at the clock. It was a white blur with a dark blur going round it. "No," he said. "I thought that was how clocks looked."

"We'll get that looked at." She wrote it down. "Harry. Is there anything you want? From me, or from the council. I can't promise, but I can ask."

"Books," he said. "As many as anyone will give me. And a way to get to a library more than twice a week. And I'd like whoever comes next to bring books with them."

"Who do you think will come next?"

"I don't know. People. You said people would want to meet me."

"I did." She closed her bag. "They will. I'll tell them about the books."

The Mail's man and his photographer arrived at half past ten, because Vernon had told both of them ten o'clock and had not considered the consequences. Dr Whitlock met them in the hall and said something to the reporter that Harry did not catch and that the reporter did not like. Then Vernon, who had taken the morning off, steered everyone into the garden, where the light was good, and Harry sat on the back step with the textbook with the cracked spine, which Dr Whitlock had left with him without a word, and read it while the reporter asked him questions, and the photographer took forty photographs of a very small boy reading a book about statistics on a back step in Surrey, and said afterwards that it was the best morning's work he had done in a year.

* * *

It was on page three of the Mail on the Tuesday, under a headline that said THE BOY WHO READS, with a photograph of Harry and the textbook and Vernon standing behind him with one hand on his shoulder, looking paternal with great effort. Vernon bought four copies. By six o'clock it was on the regional news, with film of the library and a short interview with Mrs Ashworth, who looked frightened and said the same things she had said to the Advertiser. By nine it was the last item on the national news, the light one, delivered with a smile. Harry watched it from the high chair, in the living room, with the subtitles on, because Dudley had the remote.

* * *

On the Wednesday morning there was a van in the street with a dish on top of it, a second van behind it, three cars, people on the pavement with cameras, and Mrs Polkiss standing at her gate in her dressing gown. The telephone rang at eight and went on ringing until Vernon took it off the hook. At ten a woman from the BBC knocked, and this time Vernon opened the door.

They did it in the living room. The woman sat on the sofa and Harry sat beside her, and a man stood behind a camera on three legs, and another man held a long grey thing over their heads and told everyone to be quiet. Harry had a book. It had come that morning in a parcel from Mrs Ashworth, with a note, and he had got as far as the second chapter before the van arrived, and he had it open in his lap now, because nobody had told him to put it down and because it was a good book.

The woman asked his name and how old he was, and he told her without looking up. She asked how he had learned to read, and he told her about the subtitles, the schools programme, and Countdown, and the man with the grey thing made a small noise that was a laugh being swallowed. She asked whether he liked reading, and he turned a page and said yes. She asked what his favourite book was and he said he didn't have one yet, he hadn't read enough of them, and she asked how many he had read, and he said about ninety, not counting the encyclopaedia, which was twelve, and the woman was quiet for a moment, and then said:

"Harry. Could you look at me, do you think? For the camera?"

He looked up. He gave her the whole of his attention, the way he gave it to anything that asked for it. She found, as Mr Hobday and Dr Whitlock had found, that being looked at by Harry Potter was a different experience from being looked at by a child. She lost her next question for a second, and found it again.

"What are you reading?"

He told her. It was a textbook about how living things pass on what they are, and he told her who had written it and when.

"Goodness," she said. "Is it any good?"

"It's wrong about one thing in chapter two," Harry said. "But I think he knows, because he puts it right in chapter five. So yes. It's good."

He went back to the book. The woman looked at the camera, and the camera looked at a two-year-old on a sofa in Surrey reading a university textbook, and that evening, at six and again at nine, so did everyone else.
