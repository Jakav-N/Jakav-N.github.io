# Chapter 8: Midsummer

Petunia did not move from the door. She stood with one hand on it and the other at her throat and looked at the old man on the step, and Harry, coming down the stairs behind her, saw that it was not quite fear on her face. It was the look of someone who has spent twenty years knowing a letter would come and has finally heard the flap go.

"You," she said.

"I," the old man agreed. "May I come in? I would rather not have this conversation on your step. There is a young man on the wall at number six who has been trying to work out why he is not interested in me, and I should hate to make it harder for him."

Petunia stepped back. The old man came in, ducked his head under the lintel though he had not needed to, turned, and saw Harry on the fourth stair.

Harry looked at him for exactly as long as he wanted to, which was a real second, and the man looked back. The eyes were a very light blue. The face was arranged in a kind of attention Harry had seen on Dr Whitlock and on nobody else. People looked away from Harry. This one did not.

"Harry," the old man said. "My name is Albus Dumbledore. We have met, though you will not remember it."

"I remember it," Harry said. "You were on the doorstep. You said my name, and you said the other name, the one the woman wouldn't say. You put your hand on my head and it stopped being cold."

Dumbledore did not move. Behind him Petunia made a small sound. Dudley, who had come to the kitchen door with a biscuit, looked from one to the other with his mouth open.

"You remember the doorstep," Dumbledore said.

"I remember all of it. I didn't know what any of it meant until now. May I ask you some questions?"

"I think," said Dumbledore slowly, "that you had better."

* * *

They sat in the living room, because Petunia, after a moment in which several things visibly went through her, said that they had better sit down, in the voice of a woman falling back on the only form she had. Dumbledore took Vernon's chair. Harry took the sofa, where he could see him. Petunia stood in the doorway with Dudley against her legs and did not sit and did not leave.

Harry had the questions arranged so that each one made the next one smaller. He asked the first.

"How did my parents die?"

Dumbledore looked at him, and then at Petunia, who had gone white again, and then back.

"They were murdered," he said. "On the night of the thirty-first of October, nineteen eighty-one, in their house, by a man who had been trying to kill them for some time. Your father first, downstairs. Your mother upstairs, in your room. She was trying to protect you."

"I know," Harry said. "I was there. I remember that too." He heard Petunia's breath go in and did not look at her. "She said *not Harry, please, not Harry*. She said *take me instead*. He said two words and there was a green light and she fell down. Then he said the two words to me. Nobody has ever told me anything except that it was a car crash, and I knew that wasn't true, because of the way it was said."

There was a silence in the room of a kind Harry had not heard in it before. Petunia had her hand over her mouth. Dumbledore had not moved at all, and Harry saw that this was a man who did not move when he was moved, and that he was moved now.

"No," Dumbledore said at last. "It was not a car crash. I am sorry that you were told so. I am sorry for a great many things in this house, and I will come to them. Ask your next question."

"Who are you?"

"I am the headmaster of the school your parents went to. I knew them both from the time they were eleven. I was, at the end, something like their commander, in a war you have not heard of, against the man who killed them. It was I who brought you here. That was the doorstep."

"What are you?"

He had expected the man to pause at that. The man did not pause.

"A wizard," said Dumbledore. "As your father was. As your mother was. As you are."

Harry sat with that. He had tried that word on in the cupboard, among others, and it had seemed each time too much like a word from Dudley's books. Said in that voice, in Vernon's chair, it did not.

"Why is my aunt afraid of you?"

"Because your mother was a witch and your aunt is not, and the gift went to the younger sister, and that is a hard thing to be the elder of. And because the last time anything from my world came into her life it was you, on a step, with a letter from me that asked a great deal of her and explained less than it should have. She has reason."

"When I was sixteen months old I wanted a bottle very badly, and it slid across the table to me. Since then it's happened by itself about ten times, and for the last eight months I've been making it happen on purpose, in a cupboard, and counting. What is that?"

Dumbledore's eyebrows went up a little. "Tell me about it. All of it."

Harry told him. He told him about the bottle in the first week of December 1981, the television in February, the engine on the wardrobe in May, his hair in September, Dudley and the cushion in October, and then the cupboard: the bulb on the four hundred and somethingth attempt, one in thirty by the end of January, one in twenty in February, the catch, the spider, the rules. He gave the dates and the counts. He did not give the speed, because he had not yet decided about that, and he watched the old man's face while he talked and saw it go from attention to something stiller than attention, and stay there.

"That," Dumbledore said when he had finished, "is called accidental magic. All children who have the gift do it. It comes with strong feeling and goes with it. Very few of them do it on purpose before they have a wand. None of them, in my experience, which is long, keep count."

"Then I'm one too."

"Yes."

"And that's why they were killed. Because of this."

"They were killed because they stood against a man who wanted to rule everyone who has this, and to do a great deal worse to everyone who has not. And for a reason that was particular to you, which I am not going to tell you today."

Harry looked at him. "But there is one."

"There is one."

"Will you tell me when I'm older?"

"That," said Dumbledore, "is the first thing you have asked me that I do not know the answer to."

* * *

Petunia made tea. She had not been asked to; Dumbledore had turned to her and said, "Mrs Dursley, I wonder if I might trouble you," in a voice that made it seem her own idea, and she took Dudley with her and shut the kitchen door.

Then Dumbledore told him what he had been waiting twenty months to be told, in order and without decoration. Harry had read enough history that month to know what it sounded like when a man chose which parts to say. He heard the choosing, and heard also that what was chosen was true.

There were people who could do what Harry could do, about three thousand of them in Britain, hidden for three hundred years by law and habit, with a government and a bank and newspapers and a school in Scotland of which Dumbledore was the headmaster. A dozen years ago a man had risen among them who called himself Voldemort, which was not his name, and had killed a great many people getting what men like that want. Harry's parents had fought him, gone into hiding when Harry was born, and been betrayed by a friend. On the night Harry remembered, Voldemort had killed James Potter on the stairs and Lily Potter in the nursery and had turned the same curse on Harry, and the curse had not worked.

"It has never not worked," Dumbledore said. "Not once, in all the history I know. It struck you here," and he touched his own forehead where the mark was on Harry's, "and it rebounded, and it destroyed him, or his body, which is not the same thing. I do not believe he is gone. I believe he will try to return. His followers are many of them free. You are, in my world, the most famous child alive, because you lived, and for nearly two years almost nobody in it has known where you were. That was the point. Now there is a photograph of you in a newspaper, with the address."

"That's my fault."

"It is a consequence of a thing you did for good reason. I would have done the same, I think, in a cupboard, at your age. I am not here to reproach you. I am here because it has changed what must be done."

"Why here?" Harry said. "Why them?"

Dumbledore was quiet for a moment.

"Your mother died for you," he said. "That is not a figure of speech. She was offered her life and refused it, and a thing like that, done by a witch, does not simply end. It left something on you. A protection. It is why the curse rebounded, and it is the strongest thing standing between you and the man who cast it, and it holds for as long as you can call home a place where her blood lives. Her blood lives in her sister. There is no one else." He looked at the kitchen door. "I knew what kind of house this would be. I thought it would be unkind and safe, and I judged that safe was the thing that could not be done without. I did not know about the cupboard until Sunday. I should have. That is one of the things I am sorry for."

Harry thought about that for most of an hour of his own and ten seconds of the room's: about one in five, and the certainty of this house, and the fact that he had come down on the side of staying, for his own reasons, before anyone had told him there was one.

"All right," he said. "I understand why. I don't like it, but I'd probably have done the same. Show me something."

"Show you something?"

"You've told me what you are. I believe you, I think, because of the bottle. But I've had a word for twenty months and no picture."

Dumbledore took a stick of pale wood from his jacket, and Harry's whole body went cold for a real second, because he had seen one in a doorway with the landing light behind it, before his mind caught up and said *no, look, this is not that man*. Dumbledore saw it. He laid the wand on the arm of the chair and waited, and when Harry nodded he picked it up and pointed it at the sugar bowl.

The sugar bowl became a tortoise.

There was no flash. One moment it was a sugar bowl and the next it was a small brown tortoise with a shell the pattern of the bowl's glaze, looking about itself with the air of something that has just arrived and is not sure where. It put one foot forward on the polished wood and did not like the surface.

"Is it alive?" Harry said.

"It is a tortoise in every respect that a tortoise can be tested for, for as long as it remains a tortoise."

"Where did the sugar go?"

"That is a question on which my world has several schools of thought and no agreement."

"Does it hurt it? Being that, and then not?"

Dumbledore looked at him, and for the first time something in his face changed that Harry could not read.

"No one has ever asked me that," he said. "In a hundred years. I do not believe it does. I confess that I do not know." He raised the wand. The tortoise was a sugar bowl, and the room was as it had been.

"Can I learn that?"

"In time. Ask me the next question, Harry. I think you have one you have been saving."

He had been saving it since the doorstep.

"The man said two words before the pain," Harry said. "He said them to my mother and then he said them to me. They sounded like *Avada Kedavra*."

Dumbledore closed his eyes.

He kept them closed long enough that Harry, counting, got to a minute and a half of his own, and when he opened them he looked at the sugar bowl and not at Harry.

"You remember that."

"I remember everything from that night. I've been through it about four thousand times." He had the number. "What is it?"

"It is called the Killing Curse. It does what its name says. It cannot be blocked by any shield or spell that is known. It cannot be survived. It has been cast many thousands of times, and it has never once failed to kill the person it was cast at."

"I survived it."

"Yes."

"Because of my mother."

"Yes."

"Then it can be blocked."

Dumbledore was silent.

"Once," he said.

Harry took that and put it away, and noticed as he did so that the old man had corrected himself, and had not needed to.

* * *

The tea came. Petunia set the tray down without looking at either of them and took Dudley upstairs, and the stairs creaked under them one after another. Dumbledore poured. He did it with his hands.

"Now," he said, "I have a question, and I want you to think about whether you are going to answer it, because I suspect you have thought about it already and I would rather have your considered answer than your quick one. Your aunt tells me you learned to read in a fortnight. Dr Whitlock, whom I have not met, is quoted in a newspaper as saying that you read a library in a month. Children with the gift are not, as a rule, cleverer than other children; I have taught a great many and I would know. So. How?"

Harry had believed he knew what he would do about this question, and found, now that it was in the room, that he had to decide again. He allowed himself three seconds of the room's time, which was ten minutes of his own and the longest he had ever paused in a conversation. The man had told him the truth about his parents when a lie would have been easier. *I'd rather not say* was not a lie, but it was a door shut in the face of the one person in the world who might know what was on the other side of it. And he had been alone with this for twenty months, and here, in Vernon's chair, was someone who could understand it.

"I think faster than other people," he said. "A lot faster. About two hundred times. I worked it out against the kitchen clock. If I count the way people count aloud, one elephant, two elephant, I get a little over two hundred while the second hand goes from one mark to the next, and it's the same every time I do it. It started the night my mother died, at the same moment as the pain in my head, so I think they're connected, but I don't know how. I remember everything because I have time to go over it. I learned to read in a fortnight because a fortnight was about seven years. I don't tell people. I told Dr Whitlock I'd rather not say, and she let me not say. I'm telling you because I think you'd work it out, and because I don't lie, and because I think you might know what it is."

Dumbledore did not say anything.

He did not say anything for a stretch that was long by Harry's clock and not short by the room's. He sat with the cup in his hand and looked at Harry, and Harry saw him go through something that was past surprise, somewhere he had not seen a face go before. Then Dumbledore set the cup down, carefully, on the saucer.

"Since the night," he said.

"Yes."

"Two hundred."

"A little over. It depends what you call one of my seconds, so I can't make it exact. But it's the same every time I count, and it isn't a hundred and it isn't a thousand."

"How old are you, Harry? By your own reckoning."

"I haven't counted that way. A long time. Most of it was waiting."

Dumbledore looked at him a while longer. Then he said, lower, and almost to himself, "I am a hundred and two years old, and I find I am not sure which of us is the elder." And then: "That explains a very great deal that I have been failing to explain since Saturday."

"You've thought of something," Harry said. "Just now. Several things. I can see you not saying them."

"Can you. Yes. Several. I will tell you one, which is that whatever your mother did that night, and whatever the curse did, the two together did something to you that has never happened before, to anyone, and that I do not understand, and that I do not think anyone alive understands. The rest I need to think about, and I am going to ask you to let me."

"For how long?"

"I do not know. Not forever. Will you?"

"Yes," Harry said. "If you tell me, when you're done thinking, whether you're going to tell me or not. I don't need to know what. I need to know which."

"That is a fair condition," said Dumbledore, "and a strange one, and I accept it. Now. What do you want? I have come here with a list of things I intend to do, and I would like to know, before I say them, how many are things you would have asked for."

"Books," Harry said at once. "The ones about this. All of them you'll let me have. The truth, about my parents and about what I am, as much as you'll tell me, and I'd like to know when you're not telling me. Not to be in a cupboard; that's done, but I'd like it to stay done." He stopped, and then went on, because it was true and he had been asked. "And I want things to go well for people. I decided that last night, or finished deciding it. I'd like to be in a position to do something about it, and I think this," he moved his hand slightly, meaning the sugar bowl and the wand and the whole of it, "is probably part of how."

"What is good, Harry?"

He had not expected to be asked. He did not have it in words yet, and said so.

"I don't know how to say it properly. There are fifteen million children who die every year before they're five, and most of them don't have to; there's a report. There are three million people in this country with nothing to do. There was a war that killed sixty million and nobody's sure it won't happen again. I don't think it's complicated. I think it's just hard. Fewer of those. I'll have a better answer in a year."

Dumbledore looked at him. It was the longest look yet, and Harry could not read it and did not try. It occurred to him afterwards that there had once been another boy, in another chair, who had been asked a question like that by this man and had given a different answer, and that he had just been measured against him.

"I believe you will," Dumbledore said.

* * *

The list, when it came, was shorter than Harry had feared and longer than he liked.

He would have books, brought by Dumbledore himself, beginning with the ones every child of eleven was given in his world and going on as fast as Harry got through them, which Dumbledore said like a man who did not yet believe how fast that would be. He would not have a wand. The reasons, to Harry's annoyance, were sound: a wand in the hands of a child who had read every spell in the books and could already do the thing on purpose was a danger to the child first and the street second; there was a law, which Dumbledore would bend and not break; and he wanted, he said plainly, to know Harry better first.

"I'd rather have it," Harry said. "But those are good reasons. I'd probably give them."

He would not go into Dumbledore's world yet, nor would it come to him, beyond Dumbledore and one or two others of his choosing. Those reasons Harry found less good, and said so: the men still free who had followed Voldemort were a reason for walls, not for keeping a boy from books and people, and a politics in which a famous child was a thing to be used was something he thought he could manage. Dumbledore heard him out and did not change his mind. Harry noticed that, and filed it.

There was money in the bank, to which Dumbledore held the key, and he would give Harry the facts. There would be protections on the house, put up before he left, that Harry would not see. And there would be letters: a great many people in Dumbledore's world would now write to Harry Potter, and Dumbledore proposed that those letters go to his school and be read there. He said this openly, looking at Harry while he said it, and Harry noticed that too.

"I'd like to see them. Eventually. The ones from anyone who knew my parents, at least."

"We will discuss it. Not today."

"And don't ask me to say things that aren't true," Harry said. "About any of this. If a reporter asks me whether I can do anything unusual, I'm not going to say no."

"Then say that you cannot discuss it. It has the merit of being true."

"Will you lie to me?"

The old man put down his cup.

"I will not lie to you," he said. "I may not tell you everything. There are things I have told no one, and some of them concern you, and I will keep some of them for a long time yet. But I will not tell you a thing that is false. You have my word, and in my world that has weight."

"I'd like to know when you're doing it. Not telling me."

"I will try. I am not sure I always know myself."

"Then tell me which things you're never going to tell me," said Harry, "so I can work on them on my own."

Dumbledore laughed.

It was the first time, and it surprised them both. It was a real laugh, from somewhere low down, and when it stopped the old man's eyes were wet, and he took off his spectacles and looked at them and put them back on.

"Your mother," he said, "once said something very like that to me, at fifteen, across a desk. I had forgotten until this moment. Yes. I will think about which. Ask me again in a month."

* * *

He talked to Petunia alone, in the kitchen with the door shut. Harry sat on the stairs and heard every word through the wall, and knew that Dumbledore knew he would, and knew that Dumbledore knew he knew. It was short. Most of it was Dumbledore's voice, low. He said *Lily* twice, *cupboard* once, and *I asked too much and explained too little, and I am sorry for it*. Petunia said almost nothing. Then she said one thing, in a voice Harry had never heard from her, which was *she never wrote. Not once. Not in four years*. Dumbledore said *I know*, and after that there was nothing for a while but the kitchen clock.

Vernon came home at six and found a man with a white beard to his waist sitting in his chair drinking his tea, and stood in the doorway of his own living room with his briefcase in his hand and his mouth working. Dumbledore rose and said, "Mr Dursley. You must be Vernon. I have heard so much," in a tone that made it impossible to tell what he had heard, and shook Vernon's hand before Vernon had decided to offer it. Harry watched from the sofa and did not laugh, because he had decided some time ago that he did not laugh at Vernon, and it had never been harder.

* * *

At dusk Dumbledore went out into the back garden alone and walked once round the edge of it, slowly, with the wand low at his side, saying something under his breath that Harry, at the window, could see the shape of and not hear. When he came back to where he had started he stood for a moment with his head bowed. Then he straightened, put the wand away, looked up at the window where Harry was, as if he had known all along, and inclined his head. He went out by the side gate and up the street, past the one van and the wall at number six, and the young man on the wall did not look up.

Harry sat on his bed among the books and went over what he had.

He had the word for it now: magic. He had refused the word for twenty months because it came out of Dudley's picture books, and it was the right word anyway. He knew how his parents had died, and who had killed them, and that the man was called Voldemort and was probably not dead. He knew the name of the curse. He knew that there were thousands of people like him, with a school and a bank and a government and a newspaper that had his address in it. He knew that his mother had been offered her life and had refused it, and that the refusal was still protecting him in a way nobody could explain, and that this was why he lived in this house. And he knew that the oldest and most learned person he had ever met did not understand what had happened to him on the night, had promised not to lie to him about it, and had already said there were things he would not be told.

He went through the night, from the stairs to the stone. It was the same, every sound of it, and it was not, because the green light had a name now, and the woman on the floor had known exactly what she was refusing.

He was three years old in four weeks, and he cried, for a long time, in the dark, with the books standing round the bed. He let himself. He had learned a long time ago to put his attention somewhere else when something hurt, and this once he did not, because he thought his mother would not have wanted him to, and it was the first time in his life he had been able to think a thing like that and have it mean anything.
