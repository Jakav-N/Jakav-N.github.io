# Chapter 11: The Hill and the Hat

Hagrid came on the first Wednesday in September, and Harry heard him before he saw him, because the sound came round the corner from Wisteria Walk a good ten seconds before the man did, and it was the sound of a hill moving.

He had not heard it since the doorstep. He had carried it for twenty-two months, replayed it ten thousand times, decoded the words in it one by one as he learned the words, and had never once, in all that time, expected to hear it again from outside his own head. He was at the window when it came. He stood there with his hand on the sill and listened to Hagrid come up Privet Drive talking to himself, and found that his eyes had done something he had not told them to, and let them.

Petunia opened the door because somebody had to. She had been told and had agreed, and had spent the morning cleaning a house that was already clean. When she saw what was on the step she went back against the hall wall with her hand flat on the paper and stayed there. Hagrid had to turn sideways to get through. He had on a coat that would have made a tent for a family of four and boots that each weighed what Harry weighed. He filled the hall, and then the living-room doorway, and then he saw Harry at the foot of the stairs and stopped, and his face, which was mostly beard, did a thing that Harry had seen it do once before by the light of a landing with the wall gone.

"Harry," Hagrid said, and that was as far as he got for a while.

Dudley had come to the kitchen door with a biscuit and was standing with it halfway to his mouth, perfectly silent, for the first time in Harry's experience of him. Harry went across the hall and put his hand into Hagrid's, which closed round it to the wrist, and said, "You carried me here. You were crying then too."

"Yeh remember that?" Hagrid's voice went up and cracked. "Yeh were asleep. Dumbledore said yeh were asleep."

"I was awake over Bristol. You talked the whole way. I didn't know any of the words."

Hagrid sat down on the sofa, which protested, and cried properly, into a handkerchief the size of a tablecloth, and Harry sat beside him and waited, which he was good at, and Petunia came as far as the living-room door and no further and said, in a voice with no breath behind it, that she would make tea.

* * *

He stayed three hours and talked for all of them, and Harry learned more about his parents in those three hours than in the whole of the two years before.

He learned that James Potter had been the best flier the school had seen in a generation, and Hagrid had a photograph to prove it, seven boys and girls in robes with a cup, and James in the middle with his hair on end and a grin that Harry had seen in the garden photograph and now saw in a dozen others, moving. He learned that Lily Evans had been kind to Hagrid when it was not fashionable to be, and had come to his hut for tea in her first year and gone on coming, and had once mended a wing on a creature Hagrid was not supposed to have, with a charm she was not supposed to know. He learned about the school: the lake and the squid in it, the forest and what lived in it, which Hagrid listed with the affection of a man listing his children and which included things Harry had read about in a book with a taxonomy and no family tree and several things that were not in the book at all. He learned that there was a bank under London run by goblins, with carts, and that the deep vaults were said to have dragons, though Hagrid had never seen one and would give a great deal to. He learned that Hagrid had no wand, and why, in a sentence that Hagrid said quickly and did not come back to, and Harry filed the sentence and did not come back to it either, not then.

And he learned about the night, from the other side.

"Dumbledore sent word," Hagrid said. "Middle o' the night. Go to the house, get the boy out before the muggles come. So I went. Flew. An' the top o' the house was gone, Harry, just gone, like somethin' had bitten it, an' I went in, an' yer dad was on the stairs, an'—" He stopped, and went on. "An' yeh were in the cot, awake, lookin' at me. Not cryin'. Just lookin'. I thought it was shock. I picked yeh up an' I got yeh out of there, an' I was standin' in the lane with yeh, an' that's when Sirius Black turned up."

Harry did not move.

"On that bike of his. White as a sheet, he was. Wanted me ter give yeh to him. Said he was yer godfather, said he'd look after yeh. An' I said no, I had my orders from Dumbledore, an' he argued, an' he was in a right state, an' then he sort of gave up. Went quiet. An' he said, take the bike, Hagrid, I won't be needin' it any more. An' he gave me the bike, an' I flew yeh out of there on it, an' the next day—" Hagrid's face darkened in a way that was not grief. "Well. Yeh'll have heard about the next day."

"I have," Harry said. "Thank you for telling me."

He filed the sentence exactly, in Hagrid's voice: *I won't be needing it any more.* He turned it over while Hagrid talked about something else, and it turned over in his hand two ways, like a coin. A man who had just handed his friends to a murderer and was about to go and finish the job would not be needing a motorbike. A man who had just lost everything and meant to find the one who had done it, or die trying, would not be needing one either. The sentence fitted both. He noticed, holding it, that he wanted it to fit only one, and he set the wanting to one side as he had learned to, and kept the sentence as it was, which was evidence of nothing.

When Hagrid left, at four, he stood in the hall with his coat on and said that he had asked Dumbledore if he could come again and Dumbledore had said he would think about it, and Harry said that he would ask too, and Hagrid looked at him and said, "Yeh look like him. Yer dad. But yeh've got—" and did not finish, and did not need to. He went off down Privet Drive with the Polkisses' curtains twitching behind him and the hill-sound fading round the corner toward Wisteria Walk.

Dumbledore, consulted that weekend, said once a month. Harry had expected to have to argue for it and did not.

* * *

McGonagall came on the third Saturday, alone, by the front gate, in a tweed suit that was a better imitation of a muggle suit than Dumbledore's had been, and she did not cry.

She sat on the very edge of Vernon's chair with her back straight and her handbag on her knees and looked at Harry across the living room the way, he thought, she must look at a first-year who had done something she had not seen before and had not yet decided whether to approve of. He had the clipped voice from the doorstep now with a face attached: angles, spectacles, a tight bun of dark hair with grey in it. It was not an unkind face. It was a face waiting to be given a reason.

"Professor Dumbledore tells me you have read the first-year texts," she said.

"Yes."

"All of them."

"Several times. And the second-year ones."

"Then you will not object," said McGonagall, "if I ask you some questions."

She asked them for an hour. They were the questions from the books, in the order the books put them, and he answered them in the words the books used, because that was what she was asking for and because he had the words, and he watched her face as he answered and saw her go, over the hour, from checking to listening. Somewhere around the fortieth question she stopped asking what the book said and asked what he thought, and he noticed the change in the same second she made it, and understood that he had been given his reason.

"The books say that a spell needs three things," he said. "The words, the wand movement, and the intention. All of them say it. None of them says which of the three does what. None of them asks what happens if you have two without the third. I've been trying to work that out for nine months, because I've only ever had the third."

McGonagall's spectacles caught the light. "Explain."

He explained. He told her about the bottle, the cupboard, the counts, the feather, the page that turned itself, the light that would not hold steady, and the bag of sugar that would not move. He told her about the pace: that a picture made faster than a hand could move a wand did nothing at all, however exact, and that one made at the pace of a hand did what the picture said. He told her that whatever was on the other side of the picture took pictures one at a time and at a fixed rate, that the rate was not his, and that this was the only thing he had found in nine months that he was sure was a fact about magic rather than a fact about him.

She did not say anything for a long time by his clock, and a respectable time by the room's.

"Mr Potter," she said at last, "I have taught transfiguration for twenty-seven years. I have read everything written on the theory of the subject in English and most of what has been written in Latin. I have never heard anyone describe the pace of casting as a property of the magic rather than of the caster. It is simply assumed to be the caster. It has always been assumed to be the caster."

"Has anyone tested it?"

"No," said McGonagall. "Nobody has tested it."

"Then it's assumed," Harry said. "It isn't known."

She looked at him for a moment longer, and then she did something he had not expected, which was to open the handbag on her knees and take out a notebook and a pencil and write in it, and he saw, because he could read upside down and had time, that what she wrote was what he had said.

Before she left she told him two things that were not about transfiguration.

The first was that she had stood on the step of this house on the night of the first of November 1981 and argued with Albus Dumbledore about leaving him there. She had watched the Dursleys for a day beforehand and thought them the worst sort of muggles, and had said so, and he had said that the protection on the house mattered more than the people in it. She still thought she had been right about the Dursleys. She had come to think he had been right about the protection. She did not know which of the two counted for more, and she had stopped expecting to find out.

The second was about his parents. His mother had been the best student of Charms that Flitwick had taught in his time at the school, and Flitwick said so to anyone who would listen. His father had been the most talented student of her own subject that she had taught in twenty-seven years, and the least disciplined, and had stopped being the second somewhere in his last two years at school, which she said was later than she would have liked and sooner than she had expected.

At the gate she turned and said that she would like to come once a month, if the headmaster permitted it, to talk about the theory of her subject with someone who asked questions about it, and that she thought the headmaster would permit it, and that she would see to it that he did.

* * *

Dumbledore came on the fourth Saturday with a sheet of parchment and a coin.

The parchment was the account of the vault he had promised in June. Harry read it in two looks. There were a little over thirty thousand coins called galleons in a vault under London that he might draw on for his schooling and his keep, and a second vault that had been his grandparents' and would be his at seventeen. The bank gave five pounds to the galleon. That was the whole of it. The coin was a galleon. Dumbledore had brought it because Harry had asked what one looked like, and he put it on the arm of the sofa and Harry picked it up.

It was heavy. It was heavier than it should have been for its size, which meant it was dense, which meant it was gold or something as dense as gold, and he had read the Mail's financial pages for five months and knew what gold cost that week to the pound per ounce.

"May I weigh it?"

Dumbledore's eyebrows rose. "I cannot think of a reason why not."

He weighed it on Petunia's kitchen scales, which were the kind with a dial, while Dumbledore stood in the kitchen doorway and Petunia stood as far from both of them as the kitchen allowed. The needle settled. He read it, and did the sum in the time it took the needle to settle, and did it again to be sure.

"If this is gold all the way through," he said, "it's worth about sixty pounds. Not five."

"Ah," said Dumbledore.

"Is it gold all the way through?"

"That," said Dumbledore, "is goblin business. Goblin coinage is made by goblins, and goblins do not discuss how it is made, and my world has learned over several centuries not to ask them about their metal. I would advise you not to ask either. I would advise it quite strongly." He said it in a tone that Harry had not heard from him before, which was the tone of a door being closed, and Harry heard the door close and did not try it. He put the galleon back on the arm of the sofa and said thank you, and filed the whole of it, the weight and the sum and the tone, under a heading he had been keeping since April that said *money left on a table*. He had read enough economics to know that money was not left on tables without a reason, and that the reason was usually someone who would be angry if you picked it up. He had just been told who. He did not pick it up. He wrote down where it was.

* * *

The file came the same afternoon, because he had asked for it in August and Dumbledore kept his word.

It was eleven pages. Harry had expected more, and the fact that there was not more was the first thing he learned from it. There were the reports of two Aurors, which was the word for policemen, who had reached a street in London on the morning of the first of November 1981 to find a crater and twelve dead muggles and a man standing in the middle of it laughing. There were the statements of three wizards who had seen it happen. The three agreed that a small man had shouted that Sirius Black had betrayed the Potters, that there had been an explosion, and that when it cleared the small man was gone except for a finger and the muggles were dead and Black was laughing. They did not agree about the wand in Black's hand. One of them did not mention a wand at all. There was a record that the muggle witnesses had been made to forget, which Harry read twice. There was an order, signed by a man called Bartemius Crouch, committing Sirius Black to a prison called Azkaban for life. And there was nothing else. There was no record of anyone having asked Sirius Black a single question, on that day or any day since.

Harry read it in four looks and spent the evening on it, after Dumbledore had gone, in his room, with the lamp on.

He had read, that summer, the trial of the people called Lestrange. Mrs Figg had let drop that there had been one, he had asked, and Dumbledore had brought it: sixty pages, with a charge and witnesses and questions and answers and a verdict. He put the eleven pages beside the sixty in his head and did not need to say anything to himself about the difference.

He asked Dumbledore the next question before he left, standing in the hall.

"How do you know it was him? That he was the one who knew where they were. The file says Pettigrew shouted it. It doesn't say how anyone knew it was true."

"Because your father told me," Dumbledore said, "in October, that he meant to use Sirius."

"Meant to."

"Yes."

Dumbledore did not say anything else, and Harry, who had learned to hear the shape of what people did not say, heard that there was nothing else and that Dumbledore knew it, and did not press, because the hall was not the place and because he wanted to think first.

"May I write to him?"

"No." It was gentle and it was final. "The prison is not a place that letters reach, or should. It is guarded by something I will not describe to you tonight, and I will not have a child's letter opened by it. Ask me again in a year."

Harry said that he would, and meant it. Dumbledore saw that he meant it, and went.

That night, going through the day, Harry put a number on it. He had been taught to by nobody; he had worked it out from the encyclopaedia's article on a clergyman called Bayes and from three months of the Economist's book reviews, and he did it now because he had a thing he believed and wanted to know how much. Two in three, he thought, that Black had done it. Then he noticed that he wanted it to be lower, and that the wanting was not evidence. He moved it to three in four to correct for the wanting, thought that he had moved it too far, and left it where it was, and marked it, in the place where he kept such things, as a number he did not trust. It was the first number he had ever marked that way. He did not expect it to be the last.

* * *

The first of the summaries came with the file, in Dumbledore's hand, on two sides of parchment, and it was the thing he had asked for in August and had not known what it would be like to receive.

Four hundred and eleven letters since June. Most from strangers who wished him well. Some from strangers who wished him to marry their daughters when the time came, which Dumbledore had listed under a heading of its own, with a count, and which Harry read with an interest that was mostly anthropological. Several from people who wanted things; Dumbledore had listed what, and one of the things was Harry's endorsement of a broomstick. And three, as agreed, from people who had known his parents, and those had come as letters and not as lines in a summary.

He read them in his room, slowly, by his own clock, which meant that he did not photograph them but went along the lines, because they were letters and he wanted to hear them.

One was from an old woman called Bathilda Bagshot, who had lived next door to his parents in a village called Godric's Hollow, and whose handwriting wandered across the page like something looking for a way out, and who told him what the house had looked like from her garden, and what his mother had grown in hers, which had been a great deal of lavender and some roses that had not done well, and that his mother had waved to her over the hedge every morning, and that she had not waved to anyone since.

One was from a woman called Augusta Longbottom, who wrote in a hand like a fence, and who said that her son and her son's wife had fought beside Harry's parents and were alive and in hospital and would not come out of it, and that her grandson had been born the day before Harry was, and that she had thought he ought to know that there was another boy, and that she did not know why she was writing except that it had seemed to her that somebody should, and that she expected no reply. Under the formality of it there was something else, and Harry read the letter three times to be sure what it was, and it was a woman alone with a small boy, telling another small boy that he was not the only one, in the only register she had.

One was from a woman called Molly Weasley, who said that she had not known his parents well and did not want to pretend she had, but that she was writing anyway, because she had a son exactly his age and had tried to imagine him with nobody to write to him and had not been able to bear it, and that she hoped this was not an intrusion, and that if he ever wanted to know what a wizarding family's kitchen was like at breakfast she could tell him at length, and that there were seven children and he should not expect it to be quiet.

There was no letter from Remus Lupin.

He asked. Dumbledore, in the hall with his hand on the door, said that there was one; that he was holding it; that he would say when he was not; and that he was aware this was the kind of thing he had promised to tell Harry he was doing, and that he was telling him.

"Why?"

"Because it is a letter from a man who has not decided whether he can bear to be known to you, and I am not going to make that decision for him by delivering it."

Harry thought about that and found that he could not argue with it, and said so. Dumbledore looked faintly surprised, and went.

* * *

The attic was Mrs Figg's idea, in the sense that she mentioned it, and Harry's, in the sense that he heard her.

It was the last Sunday of the month, and he was on her floor with Mr Tibbles across his legs, and she was saying, over the top of a cat, that she had two years of the Prophet up in the attic that she kept for the litter trays and really ought to clear, and Harry said, "May I read them?", and she stopped with a cat in each hand.

He watched her think about it. He had learned her face in twenty months of Wednesdays and he could see the argument go across it: that Dumbledore had said no wizarding world, and that Dumbledore had said nothing at all about newspapers, and that a newspaper was not, when you came down to it, a world. She put the cats down.

"I suppose there's no harm in it," she said, in the voice of a woman who was not sure, and went up the ladder.

There were four cardboard boxes, and he read them that afternoon and the next, on her floor, with the cats walking across the pages. He read the first days after the night, when his own name had been on every front page and there had been, he learned, a day declared in his honour. He read the trials that had gone on through the winter of 1981 and into 1982: the Lestranges, three of them, and a young man called Crouch who had the same name as the signature on the file, which he noted; a man called Karkaroff, who had named names to save himself; a man called Malfoy, who had said he had been under a curse and had been believed, and who was, two years later, giving money to hospitals and being thanked for it on the front page. He read the letters pages, which were where he learned what people in his new world thought about people like his mother, in their own words, and some of the words were ones he had heard Vernon use about other things. He read the appointments and the deaths and the Quidditch. He read the Minister, whose name was Bagnold, and the man called Fudge, who was mentioned every third issue and seemed to have done nothing in particular to deserve it. And on the letters page of an issue from the second week of December 1981, between a complaint about cauldron thickness and a recipe, he read a letter from a reader in Kent asking why Sirius Black had been sent to Azkaban without a trial when the Lestranges had been given one, and he read every issue after it for the reply, and there was no reply, in any issue, ever.

By Sunday night he had a model of his new world's politics that he thought was better than most people in it had. He knew that it was two years out of date. He knew the names, and he knew which of the names had money and which had office and which had both.

Dumbledore learned of it from Mrs Figg, who told him because she told him everything. He came on the Wednesday, which was not his day, and was displeased, and said so, in the tone of a man who has been outmanoeuvred by a three-year-old and knows it and does not intend to let it become a habit.

Harry heard him out. Then he said that he had been told no people and had not been told no newspapers; that he would not do a thing like that again without asking first; and that he was not sorry.

Dumbledore looked at him for a long time.

"No," he said. "I don't suppose you are." He was silent for a moment longer, and then he said that the Prophet would come to Harry daily from now on, by owl, through him, because he would rather Harry read it in the present than in the past, and that he would not be adding newspapers to the list of things he had failed to forbid.

Harry said thank you. He meant it. And he noticed, after Dumbledore had gone, that it was the first thing he had got from the old man without arguing for it, and that he had got it by doing a thing first and explaining it afterwards. He put that under things that worked, and resolved to use it seldom, because things that worked wore out.
