# Chapter 12: Fourteen Points

Hagrid brought the album on the second Wednesday in October, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. He put it on Harry's knees in the living room, sat back on the sofa, and watched Harry's face instead of the pages.

"Wrote to everyone I could think of," he said. "Everyone who might've had a picture. Most of 'em sent one. Took since June."

It was a thick album with a leather cover. Harry opened it at the speed Hagrid would have opened it, which was slowly, because Hagrid had made it and wanted to see it looked at. The first page was two children of eleven in black robes on a lawn, with a lake behind them and a castle behind the lake. The girl had red hair and the boy had Harry's hair. Neither of them was looking at the camera. The girl was saying something to the boy and the boy was pretending not to hear. They moved. She finished what she was saying and looked away. He looked at her. Then it began again.

"First week," Hagrid said. "Dunno who took it. Came from Professor Flitwick."

There were thirty pages. There were the two of them at thirteen and at fifteen and at seventeen, separately at first and then, from about sixteen, together. There was James on a broomstick, very high up, with the ball under his arm. There was Lily in a window seat with a book, not noticing anyone. There was a wedding, under a white arch in a garden. Lily was in white and James was in robes that didn't fit him across the shoulders, and beside James, with one hand on his arm and his head thrown back, laughing at something out of the picture, was a tall dark man whose face Harry had carried for two years.

He looked at it for a long time. Hagrid saw him looking and said, quietly for Hagrid, "That's Black. Best man. They were like brothers, him an' James." He didn't say anything else about it. Harry didn't ask. He could see from Hagrid's face what Hagrid thought, and he had the file for the rest.

The Sunday face laughed and laughed. The photograph came round every few seconds, and every time it came round Harry put it beside the face in the broken doorway with its hands in its hair. They were the same face. They were not.

Two pages on there were four young men in a garden with their arms round each other's shoulders, squinting into the sun. James. The dark man. A thin one in old clothes with a tired face, who was smiling as if he hadn't expected to be in the photograph. And a small round one at the end, half a head shorter than the others, with watery eyes and a nervous smile and his hands clasped in front of him as if he didn't know what to do with them.

"Remus Lupin," said Hagrid, pointing with a finger the width of the man's head. "Nice lad. Always poorly, never said why. An' that one's Peter Pettigrew. Always taggin' along after the other three. Never understood what they saw in him, to be honest, but James wouldn't hear a word against him." He stopped. "Brave, in the end, though. Went after Black on his own. Poor little bloke."

Harry had all four faces now. He had had James since the garden photograph and Sirius since the doorway. Remus Lupin was a name Dumbledore had given him in September, and a man who had not written. Pettigrew he had never seen until now. He looked at the small round man longer than at anyone else on the page, because this was the man who had shouted in a street and then been a finger, and the file had nothing to say about where the rest of him had gone. The face told him nothing either. It was the face of a man who was glad to have been included. He kept it.

* * *

He dictated the fourteen points to the quill on the Saturday after, three weeks after the file came, and he had waited the three weeks on purpose. He had wanted to know whether the points that stood out on a Saturday still stood out on a Tuesday, and whether anything new came up when he went back through the eleven pages in his head for the tenth time. The answer to the first was yes. The answer to the second was no. Fourteen was what there was.

He kept the letter short, because Dumbledore would read it at the speed of the world, and he numbered the points.

One of the three witnesses hadn't seen a wand in Black's hand, and the other two disagreed about which hand. Nobody in the file said where Pettigrew's body had gone, apart from the finger. The explosion was called an explosion by all three witnesses and a curse by nobody, and nobody named the curse, and Harry had now read every spell in four years of textbooks and none of them did that. Pettigrew had chosen a street full of muggles to accuse a murderer in. Black had laughed, and nobody had asked what at. The muggles had been made to forget, and the wizards had not been asked to remember anything in detail: half a page each. The order for the prison was signed the same day. *Meant to.* And last, as the fourteenth, the thing he had put first in his own head and last on the page, because he wanted Dumbledore to arrive at it the way he had: in a file about a man who had handed his closest friends to a murderer, there was no sentence in which anyone asked him why.

The answer came by owl to Mrs Figg's on the Thursday, and Mrs Figg walked it round with the Prophet and handed it over at the door like something hot.

It was one side. He read it standing in the hall.

*I have read your fourteen points. Several of them are good. I do not have answers to them.*

*I did not look as hard as I should have, in November 1981. I was grieving, and every alternative to his guilt was worse than his guilt, and I let that decide what I looked for. I am not proud of this. I do not say it to excuse it.*

*I will bring you anything further that exists. I do not think much does.*

*A.D.*

He took it upstairs and sat on the bed and went through it in his head a good many times. Then he looked at the parchment again, not for the words, which he had, but because it was the first letter the old man had written to him and he wanted to see how the hand went.

The number moved. It didn't move because the letter said anything about Sirius Black. It said nothing about him at all. It moved because of what the letter said about the man who had put Black in prison, and Harry had been counting that man's certainty as evidence. Dumbledore had known Black for ten years and had believed him guilty. Dumbledore was not a fool. That had been worth a good deal against the gaps in the file. Now Dumbledore had said, in his own hand, that he had decided what to look for before he looked. That was still evidence. It was less of it. Harry took the three in four from September and made it something nearer two in three, and marked it untrustworthy again. He noticed that he was pleased it had moved that way. He did not let that move it any further.

* * *

Dumbledore came on the last Saturday in October with the summary for the month. Listed in it, rather than forwarded, was a letter from Lucius Malfoy.

The summary gave the gist. The House of Malfoy congratulated Harry Potter on his remarkable gifts, as reported in the Daily Prophet, and wished him to know that its assistance was at his disposal in any matter in which it might be of use. Beside the entry, in Dumbledore's hand: *You will have read of this man in Mrs Figg's attic.*

He had. In the Prophets of 1982 the man had been questioned about the war and had said he'd been under a curse the whole time. The Ministry had accepted it. The letters page had been divided about evenly between people who believed him and people who didn't and said so carefully. Since then the name had turned up beside a donation to the hospital, beside a Quidditch cup, beside the Minister at a dinner. Harry knew what the letter was. It was a man with money making sure that a boy who was going to matter knew where the money was.

He understood why Dumbledore had put it in the summary instead of leaving it out, which would have been easier. Dumbledore wanted him to see it. That was a kind of respect. It was also a kind of test, and Harry didn't mind being tested on this.

"What should I do about it?" he asked.

"Nothing," said Dumbledore. "He has written. You have received. A reply would be a beginning, and silence is not a discourtesy to a man who did not expect one. He wrote so that you would know. You know."

Harry said he would do nothing, then, and filed the name where he had been filing it since the attic, which was near the top.

* * *

The proof came back on the last Friday in October. Rachel told him at the lights in Guildford, where she had taken to telling him things because he couldn't walk off in the middle.

"Dr Kemp sat on it for a week," she said. "Then he showed it to two other people in the department. They've all been through it. It's right. As far as any of them can tell, it's right. And the problem it solves is on a list some Hungarian published in the sixties with money attached. Not much money. Nobody's had it in twenty years." The lights changed. "He wants to know who wrote it."

"I'll think about that," Harry said.

He thought about it for a week, which was longer than the proof had taken, and it was the first time a question about himself had been harder than a question about mathematics.

There were four things he could do. He laid them out in the garden with a feather in front of him and went through them one at a time.

He could publish it under his own name. That was true. It would bring the mathematicians to the house the way the psychologists had come, and it would add to the thing he had decided in October, when he gave the Observer its hour, that being known was: a tool, more useful later than now, and not there when it was wanted if it was left in a drawer for a year. He could publish it under a made-up name. That was a lie, in print, with his name under it, and he had decided in June that he didn't tell those. He could let Dr Kemp publish it under his own name, which Dr Kemp hadn't asked for and would have refused, and which was a lie told on Harry's behalf by somebody else. He couldn't see that the second half of that changed the first. Or he could keep it.

He sat with the last one longest, because it was the one that cost nothing today. Then he thought about what a proof was, which was a true thing that nobody else had, and about how slowly true things moved from one head to another, which he had read a good deal about that summer. Keeping one in a drawer was a small theft from everyone who would have used it. The smallness didn't change what it was.

So it would be his name. He told Rachel on the Tuesday to tell Dr Kemp, and Dr Kemp came to the house on the Saturday and sat in the living room with the proof on his knee and asked Harry to explain the third step, because that was the one the department had argued about.

He was a tall stooping man of fifty with chalk on his cuffs. He listened without interrupting and then was quiet for a while.

"You've left out two lines there," he said finally. "Between the second claim and the third. They're obvious once you've seen them. They are not obvious before."

"I know," Harry said. "I'll put them in."

"Please do. The referee will be a person." Dr Kemp looked at him a moment longer. "I'm going to write this up properly, with you as the author and me as the person who sends it in, which is how it's done. The journal will want your age in a footnote. They'll want it because it's true and because nobody will believe it otherwise. Is that all right?"

"If it's true I don't mind it being said."

"Good," said Dr Kemp. He wrote something on the back of the proof and went away, and Harry learned in March that the journal had taken until then to decide whether a footnote was enough.

* * *

The missiles came to Greenham Common on a Monday in the middle of November. The Mail gave them the front page and most of the next two and was pleased, the way it had been pleased about the Falklands. The Prophet, which did not know what a missile was, had a Quidditch result.

He had been putting this off since June. He had read the history of the century in a day and a half that summer and had set one chapter aside. Not the one about the camps, which he had made himself go through twice. The one about what had been built since, and how much of it, and where it was pointed. He had set it aside because he knew what the arithmetic would say and didn't want to do it. On the Monday night he did it.

It took him three evenings. He read everything in the house that bore on it, which was more than he had expected, and asked Rachel for more. She brought him on the Friday a book by a man at a university who had tried the same sum and had stopped, in Harry's view, where the answer became unpleasant. Harry did not stop. He put a number on the chance that the missiles, or the ones they were pointed at, would be used in his lifetime. The number was not small. He sat in his room on the Thursday night with the lamp on and looked at it.

It was a new kind of feeling, and he spent some time on the feeling itself, because he had learned in June that his feelings were instruments and that an instrument you hadn't calibrated would lie to you. It wasn't grief. It wasn't the grey hum he got from fifteen million, which was a number refusing to become a feeling at all. This was a thing he could see quite clearly, that was very large, and that he could do nothing whatever about. He had never had one of those. Everything else wrong in his life had had a door or a person or a sum attached to it. This had none. He noticed that he wanted to turn it into the hum, because the hum let you turn the page, and decided that he wouldn't. He did not know yet what he would do instead.

The Mail said, in the week after, that a hundred million Americans had watched a film about what the missiles would do, and that it would be shown in Britain in December. He told Petunia at breakfast that he would be watching it.

"It's not suitable," she said, without turning round.

"I've read the medical literature on radiation sickness. The film will be less exact than that. I'd like to see what a hundred million people saw."

She said nothing. He had learned her silences, and this one meant that she had no answer and wouldn't argue. He took it as yes, and was right.

* * *

He tried two things at once on the Sunday after that, behind the shed, with his coat on, because it was November and the garden was his practice ground whether or not it was warm.

It was a simple idea and he had been meaning to test it since the table began. He could lift a feather every time. He could make a light at the end of his finger about one time in four. The books never once described a wizard doing two things in the same instant, and he had assumed that was because nobody had thought to.

He pictured both. He made the picture of the feather rising and the picture of the light coming, together, as one picture, and held it the way he held everything, and nothing happened. Not the feather, not the light. He tried it forty times, changing what he could, and nothing happened forty times. Then he did the feather alone, and it rose. The light alone, and it came, on the third try. Together, never.

He sat on the cold paving stone and thought about it. The books said nothing about this. The books, he was beginning to think, said nothing about a great many things because nobody had tried them, and nobody had tried them because everybody had a wand by eleven and never found out what the thing was like without one. He put it in the table under a heading he hadn't needed before and underlined it, and gave himself a week to think about what it meant before he let himself decide.

* * *

The Hungarian's letter came on the last Friday in November, forwarded by Dr Kemp with a note that said *you'll want to see this*.

It was on the notepaper of a hotel in Memphis, in a shaky hand that slanted uphill. It said that the proof was beautiful, and that the writer had set the problem in 1962 and thought about it on and off for twenty years without finding the way in, and that he enclosed the prize. He had: a cheque for fifty American dollars, signed in the same hand. Then it asked three more questions. Each was another problem from the list. The writer did not expect answers but would be glad of them, and he was seventy and travelled a good deal and could be written to care of the Academy in Budapest, which would find him eventually. The name at the bottom was Erdős.

Harry read the three questions and had nothing on any of them by the end of the evening, which had not happened to him with a piece of mathematics before. He had the first of the three by the Tuesday. Five days. He dictated it that night, and Rachel typed it and posted it to Budapest, and it was the beginning of a correspondence that lasted until the old man died, which was not for some years yet.

He gave the cheque to Pell, who looked at it and asked whether Harry would rather frame it than cash it, since he had read that people did. Harry said that fifty dollars was fifty dollars. Pell cashed it and framed a photocopy.

* * *

McGonagall came on the last Saturday in November, in the afternoon, before Dumbledore.

He had a theory about wands by then and had been carrying it since the galleon and the feather, and he put it to her carefully, because she had taught the subject for twenty-seven years and he had never held one.

"The wand isn't where the magic comes from," he said. "I've done magic without one, so it can't be. But everyone with a wand does more than I can, and does it every time, and I do a little, one time in four. So the wand is doing something between two things. It's an amplifier, so that less picture makes more happen. And it's a steadying hand, so that what happens is what was meant. I don't know which it is more of. I'd like to know."

McGonagall was quiet for a while.

"That is roughly what the oldest texts say," she said. "Nobody has said it as plainly in three hundred years. How would you test it?"

He told her. He would need to hold a wand and do the feather, and see whether the picture needed was smaller, and whether the feather went where it was sent. Then the light. Then the lock. The same four things, with and without, and the counts beside each other.

"You will need a wand to do that," she said.

"I know."

"Then we understand each other," said McGonagall, and wrote something in her own notes, which she had started making in September and had stopped pretending she wasn't.

* * *

Dumbledore came at four with the summary, and Harry asked him about the cloak.

Hagrid had let it drop two weeks before, in the middle of an hour about Quidditch, that James had had a cloak that made whoever wore it invisible and had got up to no end of trouble in it. Then Hagrid had stopped and looked as if he wished he hadn't started. Harry had asked where it was now. Hagrid hadn't known.

"I have it," Dumbledore said. "James lent it to me some weeks before the night, for a purpose of my own, and I have had it since. I intend to give it to you when you are older. I am aware that I have just told you of a thing of yours that I am keeping, which is one of the things I said in June I would try to tell you when I did it."

"I appreciate the distinction," Harry said. "How much older?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then I'll ask again in a year."

"I rather thought you would," said Dumbledore.

Harry put the cloak at the top of a list he had started in September, which was a list of things Dumbledore had that were Harry's. It had three things on it now: the cloak, a letter from Remus Lupin that had not yet been written, and the key to a vault.

The summary that month had two hundred and ninety letters in it, and one of them was not summarised but enclosed. It wasn't from anyone who had known his parents. It was from a girl at the school, twelve years old, in her second year, and her name was Alice Fenwick. She wrote that everyone in her year talked about Harry Potter and that she had thought he might like to know what the school was actually like, from someone in it, rather than what the Prophet said about it. She had told him, over six pages, with drawings in the margins: the staircases that moved, the ghost in her house, the way the ceiling in the Great Hall did the weather, what the food was like, what was wrong with the Potions master. Dumbledore's note said that he had forwarded it because it was the only one of the two hundred and ninety that treated Harry as someone you could write to rather than someone you wrote about.

Harry wrote back that evening. It was the first letter he had ever sent to anyone in his new world, and he found that he knew exactly what he wanted in it, which was questions. He asked her what she thought of the Prophet, which she also read. He asked whether the staircases moved on a schedule or when they felt like it, and whether anyone had written the schedule down. He asked what the Potions master was like when he wasn't being what was wrong with him. He asked her to tell him one thing about the school every week that she thought nobody outside it knew, and said that he would tell her one thing about the world outside it in return, and that he thought he was getting the better bargain.

He dictated it to the quill, because it was going to a witch. Dumbledore took it in his inside pocket when he left, and said that he would see it delivered.
